# Lamentations: brief for the writer

Book id `Lam` (USFM `LAM`). 5 chapters, 154 verses (22, 22, 66, 22, 22). English and Hebrew numbering agree throughout; `texts/orig/Lam.json` is the Leningrad Codex word by word. This book belongs to the **prophets lane** (tools/lanes.md): write only in content/books/Lam/, and put shared-file additions (terms, topics, works, points) in `content/proposals/prophets/`. Do not edit content/terms.json, sources.json or content/topics/ directly.

**One part, no `parts/` folder.** 154 verses is one writer's load, and the five poems answer each other all the way through (the weeping city, the divine anger, the man of ch. 3, the siege images, the refrains "no one to comfort her", "her enemies", "the Daughter of my people"). One writer keeps the wording of refrains consistent. Files go flat in `content/books/Lam/` like Ruth and Sng. That writer owns book.json. The hostile review is one `review.md` in the book folder, by a different agent.

Lamentations is heavily quoted in hymns and sermons ("How lonely sits the city", "Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by?", "The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases", "his mercies are new every morning", "great is your faithfulness", "It is good that one should wait quietly", "Restore us to yourself, O LORD"). Translate honestly and plainly; do not soften and do not add horror the Hebrew lacks. Keep the images (the widow, the winepress, the dried-up breast, the ostrich in the wilderness, the arrow in the kidneys) and explain them in notes.

**Plain English Translation layout.**
- All poetry: one verse per line, ` / ` between poetic lines. Chapters 1 and 2 give every verse three short lines, chapter 3 gives every verse one line, chapters 4 and 5 give two (chapter 5 sometimes one). Keep that shape; it is the qinah ("limping") measure, a long line followed by a short one (roughly 3 + 2 beats; Budde, Hillers), and the line breaks matter to the acrostic. Say once in a note that the measure cannot be kept in English and what the Plain English Translation does instead.
- The poems have no speaker labels in the Hebrew. The Plain English Translation adds none inside verse lines. Use section headings of 2–6 words that name the unit and, where clear, the voice ("The city speaks", "The man says", "We pray"). Speakers are set out below and on the settings cards.

**The acrostics (how the Plain English Translation shows them).** This is the book's most important formal fact and the Plain English Translation cannot reproduce it, so it is shown in four places, the same way every time (follow Psalm 119 for ch. 3 and Proverbs 31:10–31 for the note style; both are already written):
1. **Chapters 1, 2 and 4: one letter per verse.** Verse 1 begins with alef, verse 2 with bet, and so on to tav at verse 22. A note on verse 1 of each chapter (`type: word`, `kind: nu`, `top: acrostics`, `r` pointing to Psalm 119:1; Proverbs 31:10; Nahum 1:2) says so, gives the pattern, and names which Plain English Translation verses keep the letter's opening word where English allows it. Do not bend the English to fake the pattern. Where the Plain English Translation can keep the opening word, keep it (as Psalm 119 does), but meaning wins.
2. **Chapter 3: three verses per letter** (alef for 3:1–3, bet for 3:4–6 ... tav for 3:64–66). The Plain English Translation uses section headings that name the letter and the unit, in the form Psalm 119 uses ("Alef: I am the man", "Bet: ..."). All 22 stanzas get a heading. The words of a stanza all begin with that letter in Hebrew (all three verses), which is why ch. 3 is both an acrostic and a tight triple.
3. **Chapter 5: 22 verses, but not an acrostic.** Say so in a note on 5:1. Views to give: a deliberately broken or dropped acrostic as grief past form; the number 22 as an echo of the alphabet only (Albrektson, Hillers, Provan, Berlin); the liturgical repeat of 5:21 (see Jewish tradition below). Do not call it an acrostic anywhere, in text or on the chapter page.
4. **The chapter pages** (chapters.json) each carry a short table or list of the 22 letters (alef, bet, gimel, dalet, he, waw, zayin, chet, tet, yod, kaf, lamed, mem, nun, samekh, ayin, pe, tsade, qof, resh, shin, tav) with the first Hebrew word of each verse (chs 1, 2, 4) or stanza (ch. 3) and the Plain English Translation verse number, so a reader can see the pattern. Chapter 5 gets a note and no table.

Order of the letters: in **chapter 1 ayin comes before pe** (the regular order; ayin at 1:16, pe at 1:17). In **chapters 2, 3 and 4 pe comes before ayin** (2:16 pe, 2:17 ayin; 3:46–48 pe, 3:49–51 ayin; 4:16 pe, 4:17 ayin). Say why that matters, but do not state any parallel (early abecedaries, other acrostics) without a checked source: the acrostics topic (content/topics/acrostics.json) already records the pe-before-ayin order, so take its facts. Views: a second alphabetic order in circulation (Hillers, Freedman), a scribe's switch, or a deliberate break after chapter 1. No view is the settled one: label the consensus (that two orders existed in the preexilic period) only if a source states it.
- Meaning of the form: the whole alphabet, alef to tav, as completeness (Gottwald: "the A to Z of grief"), a discipline for grief, a way of saying everything and keeping order when order has collapsed. Views from Gottwald, Hillers, Berlin, O'Connor, Dobbs-Allsopp, Linafelt, Salters, Provan. Do not present any of them as a fact.
- A note on the Greek: the Septuagint and Vulgate print the Hebrew letter names (written in Greek and Latin letters) before every verse in 1–4, which is how Western Christians have known the letters through the Tenebrae readings (see Christian tradition). Describe it only after checking Rahlfs (`python tools/lxx.py Lam 1`).

**Title, attribution and the Greek preface.**
- The Hebrew title is the first word, *'eikhah*, "How!" (the cry of the funeral lament: David's lament in 2 Samuel 1:19, 25, 27; Isaiah 1:21; Jeremiah 48:17). It is also the word in the first verse of 1, 2 and 4 ('eikhah in 1:1, 2:1, 4:1; 4:2 'eikhah again). The Talmud calls the book *Qinot* "Laments" (Bava Batra 14b; 15a). Greek *Threnoi*, Latin *Threni* ("Dirges"); *Lamentationes* in the Vulgate; Jerome's "Lamentations of Jeremiah". One note, `kind: nu`, at 1:1 and an English-title explanation in book.json.
- **Jeremiah attribution.** The Hebrew text names no author. Bava Batra 15a: "Jeremiah wrote his book, the Book of Kings and Lamentations." 2 Chronicles 35:25 says Jeremiah lamented for Josiah and that the singers' laments were written in "the Laments" (*haqqinot*); Josephus, *Antiquities* 10.5.1, says Jeremiah's lament for Josiah survives in his day (check the passage); Jerome and the church fathers took it as Jeremiah's. The Greek has a prose preface at the start (Rahlfs, Lam 1:1): "And it came to pass, after Israel was taken captive and Jerusalem laid waste, that Jeremiah sat weeping and lamented this lament over Jerusalem and said ..." The Vulgate has the same preface (as a heading). On this site the Plain English Translation translates the Hebrew only; the preface goes in a note at 1:1 and in book.json, labeled as an addition of the Greek, with its wording given in your own translation from Rahlfs and its presence in the Greek, Latin and Syriac checked and stated exactly (do not claim the Syriac has it without checking). Modern debate: Hillers, Provan, Berlin, Gottwald and Dobbs-Allsopp mostly deny Jeremiah as author (the poems differ from Jeremiah in outlook and some in view of the king, 4:20; ch. 3's sense that "the LORD's loyal love never ends" differs from the harsher parts of Jeremiah; the poet shows Jeremiah's views on the false prophets, 2:14, but not his instructions to accept Babylon) while some conservative commentators defend it (name them only from a checked work); present both. Some think chs 1, 2, 4 are eyewitness (Albrektson, Hillers), chapter 5 later, ch. 3 an addition by another poet; some think one poet. Name the unity debate fairly. Dating consensus: all five poems after 586 BC and probably within the next generation, with most scholars placing 1, 2 and 4 within a few years of the fall and the others up to the exile's early years; the minority view of a later (Persian) date is a minority.
- **Where the book sits.** Hebrew Bible: in the Writings, among the Five Scrolls (Megillot: Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther), each read at a festival or fast: Lamentations on the Ninth of Av (Tish'ah be-Av). In the Talmud's order (Bava Batra 14b) Ruth, Psalms, Job ... Lamentations; Leningrad Codex lists the Five Scrolls together. Greek, Latin and Protestant Bibles: after Jeremiah (in the Greek and Orthodox Bibles after Baruch). The Letter of Jeremiah is Baruch 6 in the Latin Vulgate and a separate book after Lamentations in some Greek manuscripts and in Rahlfs (check). Say this in book.json under "canon".

**Lenses (fixed; use these keys in every chapter's `views`).** H C J X L. The usual F (Still to come) lens is dropped on purpose: the book looks back, and its hope (3:21–33; 5:19–22) is a hope in God's character and a prayer to be restored, not a prophecy of future events. Edom's cup (4:21–22) and the prayer "bring us back" (5:21) are handled in H and X, and the question whether the restoration has come goes in `views` of 5:21–22, not a separate lens. L takes the place of F so that the book's speech about suffering gets its own thread, and the writer states this in book.json.

The date of each poem; Babylonian Chronicle and the Lachish letters only as already cited in the site's Kings and Jeremiah. Use 586 BC (the site's date), with the 587 view named in book.json.
- `C` Critical: authorship and unity; the Mesopotamian "lament over the destruction of a city" (the Lament for Ur, for Sumer and Ur, for Nippur, for Eridu; the *balag* and *eršemma* laments) and the city-goddess who weeps (Dobbs-Allsopp, *Weep, O Daughter of Zion*, 1993; Hallo; Kramer; Samet; Cohen) against the idea of an Israelite tradition of its own (Gunkel, Westermann, Hillers); Daughter Zion as a personified city and as a widow, a mother, and an abandoned or raped woman (O'Connor, Maier, Dobbs-Allsopp, Mandolfo, Linafelt, Boase); the shifting speakers and the role of 3:1–21, "the man"; the acrostics; the poems' relation to Psalms 74, 79, 80, 83 and 137 and to Jeremiah, Ezekiel and Isaiah 40–66; textual criticism (witnesses below).
- `J` Jewish tradition: the Ninth of Av (the Temple fell on the 9th of Av; Mishnah Ta'anit 4:6's five disasters; the Fifth-month fast of Zechariah 7:3–5; 8:19; Jeremiah 52:12 and 2 Kings 25:8 give the 10th and the 7th of the month and the rabbis' reconciliation; see below), the chanting of the scroll in a low voice in a darkened synagogue, the *kinot* (elegies), the repeat of 5:21 after 5:22 (the Masoretes avoid ending on "unless you have utterly rejected us": the same practice at the end of Isaiah, Malachi and Ecclesiastes), *Eikhah Rabbah* (Lamentations Rabbah, with its great proems and stories such as the mother Miriam bat Tanchum? check before naming any story), the Targum of Lamentations, Rashi and Ibn Ezra, Maimonides on the fast days, the Hasidic and modern readings (Soloveitchik, Wiesel, the post-Shoah readings), and Bava Batra 15a on authorship.

The Plain English Translation does not read Christ into the Hebrew; these are readings, labeled.
- `L` Lament and suffering today: how the book handles guilt and protest together (the poet admits the sin, 1:5, 8, 14, 18, 20, 22; 3:39–42; 5:7, 16; and also accuses God of cruelty, 2:1–8, 3:1–18, 5:20–22: Linafelt, O'Connor, Brueggemann, Stuart, Parry, Dobbs-Allsopp, Hens-Piazza, Moore, Mandolfo's dialogic reading, Boase); trauma and memory, victim-blaming, women's silence and voice, survivor testimony, liturgical grief after war and disaster, the Shoah, theodicy (the Joban parallel); the place of the book in pastoral and Black and liberationist theology (the church of the oppressed; Dube; Cone is not to be cited without a checked work); and how Christians and Jews use the book at funerals and national mourning.

**Who it was for, and why (settings).** Cover the proposals fairly: (1) the survivors in the ruined city and its villages, gathered at the burned temple site for fasts and mourning (Jeremiah 41:5; Zechariah 7:3–5; 8:19), who sang the laments as liturgy; (2) the exiles in Babylon, who heard or read them as a way to say what happened; (3) the Mesopotamian-style "city lament" tradition sung at a destroyed or restored temple (Dobbs-Allsopp), which shows how a people could talk to their God after a divine defeat; (4) professional mourners (Jeremiah 9:17–20) and temple singers (2 Chronicles 35:25) with a role in public lamentation; (5) later Jews who read the book each year on the Ninth of Av and later Christians who heard it in Holy Week; (6) the victims of other disasters who took it over. Each chapter's cards must say which of these the evidence supports and which are guesses.

**Places that need full treatment (notes, traditions cards, deep sections).**
- 1:1: *'eikhah*, the widow, the princess, the forced labor (*mas*); the title, the Greek preface and the attribution (full note, and the book-level `traditions` or `views` entry).
- 1:2, 9, 16, 17, 21: "no one to comfort her" (*en menachem*), the refrain across the book and Isaiah 40:1 as its answer.
- 1:8–10: "her nakedness", the unclean thing (*niddah*), the foreigners in the sanctuary (Deuteronomy 23:3–4). Warn about reading the violation as only metaphor.
- 1:12: "Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by?" Hebrew *lo 'alekhem*: the Hebrew has a problem (the particle *lo*, the Greek, the Peshitta and the Vulgate; and "to you" as a question or an exclamation); the use at Good Friday and in the Jewish liturgy.
- 1:14–15: the yoke of the LORD, the winepress.
- 1:18: "The LORD is righteous" (*tsaddiq hu YHWH*), the confession; the poet's insistence that the nations be punished (1:21–22): what the poem asks for and the Christian and Jewish uses.
- 2:1: the footstool, "the splendor of Israel", God as the enemy; 2:4–5, God "like an enemy"; 2:6–7 the sanctuary as a garden booth; 2:9 "no more instruction" (*torah*) and no prophetic vision; 2:14 false visions; 2:15 "the perfection of beauty, the joy of all the earth" (Psalm 48:2 and Psalm 50:2).

A note on how the Jewish and Christian traditions have read this: as historical, as covenant curse, as hyperbole (Gerstenberger? check), and its use for the Roman siege (Josephus, *War* 6.3.4, as a parallel).
- 3:1–21: "the man": the views; the Job images; 3:14 "a laughingstock" beside Jeremiah 20:7; 3:15 "wormwood"; 3:16 "gravel" (*chatsats*); 3:18 "my hope is gone" (the "my splendor/my endurance"); the Hebrew *nitsci* ("my strength", or "my enduring").
- 3:19–24: the turn. "This I call to mind" and the three words: the Hebrew *zot 'ashiv 'el libbi*, "this I bring back to my heart". The famous verses 3:22–23: "The acts of the LORD's loyal love (*chasdei YHWH*) surely do not end/we are not finished (*lo tamnu*?); his compassions (*rachamav*) do not fail (*lo-khalu*); they are new every morning; great is your faithfulness (*rabbah emunatekha*). 'The LORD is my portion' (*cheleqi YHWH*), says my soul (*nafshi*), so I will wait for him." Full note, a `views` note from each lens, and a `traditions` card (below). Mention the hymn "Great Is Thy Faithfulness" and that the poem's "faithfulness" is God's, not the poet's.
- 3:25–39: waiting quietly, "bear the yoke in youth", "put his mouth in the dust" (3:29), the cheek (3:30), 3:31–33, "he does not afflict from the heart (*millibbo*)" (3:33: the Hebrew says literally "from his heart", which many read as "willingly" or "for the pleasure of it"; the traditions card on suffering and sin, below); 3:34–36 "to crush", "to deprive of justice", "to subvert": three infinitives, "the Lord does not approve"/"does the Lord not see?"; 3:37–39 the sovereignty of God over good and evil and the rhetorical question: "Why should the living complain, each one, about his sins?"
