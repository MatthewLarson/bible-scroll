# Isaiah: shared brief for the part writers

Book id `Isa`. This book belongs to the **prophets lane** (tools/lanes.md): you write only in content/books/Isa/, and shared-file additions go in content/proposals/prophets/. Eight parts, all written at the same time (no part can see another's notes):
- 01-06: the opening oracles, the song of the vineyard and Isaiah's call. **This writer owns book.json.**
- 07-12: the Immanuel chapters, the child of 9:6, the shoot of Jesse, the song of chapter 12.
- 13-23: the oracles against the nations.
- 24-30: the "Isaiah Apocalypse" (24–27) and the first woes (28–30).
- 31-39: the last woes (31–33), Edom and the highway (34–35), and the Hezekiah chapters (36–39).
- 40-44: "Comfort my people", the first servant song (42:1–9), the idol satire, Cyrus named (44:28).
- 45-55: Cyrus the anointed, the fall of Babylon, the second, third and fourth servant songs (49:1–13; 50:4–11; 52:13–53:12), Zion restored.
- 56-66: the chapters after the return: foreigners and eunuchs, the true fast, the anointed messenger (61), the new heavens and new earth.

**Brief and drift audit.**
- Follow tools/agent-writer.md exactly, with STYLE.md and PLAN.md: the drift audit, the References rule, "Who it was for, and why", "Where Christians differ" and "Books outside the 66".
- Compare against the NIV and ESV only in your head. Never write out their text; log only verse number, outcome and a few words of reason.
- Isaiah is among the most memorized books (Handel's Messiah, Christmas and Good Friday readings, 40:31, 53). Rebuild every verse from `node tools/literal.js Isa <ch>`. A familiar line may stay only where the Hebrew forces it. In Psalms one audit marked drifted lines "clean"; audit honestly, line by line.

**Your part.**
- Write plain.txt, notes.json, variants.json, glosses.json, chapters.json, topics.json and sources.json in content/books/Isa/parts/<your part>/ for your chapters only. Read STYLE.md "Large books written in parts".
- The lenses are fixed in book.json: H (History and fulfillment), C (Critical), J (Jewish tradition), X (Christian), F (Still to come). Only the 01-06 writer edits book.json.
- book.json covers: the prophet and his times (the kings of 1:1, the Assyrian crisis); authorship and composition, with each view in its strongest form (one Isaiah, the traditional view held by many conservative Jewish and Christian scholars; First, Second and Third Isaiah, the critical consensus since Döderlein and Duhm, with Ibn Ezra's medieval hint at 40–66; the recent "book as a unity" editing views); the Great Isaiah Scroll and the other Qumran copies; the Greek Isaiah; the book's structure and themes (the Holy One of Israel, Zion, the remnant, justice, the servant, the new exodus, the new creation); the book in the New Testament and the church ("the fifth gospel"), in synagogue (the seven haftarot of consolation after the Ninth of Av) and in later tradition (the Martyrdom and Ascension of Isaiah, Hebrews 11:37); audience views (who 40–55 and 56–66 were for). It needs `traditions` only for book-wide questions.
- The other writers may wait on book.json's lead, facts and audience before `--check` is fully clean, so the 01-06 writer fills those first, before translating.

**Numbering.** plain.txt uses English verse numbers. Two places differ from the Hebrew: English 9:1 = Hebrew 8:23 and English 9:2–21 = Hebrew 9:1–20 (part 07-12); English 64:1 = Hebrew 63:19b and 64:2–12 = Hebrew 64:1–11 (part 56-66). Say so in one note at the start of each, and use the English numbers everywhere.

**Poetry and prose.** Most of Isaiah is poetry: one verse per line, ` / ` between poetic lines, parallel lines kept parallel, no padding. Prose (6–8 in part, 20, 36–39, parts of 7, 22, 37, 38) has no ` / `. Keep Isaiah's wordplays where English can (5:7 mishpat / mispach and tsedaqah / tse'aqah; 7:9 ta'aminu / te'amenu); otherwise explain them in a note. Where the Hebrew is uncertain, say so in a note (`kind` `deb` or `mis`).

**Terms.** Re-read content/terms.json and content/proposals/prophets/terms.json before you start, and follow both. The prophets' terms are there now, following the site's earlier books: koh amar YHWH "The LORD says this"; ne'um YHWH "the LORD declares"; massa' "oracle"; hoy "Woe to"; "the day of the LORD"; "the Holy One of Israel"; "Daughter Zion"; 'almah "young woman"; the servant in lower case; mishpat and tsedaqah "justice and righteousness"; "the Chaldeans"; 'elilim "worthless idols"; and the older "the LORD of Armies", "the Lord GOD", hesed "loyal love", Sheol, go'el "redeemer" for God. If your part needs a new shared term, check both files and the other lanes' proposals first, then add it to content/proposals/prophets/terms.json with a short read-modify-write, and say so in your report. Translate each key word the same way through the book unless the context forces otherwise, with a note when it does.

**Parallels inside the Bible** (the earlier text on the site is the model; match it exactly where the Hebrew is the same, and list each difference in a note):
- 36:1–39:8 = 2 Kings 18:13–20:19 (content/books/2Ki/parts/18-25/plain.txt). Isaiah lacks 2 Kings 18:14–16 (Hezekiah's tribute) and has Hezekiah's psalm (38:9–20), which Kings lacks; 38:21–22 stand where 2 Kings 20:7–8 has them earlier. Part 31-39 reuses the Kings Plain English Translation wherever the Hebrew matches, including its line breaks in 37:22–35 (2 Kings 19:21–34).
- 2:2–4 = Micah 4:1–3. Isaiah is the model; the Micah writer will follow it later. 15–16 overlap Jeremiah 48, and 37:16–20 is Hezekiah's prayer (also 2 Kings 19:15–19): Isaiah sets the wording for Jeremiah.
- Inside Isaiah: 11:6–9 and 65:25; 35:10 and 51:11; 40:3 and 57:14 / 62:10; 2:9–21 refrains. The earlier verse is the model; if it is in another part, read that part's plain.txt once it exists, or write yours and list the pair in your report.

**Textual evidence.**
- The Leningrad Codex is the base. Use texts/auto/Isa.json for Ketiv/Qere and marked readings.
- **The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a, about 125 BC) is complete**, and 1QIsa-b (1Q8) and 4QIsa-a to -r (4Q55–4Q69) cover much of the book. Read them yourself with `.cache/dl/tfenv/bin/python tools/dss.py Isa <ch>` (run from bible/; its header explains the marks; restored words are in brackets and are never a scroll's reading). Most differences are spelling (the scroll writes vowel letters, -kah for -k, hu'ah for hu'); give an entry only where the meaning changes, a major translation footnotes it, or the KJV differs. Known places: 6:3 (holy twice?), 7:14 (wqr' 'and he will call'), 14:4 (madhebah), 21:8 ("the seer" for "a lion"), 40:6 ("and I said"), 45:8, 49:12 (Sinim/Syene), 52:14, 53:11 ("light"), 60:19, and longer pluses and minuses (2:9b–10 absent in 1QIsa-a; 40:7–8; 34:17–35:2 written by a second hand). Check each one yourself.
- **The Greek**: Isaiah's Old Greek is a free, sometimes paraphrasing translation. On this site "the Greek" means the critical text (Ziegler's Göttingen Isaiah, 1939, or Rahlfs). `python3 tools/lxx.py Isa <ch>` prints Swete's text, which is Codex Vaticanus with others where it fails: name a reading only Swete's codex has by that codex. The New Testament often quotes Isaiah in the Greek's wording (6:9–10; 7:14; 9:1–2; 11:10; 28:16; 29:13; 40:3; 40:6–8; 42:1–4; 49:6; 52:5; 53:1; 53:4; 53:7–8; 59:20–21; 61:1–2; 64:4; 65:1–2): say in the note which text the New Testament follows.
- The Targum of Isaiah (Targum Jonathan; Chilton's translation in The Aramaic Bible, 1987) is the key Jewish witness, with a messianic reading of 9:6, 11:1–6, 42:1, 43:10, 52:13 and 53. The Vulgate, the Peshitta and the later Greek versions (Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion: 7:14 neanis) are later witnesses.
- Say once, on your first chapter page, which witnesses you read yourself (the scrolls through dss.py; Swete; anything you could open online). No reader-facing text may say "from memory", "not rechecked" or "I have seen it myself": verify, or write "reported by <source>".

**Who it was for, and why.**
- Every oracle, song, prayer and narrative scene gets a settings card, and most chapters have more than one.
- Say who speaks (Isaiah, the LORD through him, the servant, Zion, the people, a later voice) and to whom: Ahaz at the conduit (7:3), the leaders of Jerusalem, the women of Zion, the northern kingdom, a foreign nation (usually spoken to Judah, about the nation), exiles in Babylon, the community back in Judah.
- For 40–55 and 56–66 the audience is debated: give the traditional view (Isaiah, in Hezekiah's day, writing for a future generation) and the critical view (a prophet among the exiles in the 540s BC; prophets in Judah after 538) in `views`, and do not state either as settled.
- Use `readers` for the collected book's later readers.
