## The date and authorship of Daniel (every part states its own chapters' evidence; 01-03 gives the whole debate)
Present both fairly, each in its strongest form, and name who holds what. Do not state either as settled.
- **Sixth-century (traditional).** Written by Daniel in Babylon and Persia, c. 605-530 BC; the Talmud (Bava Batra 15a) credits the Men of the Great Assembly with writing it down; Jesus speaks of "the prophet Daniel" (Matthew 24:15); Josephus (Antiquities 11.8.5) says the book was shown to Alexander the Great. Modern defenders: E. J. Young, Joyce Baldwin, Gleason Archer, Gerhard Hasel, Alan Millard on the language and the court, and others. Their strongest points: the book's claims of itself, Daniel in the Old Greek and at Qumran, the Aramaic of 2-7 closer to the Imperial/Official Aramaic than some critics allow, the Belshazzar evidence (no ancient writer after the sixth century except Daniel and Baruch knew Belshazzar as king: the Nabonidus Chronicle and the Verse Account show him as the crown prince ruling in Babylon while his father was at Teima), the court details (the Persian loanwords, Babylonian training, "the law of the Medes and Persians").
- **Maccabean (critical majority since the 1700s).** Chapters 1-6 are older tales; the whole book was completed during the persecution of Antiochus IV Epiphanes, c. 167-164 BC, by a Jewish scholar-teacher (the maskilim of 11:33-35; 12:3). The case runs from Porphyry (3rd century AD, whom Jerome answers in his commentary) through Collins, Hartman and Di Lella, Seow and Newsom. Strongest points: chapters 8 and 11 describe events to 164 BC in exact detail and then miss (11:40-45 does not match Antiochus' death in Persia); the author's vagueness about the Neo-Babylonian and Persian kings (Darius the Mede; "Belshazzar the son of Nebuchadnezzar"; Nebuchadnezzar's seven years vs the Prayer of Nabonidus 4Q242); the three Greek loanwords for the instruments of 3:5, 7, 10, 15 (kitharis, psanterin, sumphonia; the dating of the last is debated), and Persian administrative words; the Aramaic of the book compared with the Genesis Apocryphon, the Elephantine papyri and the Qumran Aramaic; Sirach 44-50 (c. 180 BC) names no Daniel; Daniel is among the Writings in the Hebrew canon; the genre of apocalypse (pseudonymous, visions explained by an angel, "history" as prophecy). The oldest Qumran copies (4QDan-c, 4QDan-a) are dated to the late second century BC: weigh what that does to a late date (very little time between composition and copying).
- **Mediating views** (name them; do not hide them): the court tales earlier (Persian or early Hellenistic) and the visions Maccabean; a sixth-century core with Maccabean editing (Goldingay on some chapters); a date in the late Persian or early Hellenistic period (Lucas; Millard); the book completed by 164 BC and chapters 1-6 composed in the third century (Collins and others on the "Daniel tales").
- **Prediction after the event?** Say plainly that the late dating makes chapters 8 and 11 history written as prophecy (vaticinium ex eventu), the early dating makes them prophecy; state both without ridicule. Note Christians who date the book late and still accept it as Scripture (many Catholic scholars since Pius XII's Divino afflante Spiritu era, Protestant critical scholars), and Jews who date it early on traditional grounds.
- Josephus, Jerome, Ibn Ezra, Rashi, Saadia Gaon, Maimonides and Abravanel must be quoted only where their position can be checked (Sefaria, with WebFetch). Never invent a quotation.

## Languages, poetry and prose
- **Hebrew 1:1-2:4a; Aramaic 2:4b-7:28; Hebrew 8:1-12:13.** The switch is marked by the word "Aramaic" (aramit) in 2:4 itself. Note the switch at 2:4 and the return at 8:1 in the Plain, and keep the Aramaic forms recognizable in notes (malkuta "kingdom", 'elah "God", qaddishin "holy ones", bar enash "one like a son of man", 'attiq yomin "Ancient of Days"). Say the Hebrew vocabulary in the Aramaic part comes from Aramaic words, not Hebrew; do not use Hebrew terms for Aramaic words.
- Daniel is mostly prose. Poetry: Daniel's hymn of praise (2:20-23), Nebuchadnezzar's doxologies (4:3; 4:34-35 in the English numbering 4:34-35), the poetic stretches of 7:9-10, 7:13-14, 7:23-27 (a poetic layer in the vision report), and 12:3. Follow the layout of the Leningrad Codex and the scholarly consensus on which verses are verse (Collins' commentary lists them); mark prose with no ` / ` and poetry one verse per line with ` / ` between poetic lines. Prayers (9:4-19) are prose in form but liturgical: keep their parallel lines.
- The letter-like decrees (3:29; 4:1-3; 6:25-27) and royal speech are prose.

## Textual evidence (reading these yourself, not from memory)
- **The Masoretic Text** (Leningrad Codex) is the base, bilingual. Use texts/auto/Dan.json for Ketiv/Qere and marked readings.
- **Qumran.** Eight copies preserve parts of Daniel: 1QDan-a (1Q71), 1QDan-b (1Q72), 4QDan-a to -e (4Q112-4Q116) and 6QpapDan (6Q7). Read them yourself: `.cache/dl/tfenv/bin/python tools/dss.py Dan <ch>` (run from bible/; its header explains the marks; restored words in brackets are never a scroll's reading). They show the Hebrew/Aramaic switch at 2:4 and 8:1 (check how each copy handles the boundaries and chapter 7-8: 1QDan-b and 4QDan-a), generally agree with the MT, and are the oldest manuscript evidence (4QDan-c and 4QDan-a copied c. 125-50 BC by the editors' dating; report editors' dates only as "reported by Ulrich/Flint" if you cannot check). List every scroll that preserves a place, including those that agree with the MT. Related Qumran Aramaic texts of the Daniel cycle (Prayer of Nabonidus 4Q242; Pseudo-Daniel 4Q243-245; the "Son of God" text 4Q246 = works.json `aramaic-apocalypse-4q246`; the Melchizedek scroll 11Q13 = `melchizedek-scroll`; Florilegium 4Q174 = `florilegium-4q174`) are works, not witnesses to Daniel: cite them by their works.json names in notes only.
- **The Greek: two versions, and they differ by more than any other Old Testament book.**
  - **The Old Greek** (the Septuagint proper), c. 2nd century BC, preserved in Papyrus 967 (3rd century AD; Chester Beatty-Cologne-Princeton, with Susanna and Bel at the end, and with chapters 7-8 placed before 5-6 in the order reported by the editors) and Codex Chisianus (88, Swete's "Chigi") with the Syro-Hexapla. It is freer, adds to chs. 3-6 and gives a different text of 4-6 and a notably different 9:24-27 and 11. After the 3rd century the church stopped reading it.
  - **Theodotion** (a revision of the Greek toward the Hebrew, 1st century BC to 1st century AD: the Kaige tradition; quoted already in the New Testament and Revelation); in Christian Bibles it replaced the Old Greek (so Jerome in the preface to Daniel: the churches read Theodotion).
  - `python tools/lxx.py Dan <ch>` prints Swete's Old Greek (from Chigi), `python tools/lxx.py Dat <ch>` Theodotion; Sus Sut Bel Bet are Susanna, the Song of the Three, and Bel with the Old Greek and Theodotion. Swete is one manuscript; the critical text is Ziegler's Göttingen Daniel (1954) and Rahlfs. On this site "the Greek" means the critical text; name a reading only Swete's codex has by that codex. Always say which Greek (Old Greek or Theodotion) a variant uses.
  - Give a variant entry wherever the Old Greek or Theodotion differs in substance, adds or omits a verse (for example: the long additions to ch. 3, 4:1-3 and 4:34 in the Old Greek, 5:1-4, 5:10-12, the plot of 6:5-6, 7:13 "like a son of man" vs "as an ancient of days" in the Old Greek; 9:24-27; 11:30-45; 12:2-3, 12:11-12 in the Old Greek and Theodotion). Mention that Origen (Letter to Africanus) and Jerome (Preface to Daniel, Commentary on Daniel) discuss the problem.
- The Targums: there is **no** Targum to Daniel in the standard rabbinic collection (a late Aramaic Targum to the Writings, Daniel included, is a different thing). The Peshitta, the Vulgate and the later Greek versions are late witnesses. Medieval Jewish commentators (Saadia Gaon's Arabic Daniel; Rashi; Ibn Ezra; Joseph ibn Yahya; Abravanel's Ma'ayenei ha-Yeshuah) read on Sefaria: Rashi's and Ibn Ezra's commentaries to Daniel are there.
- Say once, on your first chapter page, which witnesses you read yourself. No reader-facing text may say "from memory", "not rechecked" or "I have seen it myself": verify, or write "reported by <source>".

## Daniel's seventy sevens (9:24-27): the views to state fairly (08-10 owns the full statement; a short statement for the book's other parts goes through the topic `seventy-sevens`)
State each reading's starting point, its count, its anointed ones, its "cut off", its last seven, its covenant-maker, and who holds it; use the churches' own sources where there are any.
1. **Antiochene / Maccabean** (the critical majority since the 19th century; Collins, Montgomery, Porteous; many Catholic and Protestant critical scholars): the seventy years of Jeremiah are reinterpreted as seventy sevens of years (490 years) from 587/586 BC (or the decree in Jeremiah's word) to 164 BC; 7 sevens (49 years) to an anointed one, a ruler (Cyrus, Jeshua the high priest, or Zerubbabel; 538 BC), 62 sevens to the cut-off anointed one (Onias III the high priest, murdered c. 171 BC; 2 Maccabees 4:30-38), the final seven (c. 171-164 BC) as the persecution, the covenant with the many (the Hellenizing Jews: 1 Maccabees 1:11-15), the half-week 167-164 BC, the abomination (1 Maccabees 1:54). The author's chronology is schematic, not arithmetic (the count does not fit the 490 actual years). Hold the arithmetic gap fairly: some defenders of the view count differently.
2. **Messianic / Christ** (the dominant reading in the early church and among Reformers): the 490 years extend to Christ. Julius Africanus (c. AD 240, via Eusebius, Demonstratio 8.2): Artaxerxes' 20th year (445/444 BC) to Christ's baptism or passion; Clement of Alexandria (Stromata 1.21), Tertullian (Against the Jews 8), Eusebius, Augustine (City of God 18.34), Jerome's Commentary (which records several, including Hippolytus's and Africanus's counts); the Reformers (Calvin on Daniel 9, who ends it at Christ's death and the destruction of Jerusalem). Modern: E. J. Young (The Prophecy of Daniel, 1949; symbolic sevens), Hengstenberg, Keil, Kline, Archer, Baldwin. Christ is the anointed one cut off (9:26), he "confirms a covenant" in the last seven (9:27), the sacrifice ends at the cross (Hebrews 9-10), the city and sanctuary fall in AD 70. Variations: starting point 458/457 BC (Ezra 7), 445/444 BC (Nehemiah 2), 538 BC (Cyrus, Isaiah 44:28; 45:1), or a symbolic count.
3. **Seventh-day Adventist** (a Christ-centered reading with a different start): the 70 sevens are cut off from the 2,300 evenings and mornings of 8:14; both begin with Artaxerxes' decree in 457 BC; 69 sevens to AD 27 (Christ's baptism, the "anointed one"); the last seven to AD 34; the 2,300 "days" (years) end in 1844 (Fundamental Belief 24; and the sanctuary cleansing).
4. **Dispensational / futurist** (Anderson, The Coming Prince, 1895; Scofield Reference Bible; Walvoord, Pentecost, Hoehner): sixty-nine sevens of 360-day years (483 years) from the decree of Artaxerxes in 445/444 BC to Christ's entry into Jerusalem; a **gap** (the church age) before the seventieth seven, which is still future (the great tribulation, seven years: Revelation 6-19); 9:26 "the people of the coming ruler" (the Romans) destroy Jerusalem in AD 70; "the coming ruler" is the Antichrist, who makes a covenant with the many (Israel) for a seven, breaks it in the middle (9:27), and sets up the abomination (Matthew 24:15; 2 Thessalonians 2:4). Critics (including other premillennialists) dispute the gap and the "he" of 9:27.
5. **Historicist** (the Protestant tradition from the Reformation to the 19th century; Joseph Mede, Isaac Newton, Elliott): the 490 years run to the first century; the day-year principle for the 1,260, 1,290, 1,335 and 2,300 days of the other chapters (as years) toward the pope as the little horn (Luther, Calvin, the Westminster Confession 25.6 of 1646, the Second London Confession 26.4 (1689, Baptist)): note that later editions of the Westminster Confession (the American 1788 revision) removed this; Catholic counter-readings (Ribera and Bellarmine, futurist; Alcazar, preterist) arose in answer.
6. **Preterist** (a modern name; Moses Stuart, Kenneth Gentry, R. C. Sproul/Chilton's postmillennial views): fulfilled in the first century: the ministry of Jesus (confirming the covenant in a half-seven) and AD 70.
7. **Jewish tradition** (state them only as far as you can verify): the 490 years are the Second Temple era (Seder Olam Rabbah 28; Rashi on 9:24-27, who counts 70 years of exile and 420 of the Second Temple; Ibn Ezra; Saadia Gaon, Abravanel, the Talmud's discouragement of calculating the end (b. Sanhedrin 97b: "may the bones of those who calculate the end rot")); the "anointed one" in 9:25 is Cyrus or Joshua/Zerubbabel and in 9:26 a high priest or the last of the line; Ramban (Sefer ha-Ge'ulah) and Abravanel read it as pointing to the Messiah and to the end of exile. Never present a Jewish reading from a Christian source without saying so; use Sefaria.
8. **Allegorical / symbolic** (older and some modern readings: the 490 years is a symbolic fullness, "seven times seventy"): Young, Kline's symbolic-messianic reading; many conservative scholars.
9. Name the other lines the sources keep alive: the count from Jeremiah's original word (25:12, 586 BC); the proposals using the Seder Olam's chronology; the Qumran count (11Q13; 4Q180-181: 490 years as ten jubilees); Bible Chronology research (Hoehner on the date of the crucifixion: AD 33; Anderson AD 32). Do not endorse any one dating.
- Each reading gets the same questions: the **starting point**, the **count and its unit** (years of 360, 365 days, or schematic), the **identity of the "anointed one, a ruler"** (9:25), the **"anointed one" who is cut off** and the sense of ve'ein lo, the **"ruler who is to come"** and the **"he" of 9:27**, the **gap**, and the **fate of the temple**. ## Lead decisions (after the writers, before the fixes; every fixer applies these)

- **The desolating abomination:** "the abomination that desolates" at 11:31 and 12:11; 8:13 stays "the rebellion that desolates"; 9:27 keeps the Hebrew's own wording (plural "abominations", a desolator). Each of these notes says that Matthew 24:15 (site Plain: "the abomination of desolation") and Mark 13:14 quote the Greek rendering. Part 11-12 changes "desolating abomination".
- **'God of heaven' (2:18, 19, 37, 44):** "the God of the heavens", as in Ezra and Nehemiah on the site (a lead decision; the original brief said "God of heaven"). Note the Aramaic 'elah shemaya.
- **Source keys:** a source key is global; where two parts or books define one key differently, the later-written definition is renamed with a suffix and its citations updated (do not change the earlier book's file). Known clashes: sda-beliefs (08-10 vs shared), xenophon-cyropaedia (Ezr 01-06 vs 04-07), beaulieu-nabonidus (Jer 50-52 vs 04-07), day-daniel-ugarit (Ezk 08-15, wrong journal) vs day-daniel-vt.
- **Sources and 'from memory':** a fact that cannot be verified is removed or written as "reported by <named source>". Never "from memory" in reader-facing text.
- **Greek:** "the Old Greek" and "Theodotion" are always named separately; say if only Swete's print was read.
