## The question
1:1 I am about to set out a most philosophical argument: whether godly reason is master in its own right over the passions. So I would rightly advise you to give your full attention to this philosophy.
1:2 The subject is needed by everyone who wants understanding. Besides, it holds the praise of the greatest virtue, I mean good sense.
1:3 If, then, reason is seen to overcome the passions that hold back self-restraint: gluttony and craving,
1:4 it is also seen to be lord over the passions that block justice, such as malice, and over those that block courage: anger, pain and fear.
1:5 "Then how," some might perhaps say, "if reason rules the passions, is it not master over forgetting and not knowing?" They are trying to say something laughable.
1:6 Reason does not rule over its own passions. It rules over the passions that oppose justice, courage, self-restraint and good sense. And it rules these not so as to destroy them, but so as not to give in to them.
1:7 I could show you from many places and in other ways that godly reason is sovereign over the passions.
1:8 But I could prove it much better from the noble courage of those who died for virtue: Eleazar, and seven brothers and their mother.
1:9 All of these looked down on pains that went as far as death, and so they showed that reason rules over the passions.
1:10 It falls to me to praise for their virtues the men who, at this season, died with their mother for what is noble and good. For the honors they have, I would call them blessed.
1:11 All people admired them for their courage and their endurance, and so did the men who tortured them. They became the cause of the tyranny over the nation being broken. They conquered the tyrant by their endurance, so that through them the homeland was cleansed.
1:12 I will be able to speak about that very soon. First I will begin with the theme, as I usually do. Then I will turn to my account of them, giving glory to the all-wise God.
## What reason is
1:13 So this is our question: is reason sovereign over the passions?
1:14 Let us sort out what reason is, and what a passion is, how many kinds of passions there are, and whether reason overcomes all of them.
1:15 Reason, then, is the mind that, together with a right way of life, puts the word of wisdom first.
1:16 And wisdom is the knowledge of things divine and human, and of their causes.
1:17 This is the training the law gives. Through it we learn the things of God with reverence and the things of people for our good.
1:18 The kinds of wisdom are good sense, justice, courage and self-restraint.
1:19 Good sense rules them all, since by it reason overcomes the passions.
## The family tree of the passions
1:20 The passions have two broad sources that take in all the rest: pleasure and pain. Each of these grows naturally in the soul too.
1:21 Many passions follow in the train of pleasure and of pain.
1:22 Before pleasure comes craving, and after pleasure, joy.
1:23 Before pain comes fear, and after pain, grief.
1:24 Anger is a passion that pleasure and pain share, if one thinks back to when it came over him.
1:25 In pleasure there is also the malicious frame of mind, which takes more shapes than any other passion.
1:26 In the soul it is boasting, love of money, love of glory, love of quarreling and envy.
1:27 In the body it is eating anything at all, gorging, and eating alone.
1:28 Pleasure and pain are like two plants of the body and the soul, and these passions are their many shoots.
1:29 Reason is the gardener over all of them. It clears around each one, nips it back, ties it up, waters it, and in every way moves it from place to place. So it tames the wild thickets of habits and passions.
1:30 For reason is the leader of the virtues, and sovereign over the passions. First, then, look at the things that hold back self-restraint, and see that reason is master in its own right over the passions.
## Reason and the food laws
1:31 Self-restraint, then, is mastery over the cravings.
1:32 Some cravings belong to the soul and some to the body, and reason plainly overcomes them both.
1:33 Otherwise, when we are drawn toward foods that are forbidden, why do we turn away from the pleasures they would give? Is it not because reason is able to overcome the appetites? I think so.
1:34 That is why, when we crave sea creatures, birds, four-footed animals, and every kind of food that the law forbids us, we keep away from them because reason has mastery.
1:35 The passions of the appetites are resisted and bent back by the self-restrained mind, and all the urges of the body are muzzled by reason.
## Joseph and the craving for pleasure
2:1 And why be surprised if the soul's cravings to share in beauty are made powerless?
2:2 That is why the self-restrained Joseph is praised: by reason, in his thinking, he overcame the urge for pleasure.
2:3 He was young and in his prime for sex, yet by reason he made the passions' maddening sting powerless.
2:4 And reason plainly overcomes not only the goad of the urge for pleasure, but every craving.
## The law trains the cravings
2:5 For the law says, "You must not crave your neighbor's wife, or anything at all that belongs to your neighbor."
2:6 Now since the law has told us not to crave, I could all the more persuade you that reason is able to rule the cravings, just as it rules the passions that block justice.
2:7 Otherwise, how is someone who by nature eats alone, a glutton or a drunk, trained over again, unless it is clear that reason is lord over the passions?
2:8 As soon as a person lives by the law, even if he loves money, he forces his own nature. He lends to people in need without charging interest, and when the seventh years come he cancels the loan.
2:9 Even if he is stingy, he is ruled by the law because of reason. He does not glean the leftovers of his harvests or pick his vineyards clean of their last grapes. In the other cases too one can see that reason is the one ruling the passions.
2:10 For the law rules even over goodwill toward parents: it does not betray virtue for their sake.
2:11 It overcomes love for a wife, and rebukes her when she breaks the law.
2:12 It is lord over love for children, and punishes them for wrongdoing. It is master over closeness with friends, and exposes them for wickedness.
2:13 And do not think it strange that reason is able, through the law, to overcome even hatred.
2:14 So a person does not cut down the enemy's orchard trees. He keeps safe for their owners what belongs to his foes, and he helps lift up what has fallen down.
## Reason and the harsher passions
2:15 Reason plainly rules even the more violent passions: the love of power, empty glory, boasting, arrogance and envy.
2:16 The self-restrained mind pushes away all these malicious passions, as it does anger, for it is master over that too.
2:17 Moses, for example, burned with anger at Dathan and Abiram, yet he did not act against them out of anger. He let reason judge his anger.
2:18 For the self-restrained mind, as I said, is able to win the victory over the passions. Some of them it can change, and others it can even make powerless.
2:19 Why else does our all-wise father Jacob blame Simeon and Levi and their men for slaughtering the people of Shechem, the whole nation, without reason? He says, "Their anger be cursed."
2:20 If reason were not able to rule anger, he would not have spoken that way.
## The mind on its throne
2:21 For when God fashioned the human being, he planted the passions and habits all around him.
2:22 At that time he also gave the mind a throne, by way of the senses, as the holy leader over everything.
2:23 And he gave it a law. If the mind lives by that law, it will be king over a self-restrained, just, good and courageous kingdom.
## Reason helps, it does not uproot
2:24 "Then how," someone might say, "if reason rules the passions, does it not rule forgetting and not knowing?"
3:1 But that reasoning is quite laughable. Reason is not seen to overcome its own passions, but those of the body.
3:2 For example, none of you can cut out craving, but reason can make sure that you are not made a slave to craving.
3:3 None of you can cut anger out of the soul, but it is possible to come to the soul's help against anger.
3:4 None of you can cut out malice, but reason can fight beside you so that you are not bent by malice.
3:5 For reason is not the one who pulls up the passions by the roots. It is the one who wrestles against them.
## David's thirst
3:6 You can see this more clearly from the thirst of King David.
3:7 All day long David had kept up the attack on the foreigners. With the soldiers of his nation he had cut down many of them.
3:8 When evening came, he made his way to the king's tent, dripping with sweat and very tired. The whole army of our forefathers was camped around that tent.
3:9 All the others were at their supper.
3:10 But the king was thirsty in the extreme. Though he had plenty of springs, he could not cure his thirst with them.
3:11 Instead, an unreasoning craving for the water in the enemy's hands took hold of him. It tightened its grip and parched him, it weakened him and burned him up.
3:12 The king's shield-bearers grumbled about his craving. But two strong young soldiers, out of respect for the king's craving, put on full armor, took a water jar, and climbed over the enemy's palisades.
3:13 They slipped past the guards at the gates and went on through the whole enemy camp, finding their way.
3:14 They boldly found the spring and filled the jar from it with a drink for the king.
3:15 He was all on fire with thirst. Yet he reasoned that the drink was a terrible danger to his soul, since it was counted as worth the same as blood.
3:16 So he set reason against his craving and poured the drink out to God.
3:17 For the self-restrained mind is able to defeat the forces of the passions and to put out the fevers of their frenzies.
3:18 It can throw in wrestling the pains of the body, even when they go beyond all measure. By the nobility of reason it can spit out every hold the passions take.
## The history begins
3:19 Now the time calls us to give the proof from the history of self-restrained reason.
3:20 Our fathers had deep peace because they kept the law well, and they prospered. So King Seleucus Nicanor of Asia even set money apart for them for the temple service, and he approved their way of life.
3:21 Just then some men started a revolt against the harmony the people shared, and they met with many kinds of disaster.
## Simon's betrayal
4:1 A man named Simon was a political rival of Onias, who at that time held the high priesthood for life and was a fine and good man. Simon slandered Onias in every way he could, but he could not damage him in the nation's eyes. So he fled the country, meaning to betray his homeland.
4:2 He came to Apollonius, the governor of Syria, Phoenicia and Cilicia, and said,
4:3 "I have come because I am loyal to the king's interests. I am here to report that private money, many tens of thousands, has been stored up in Jerusalem's treasuries. It has nothing to do with the temple, and by right it belongs to King Seleucus."
4:4 Apollonius took in each point. He praised Simon for looking after the king's interests, then went up to Seleucus and told him about the stored money.
4:5 He was given authority over the matter, and he came quickly into our homeland, bringing the accursed Simon and a very heavy force.
4:6 He said that he had come on the king's orders to take the private money from the treasury.
## Angels at the temple
4:7 The nation protested at his words and spoke against them. They thought it a terrible thing that people who had entrusted their deposits to the temple treasury should be robbed of them. So they tried to stop him as best they could.
4:8 But Apollonius went off to the temple with threats.
4:9 In the temple the priests, with women and children, begged God to shield the place, now that it was being scorned.
4:10 Apollonius was going up with his armed force to seize the money. Then angels riding horses came into view from heaven, their weapons blazing like lightning all around them, and they filled the soldiers with great fear and trembling.
4:11 Apollonius fell down half dead in the court of the temple that is open to all peoples. He stretched out his hands to heaven. Weeping, he pleaded with the Hebrews to pray for him and to make peace for him with the army of heaven.
4:12 He said that he had sinned, so much that he deserved to die. If he was saved, he would sing to all people of how blessed the holy place is.
4:13 Onias the high priest was moved by these words. Besides, he was wary that King Seleucus might think Apollonius had been killed by a human plot and not by divine justice. So he prayed for him.
4:14 And Apollonius, saved against all expectation, went off to tell the king all that had befallen him.
## Antiochus and Jason
4:15 After the death of King Seleucus, the rule passed to his son Antiochus Epiphanes, a proud and fearsome man.
4:16 He removed Onias from the high priesthood and made Jason, the brother of Onias, high priest.
4:17 Jason had agreed to pay three thousand six hundred sixty talents every year if Antiochus would hand the office over to him.
4:18 So Antiochus allowed him to be high priest and to lead the nation.
4:19 Jason made the nation give up its way of life, and he turned its government toward every kind of lawbreaking.
4:20 Not only did Jason build a gymnasium on top of our homeland's citadel; he also put an end to the care of the temple.
## The decree against the law
4:21 Divine Justice was outraged at this, and made war on Antiochus himself.
4:22 He was fighting Ptolemy in Egypt. There news reached him that word had gone around that he was dead, and that the people of Jerusalem were overjoyed at it. So he hurried back against them.
4:23 When he had plundered them, he issued a decree that any of them found living by the law of their fathers should die.
4:24 But he could in no way break down the nation's loyalty by his decrees. He saw all his threats and punishments come to nothing.
4:25 Women were even thrown off cliffs with their babies because they had circumcised them, and they knew beforehand that this would happen to them.
4:26 Since the people despised his decrees, he took it on himself to use tortures, trying to force each person in the nation to taste defiling foods and swear off Judaism.
## The tyrant on his seat
5:1 The tyrant Antiochus took his seat on a high place with his counselors, and his troops stood around them in full armor.
5:2 He ordered his guards to drag in every Hebrew, one by one, and make them taste pork and food sacrificed to idols.
5:3 Anyone who refused to eat the defiling food was to be killed on the wheel.
5:4 Many were rounded up. The first one brought out of the flock and led up close to him was a Hebrew named Eleazar. He was a priest by birth, and in his understanding an expert in the law. He was well along in years, and because of his age many of the tyrant's court knew him.
## Antiochus speaks to Eleazar
5:5 When Antiochus saw him, he said,
5:6 "Before I begin the tortures against you, old man, I would give you this advice: taste the pork and save your life. I have respect for your years and your white hair. But though you have had them so long, you do not seem to me to be a philosopher, since you hold to the religion of the Jews.
5:7 Nature has freely given us this animal's flesh, the finest meat there is. Why do you loathe eating it?
5:8 It is foolish not to enjoy pleasant things that bring no shame. It is wrong to turn your back on nature's gifts.
5:9 And I think you will act even more foolishly if, in your vain pride about the truth,
5:10 you go on to despise me as well, and bring punishment on yourself. Won't you wake up from that babbling philosophy of yours?
5:11 Won't you scatter the nonsense of your reasoning? Take up a mind worthy of your years, and pursue as a philosopher the truth of what is good for you.
5:12 Bow to my kindly encouragement, and have pity on your old age.
5:13 Think about it. Even if some power watches over this religion of yours, it would forgive you for any lawbreaking you do under force."
## Eleazar answers
5:14 When the tyrant had urged him in this way to eat the unlawful meat, Eleazar asked to speak.
5:15 He was given permission to speak, and he began to address the people like this:
5:16 "Antiochus, we have been persuaded that we must live by a divine law. So we think no force is stronger than our obedience to our law.
5:17 That is why we will not agree to break the law in any way.
5:18 Even if our law were not truly divine, as you suppose, and we only thought it divine, even then we would not be free to throw away our good name for godliness.
5:19 So do not think it would be a small sin for us to eat defiling food.
5:20 To break the law in small things or in great ones weighs the same.
5:21 Either way, the law is treated with contempt just the same.
5:22 You mock our philosophy, as if our life by it had no sound reason in it.
5:23 But it trains us thoroughly in self-restraint, so that we rule over all pleasures and cravings. It drills us in courage, so that we endure every pain of our own free will.
5:24 It schools us in justice, so that we deal fairly with everyone in all our ways. It teaches godliness, so that we worship with fitting honor only the God who truly is.
5:25 So we do not eat defiling food. We believe that the law was set in place by God. So we know that the Creator of the world, when he gave the law, shows us a sympathy that fits our nature.
5:26 The foods that would suit our souls he has allowed us to eat. The meats that would work against them he has forbidden.
5:27 You act like a tyrant. You force us not only to break the law, but to eat as well, so that you can laugh at us as we eat this defiling food, which we hate most of all.
5:28 But you will not have this laugh at my expense.
5:29 I will not set aside the holy oaths our forefathers swore to keep the law,
5:30 no, not if you dig out my eyes and melt away my insides.
5:31 Old age has not made me so weak and unmanly that my reason cannot grow young again for godliness.
5:32 Prepare the wheels for this, and blow the fire up hotter.
5:33 I will not pity my old age so much that I myself would tear down the law of our fathers.
5:34 I will not lie to you, law, my teacher. I will not run from you, self-control, my dear friend.
5:35 I will not shame you, reason, lover of wisdom. I will not deny you, priesthood I prize, and understanding of the lawgiving.
5:36 You will not defile the mouth that my old age has made worthy of honor, or the long years of a life lived by the law.
5:37 The fathers will welcome me pure, as one who did not fear your forces, even to death.
5:38 You may rule as a tyrant over the ungodly, but you will not be master over my reasonings about godliness, neither by your words nor by your deeds."
## The whips
6:1 When Eleazar had answered the tyrant's coaxing with a speech of his own, the guards standing by dragged him roughly to the instruments of torture.
6:2 They began by stripping the old man of his clothes. Yet he was still dressed in the dignity that godliness gives.
6:3 Then they bound his arms behind him on each side and beat him with whips.
6:4 From the other side a herald kept shouting, "Obey the king's commands!"
6:5 But Eleazar, a man great of heart and truly noble, was not turned in any way. It was as if he were being tortured in a dream.
6:6 The old man raised his eyes high toward heaven while the whips tore at his flesh. He was streaming with blood, and his sides were cut open.
6:7 His body could not stand the pain, and he fell to the ground. But his reason he held upright and unbending.
6:8 A cruel guard leaped at him and drove his heel into his flanks, so that he would get up when he fell.
6:9 But he endured the pains, scorned the force used on him, and held out through the abuse.
6:10 Like a noble athlete taking blows, the old man defeated his torturers.
6:11 His face was running with sweat and he was gasping hard for breath, yet even his torturers admired his courage.
## The offer to pretend
6:12 Some of them felt sorry for him because he was old.
6:13 Some felt for him because they had known him, and some were amazed at how he held out. So some of the king's men came up to him and said,
6:14 "Why throw yourself away like this, Eleazar, in this misery, with no sense at all?
6:15 We will bring you some of the cooked food. You just pretend to taste the pork, and save yourself."
6:16 Eleazar cried out, as if the advice were a more bitter torture:
6:17 "We are Abraham's children. May we never think so badly that we lose heart and act out a play that is not fit for us.
6:18 It would make no sense if we have lived a life of truth until old age and have kept our good name in it by the law, and then now we turn around.
6:19 We ourselves would become a model of ungodliness for the young, an example of eating defiling food.
6:20 It would be shameful to live a little longer, and to be laughed at by everyone for cowardice all that time.
6:21 The tyrant would despise us as unmanly, while we failed to defend our divine law to the death.
6:22 So you, Abraham's children, die nobly for godliness.
6:23 And you, guards of the tyrant, why are you waiting?"
## The fire and the prayer
6:24 They saw how great of heart he was in the face of their force, and that not even their pity changed him. So they led him to the fire.
6:25 There they used cruelly crafted tools to burn him. They threw him over the fire and poured foul-smelling liquids up his nose.
6:26 He was burned to the bones by now and about to faint. He raised his eyes toward God and said,
6:27 "You know, God, that I could have saved myself. Because of the law I am dying by tortures of fire.
6:28 Be merciful to your nation. Let the penalty we pay on their account be enough for you.
6:29 Make my blood their cleansing, and take my soul as a life in exchange for them."
6:30 With these words the holy man died, noble under torture. To the last of the death agonies he stood firm by reason, because of the law.
## Reason proved master
6:31 Beyond all doubt, then, godly reason is master over the passions.
6:32 If the passions had ruled over reason, I would have given them the witness that they have mastery.
6:33 But now that reason has conquered the passions, it is right for us to give it the authority to lead.
6:34 It is only just for us to admit that the power belongs to reason, since it overcomes even pains that come from outside.
6:35 To say otherwise would be laughable. And I show not only that reason has ruled over pains, but that it rules over pleasures too, and does not give in to them.
