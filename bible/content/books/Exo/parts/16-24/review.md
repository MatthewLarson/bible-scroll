# Hostile review: Exodus 16–24 (part `Exo/16-24`)

Reviewer: agent reviewer, 2026-10-23. Scope: chapters 16–24 only (plain.txt, notes.json, variants.json, glosses.json, chapters.json, topics.json, sources.json in `parts/16-24/`, and the eight new topic cores ten-commandments, mount-sinai, manna, law-of-moses, slavery, eye-for-eye, jethro, joshua). `node tools/build.js --check Exo/16-24` reports 0 errors and 0 warnings (248 verses, 194 notes, 18 variants, 79 glosses, 9 chapter pages, 20 topic contributions).

## Verdict

The study material is strong. I checked well over 100 rabbinic, patristic, classical and modern citations, and almost all are right. This includes the Mekhilta on Jethro's seven names, Sanhedrin 56b on Marah, Makkot 23b–24a, Shabbat 88a–b, Bava Kamma 79b and 83b–84a, Kiddushin 22b, Berakhot 5a and 17a, Mishnah Makkot 1:10, Yadayim 4:4, Rosh Hashanah 3:8, Middot 3:3–4 and Chullin 115b. It also includes Maimonides on coveting and on the qualities of a judge, Philo's <i>Special Laws</i> 3.108, Augustine's <i>Questions</i> 2.80, Ratner and Zuckerman on KTU 1.23, Greenberg's 1951 and 1960 articles, Speiser on <i>pelilim</i>, the Mesad Hashavyahu ostracon, and Eshnunna §53. The views are mostly fair, and the slavery and witch-trial material is honest.

The Ten Commandments themselves (20:2–17) are a fresh translation. The translation problem lies elsewhere. In the case laws of chapters 21–23, in Jethro's chapter, and in parts of 19 and 24, the Plain English Translation follows the NIV, or in places the ESV, verse after verse. I count about 50 verses, and I confirmed 46 of them against the ESV and NIV texts today. Quotations of other books inside the notes show the same habit.

There are also several factual errors on points readers will check:
- the Lutheran numbering of the commandments;
- the Nash Papyrus's order for the coveting command;
- which witnesses follow the Ketiv at 21:8 (the KJV and the Septuagint follow the Qere);
- the claim that <i>ratsach</i> is never used of an execution.

Other problems:
- About 27 prose references put a bare chapter:verse after another book's name, so they link to the wrong Exodus verse.
- 21:22 never says plainly how the verse is used in the abortion debate.
- The eight new topic cores are bare.

Findings: **1 critical, 10 major, 13 minor** (24 in all). Item 25 lists checks that passed.

### 3. MAJOR: the Lutheran numbering of the commandments is wrong in four places

**Where:**
- notes.json 20:3 (views): "Catholic and Lutheran... (9) your neighbor's wife; (10) your neighbor's goods (following the order of Deuteronomy 5:21)."
- chapters.json ch. 20, deep 1 (the table, column "Catholic and Lutheran," rows 9–10).
- topics.json ten-commandments, section "Counting the ten": "split the craving command into two (wife; goods)."
- variants.json 20:17 (first entry), `why`: "the Catholic and Lutheran split into a ninth (wife) and tenth (goods) follows Deuteronomy's order."

**What is wrong:** only the Catholic count follows Deuteronomy's order. Luther's Small and Large Catechisms follow Exodus 20:17. Their ninth commandment is "You shall not covet your neighbor's house." Their tenth is "You shall not covet your neighbor's wife, or his manservant, or his maidservant, or his cattle, or anything that is his." The Lutheran split therefore keeps the house first.

**Fix:** keep Catholic and Lutheran together for commandments 1–8. Then say that Catholics (following Augustine and Deuteronomy 5:21) make the ninth the wife and the tenth the goods, while Lutherans (following Exodus 20:17) make the ninth the house and the tenth the wife and everything else. Correct the table with two rows or a footnote.

### 4. MAJOR: the Nash Papyrus is put on the wrong side of the coveting variant (20:17)

**Where:** variants.json 20:17, "Wife first or house first?", reading 0 `w`: "Nash Papyrus is reported to agree with the house-first order."

**What is wrong:** the Nash Papyrus follows Deuteronomy here. Its line 20 reads, with restorations, "[wife of your neighbor. Do n]ot desire the h[ou]se of your neighbor, [his] field, [his male servant]..." (the transcription in Biblical Archaeology Society, "Nash and the Bible: Side by Side"). That gives the wife first, the second verb <i>titavveh</i> ("desire") for the house, and the added "his field." These are exactly the three Deuteronomic features the entry ascribes only to the Septuagint. The Nash Papyrus is the strongest early Hebrew witness for this reading.

**Fix:** move the Nash Papyrus to reading 1 (with the Septuagint), noting that "wife" is restored in a damaged line. Say that the papyrus mixes the Exodus and Deuteronomy forms throughout, which the 20:10 entry already notes.

### 5. MAJOR: the witnesses for the Ketiv and Qere at 21:8 are wrong

**Where:** variants.json 21:8. Reading 1 lists "Ketiv...; Septuagint; Vulgate," and `why` says "the KJV follows it [the Ketiv]."

**What is wrong:**
- The KJV follows the Qere: "If she please not her master, who hath betrothed her to himself" (`texts/en/Exo.json`). The ASV ("who hath espoused her to himself") and WEB do too. Only Young's Literal Translation follows the Ketiv ("so that he hath not betrothed her").
- The Septuagint reads <i>hēn autō kathōmologēsato</i>, "whom he betrothed to himself." Its <i>autō</i>, "to himself," renders <i>lo</i> ("for him"), which is the Qere.
- The Vulgate's <i>cui tradita fuerat</i>, "to whom she had been handed over," has no negative.

So the major ancient versions support the Qere, which strengthens the writer's choice.

**Fix:** reading 1 should list only the Ketiv and Young's Literal Translation. Reading 0 should add the Septuagint, the Vulgate (check its wording), the KJV, the ASV and most modern versions. Delete the KJV claim from `why`.

### 6. MAJOR: 21:22 does not present the abortion debate explicitly or fairly enough

**Where:** notes.json 21:22 ("Miscarriage or early birth?"), the variants at 21:22–23, chapter 21, and the eye-for-eye topic.

**What is wrong:** the brief and STYLE require the strongest form of each side. The note gives the two readings in one sentence each and never says that the verse is a standard text in the modern abortion debate, or how each side uses it. Missing:
- **Premature-birth reading** (e.g. Jack Cottrell, John Frame, Bruce Waltke in his later work, Walter Kaiser):
  - Hebrew has the verb <i>shakal</i> for "miscarry," used in this very section (23:26, "no woman will lose her young"), and <i>nefel</i> for a stillborn. The law uses neither.
  - <i>Yeled</i> is the ordinary word for a born child.
  - "Harm" (<i>ason</i>) is not limited to the woman.
  - On this reading the child's life is protected by "life for life."
- **Miscarriage reading:**
  - It is used by some to argue that the fetus is not treated as a full person (a fine, not death).
  - Its defenders note that it is the reading of the Mekhilta, Rashi, the ancient Near Eastern parallels and most critical scholars.
  - Jewish law developed it into the view that the fetus is not yet a <i>nefesh</i> (Mishnah Ohalot 7:6; Sanhedrin 72b), which shapes Jewish positions on abortion today.
- **Answers on both sides:** the case is an accidental injury in a brawl, not a deliberate act. So pro-life writers argue that even the miscarriage reading penalizes the loss of the fetus and does not license abortion. Pro-choice writers answer that the law still values the fetus below the mother.
- The early Christian prohibition of abortion (Didache 2:2) did not rest on this verse.

**Fix:** expand the 21:22 note (or add a chapter 21 deep section) with both positions and the names of the scholars who hold them. Say plainly that the text cannot settle the modern question by itself.

### 8. MAJOR: 23:15 translates a reconstructed reading, not the Masoretic Text, with no variant entry

**Where:** plain.txt 23:15, "No one is to come to see me with empty hands," and note 23:15 ("The Plain English Translation keeps the older idiom").

**What is wrong:** the Masoretic vowels read <i>yera'u fanai</i>, "they shall be seen before me" or "appear before me." "See me" follows a proposed earlier vocalization (<i>yir'u</i>). That may well be right, but the site translates the Masoretic Text, and it does so at 23:17 ("appear before the Lord"). ### 9. MAJOR: variants are missing that the notes themselves mention, or that change the meaning

**Where and what:** STYLE says to prefer variants.json for manuscript differences.
- **20:13–15, the order of murder, adultery and theft:**
  - The Nash Papyrus, Philo, Luke 18:20, Romans 13:9 and James 2:11 have adultery before murder.
  - The Septuagint of Exodus in Codex Vaticanus has adultery, theft, murder.
  - At present this is only a sentence in note 20:14. It is the best-known variant in the Decalogue after the Samaritan tenth commandment.
- **21:15–17 in the Septuagint:**
  - The law on cursing parents comes before the law on kidnapping.
  - The kidnapping law adds "one of the sons of Israel," as in Deuteronomy 24:7, which narrows the law.

- **22:20:** the Samaritan Pentateuch reads "to other gods." At present this is only in the note, as "reported."
- **23:7:** the Septuagint adds "for gifts" ("you shall not acquit the ungodly for gifts"). At present this is only in the note.
- **23:23:** the Septuagint and Samaritan Pentateuch add the Girgashites, making seven peoples. At present this is only in the note.
- **17:16:**
  - The note never mentions that the Septuagint reads <i>en cheiri kryphaia</i>, "with a hidden hand," taking <i>kes</i> from <i>kasah</i>, "cover."

- **22:28:** not a variant but a KJV difference. The KJV reads "Thou shalt not revile the gods" (`texts/en`). Note 22:28 explains the Septuagint plural but should say that the KJV follows that sense.

**Fix:** add variants.json entries for 20:13–15 (one per verse, each saying they go together), 21:16, 22:20, 23:7 and 23:23. Add the Septuagint reading to the 17:16 note, and mention the KJV in the 22:28 note.
