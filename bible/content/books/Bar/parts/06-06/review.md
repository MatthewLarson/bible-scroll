# Hostile review: Bar 06-06 (Letter of Jeremiah, Baruch 6, 72 verses in Swete's numbering)

Reviewer: separate hostile-reviewer agent, 2026-10-31. `VERBOSE=1 node tools/build.js --check Bar/06-06` gives 0 errors, 0 warnings (72 verses, 57 notes, 9 variants, 23 glosses, 1 chapter page, 13 topic contributions).

## Verdict

The Plain is faithful and readable. I checked all 72 verses against Swete's Greek in `texts/orig/Bar.json`. I found no mistranslation that reverses a sense, no invented verse and no missing clause. The gods/idols choice is right: the whole argument is the refrain "they are not gods", and "not idols" would not make sense. Quotes of Jeremiah 10, Isaiah 44, Isaiah 46 and Psalm 115 match the site's current Plain, with one exception (finding 11). I confirmed in an open source: Tertullian, Scorpiace 8, and the Plain's English of it; Herodotus 1.199 and 2.66-67 (Macaulay); Rashi on Jeremiah 10:11; Athanasius, Festal Letter 39; Wright's NETS introduction (read in full as PDF); Codex Marchalianus (Q) as 6th-century and containing the Letter. The weakness is the fact layer, which STYLE.md rule 2 forbids. There is a date range that no scholar is cited for and that the writer's own Herodotus note contradicts. The Qumran 7Q2 claim, the Akitu claim and the Codex Γ identification rest on Wikipedia and search snippets. A source key (`djd3`) is cited for a book nobody opened. Several statements about what scholars, reviewers or "many modern translations" say go beyond what the sources say. One variant entry misreports Swete's apparatus. Two spots echo the NETS closely. No critical findings. Eleven major, thirteen minor.

Count: critical 0 / major 11 / minor 13.

## Major

1. **Major. The date range "late 500s to about 100 BC" is unsupported and contradicts the part's own Herodotus note.** Where: chapters.json `readings` (second box), `views.H`, `deep` "Who wrote it, and when", and the settings `readers`. The writer says "most scholars place the letter somewhere between the late 500s and about 100 BC". No source in the part gives that range. The only lower bound I could find in an open page comes from Wikipedia's "Letter of Jeremiah": no earlier than 540 BC because it uses Isaiah 44 and 46, with C. J. Ball's estimate of 317-307 BC (Ball, `ball-epistle-apot`, is in the book's sources). "Seven generations" and the knowledge of Herodotus-like material also push it later. Note 6:42 and the settings say the letter may draw on Herodotus (written about 430 BC), which cannot sit with a 500s date. Fix: replace with the bounds the sources support, with holders named: Ball c. 317 BC from the generation count; upper bound 7Q2 and 2 Maccabees 2:2 (and say that the lower bound is a borrowing argument, not a date anyone fixed). Open Ball or Adams (`adams-thb-letter`, listed in the book's sources) and cite the actual proposal. Otherwise drop the number.

2. **Major. The 7Q2 claim rests on Wikipedia, and `djd3` is cited for a book that was not opened.** Where: notes 6:44 ("The oldest copy", `s: djd3`), chapters `views.H`, settings `readers`. The scratch file `sources-checked.md` says the source was the Wikipedia pages "List of manuscripts from Qumran Cave 7" and "Letter of Jeremiah", plus a WTJ 34 page by Estrada at galaxie.com. Estrada's article is about O'Callaghan's New Testament identifications. I searched and could not find "C. H. Roberts dated the hand to about 100 BC" in it or anywhere else. What I did confirm: 7Q2 is identified as Epistle of Jeremiah (sources say 43b-44), Greek papyrus, Baillet in DJD III; dates in the pages I could open vary (Wikipedia "no later than 100 BC", Harrington "about 100 BC" at earlyjewishwritings.com, a blog "first century BCE"). Fix: state only what an opened source says ("identified by Baillet as verses 43-44; dated about 100 BC by the sources I opened, which differ slightly"), remove the Roberts attribution unless opened, and either open DJD III (or the Leon Levy library entry) or remove `djd3` from `s` on notes and settings. Also "a few words" should be checked against that publication (the fragment has about five short lines).

3. **Major. The Akitu claim rests on Wikipedia, and the page does not say what the note says.** Where: note 6:3 ("Gods in procession"), gloss link `l` "Akitu festival". The note says statues went in procession "above all at the New Year festival (the akitu) in the spring month of Nisan, when the king and the gods, decked in gold and gems, went in procession". Wikipedia's "Akitu" says "adorned with gold and precious stones" (ninth day, citing an SBS Language article), never "gems", and it is internally inconsistent (festival on 1 Nisan in the lead, 4 Nisan in the body). It is a thin base for "above all". Fix: use a scholarly source opened in session (for example a standard work on the Babylonian New Year festival), or trim to what Isaiah 46:1, 46:7 already show and drop the Akitu detail. Also say that the Letter may be a Hellenistic work (see finding 1), so a festival of Nebuchadnezzar's Babylon is background, not a description of what its readers saw.

4. **Major. Note 6:40 says the Plain "keeps the Greek's open sense", but the Plain decides it; the note's authority claims are loose.** Where: plain.txt 6:40 ("plead for the man to speak"), note "Who is meant to speak?". The Greek `ἀξιοῦσιν φωνῆσαι` has no stated subject. The Plain makes the mute man the speaker. NETS, as printed (Wright), makes Bel the one asked to "utter an articulate sound", and Wright says the third clause "is not clear as to whether" the mute man or Bel is to speak. The note then claims "Most readers, like the Vulgate and the KJV, take it that they beg Bel to make the mute man speak". No source says that. The Vulgate (`postulantes ab eo loqui`) and the KJV ("intreat Bel that he may speak") are as open as the Greek. The note also says Wright "calls 6:40-41 perhaps the hardest passage in the letter"; Wright writes that these verses are perhaps the best example of Greek that makes little sense. Fix: either word the Plain to stay open (for example "ask Bel to speak up, as though...") or change the note to say the Plain takes one side; remove "most readers"; correct the Wright paraphrase.

5. **Major. The 6:22 variant misreports Swete's apparatus.** Where: variants.json 6:22 (and the `p` and `why`). Swete's note at 6:22 reads `φοβεισθε] ηφοβηθητε AQΓ`. That is the form ηφοβηθητε (augmented), not `φοβηθῆτε`. The entry prints the A/Q/Γ reading as `μὴ οὖν φοβηθῆτε αὐτά` and the Plain-side English as "So have no fear of them", and says "three other codices have the usual form". The writer silently normalized the reading; it may be an itacism or a digital slip in Swete's text, but the entry must say what is printed. The same apparatus at 6:68 gives Q* a garbled `ςφοβητε`, corrected by Qa to `φοβηθητε`, which suggests Q's first hand had a present there too; this is not mentioned although 6:68 is cited as a refrain. Fix: print the reading as Swete's note has it, mark the normalization, and check Ziegler (`ziegler-jeremias` is in the book's sources) before saying the codices agree with 6:14, 28, 64 and 68. Γ in the Letter: see finding 22 (minor) on the siglum.

6. **Major. Views are attributed to groups, with no holder named, and one is the writer's own inference.** Where: chapters.json `readings` first box ("Catholic and Orthodox readers have received the letter under Jeremiah's name ... The 'seven generations' become a prophecy of the long Dispersion"); note 6:2 explanation (3) ("Those who hold the letter as Jeremiah's read the number as a prophecy of the long Dispersion"); `views.J` last sentence; settings third view ("Jeremiah's own captives"). I found no named person or document in the part who reads the seven generations as a prophecy of the Dispersion, and the first box also says the letter is "a companion to his letter of Jeremiah 29: settle in Babylon", while note 6:1 says this letter says nothing about settling. The Rashi material is real (Rashi on Jeremiah 10:11 says the Aramaic verse is a letter Jeremiah sent to Jeconiah and the exiles, and I read it at Sefaria), but "The Letter of Jeremiah is a long form of that same picture" is the writer's own link and is not labeled as theory. Fix: name who holds each reading (a Father, a commentator, a catechism) from an opened source, or mark it "a reading that follows from taking the letter as Jeremiah's" and cut the Dispersion claim; label the Rashi link as Claude's observation (`spec`).

7. **Major. Note 6:71 and variant 6:71 overstate what NETS and the Latin show.** Where: variants.json 6:71 `b`, `why`. (a) "The NETS introduction ... like many modern translations, takes the idea seriously" is not in Wright: he keeps "marble" because NETS translates the Greek as it stands, adds a footnote "Perhaps sparkling cloth", and says nothing about "many modern translations". (b) "The Latin is a translator's attempt to make sense of it" has no source. The Vulgate says `purpura ... et murice, quae supra illos tineant` (purple and murex, "which are moth-eaten upon them"); the Old Latin may rest on a different Greek or a gloss. Also `p` says "a purple dye instead", but the Latin has both purple and murex, plus the moth. Fix: say that Moore proposed the Hebrew homograph, Wright reports it and keeps "marble" with a footnote of his own, and that the Latin differs; drop both claims.

8. **Major. Budin: one review is made into several, and "though not closed" has no source.** Where: note 6:42 ("reviewers find her case strong though not closed"); settings view "The Greek-speaking Diaspora"; `s: budin-myth`. I read BMCR 2009.04.28: the reviewer says chapter 5 "demonstrates with good arguments" that Lucian and the Letter were influenced by Herodotus ("consciously or subconsciously", p. 111) and raises no objection to that chapter. One reviewer, no reservation. The key `budin-myth` describes the book, which was not opened. Fix: "a reviewer in BMCR (2009) found her argument for this chapter persuasive", cite the review as the source (add a key), and remove "not closed" or find who closes it. Also keep the Greek-speaking Diaspora view labeled as one proposal that follows from Budin; the part presents it as plausible without noting that Budin's claim is about the source of a literary motif, not about where readers lived.

9. **Major. The cats claim is an absolute resting on a search of the site's own files.** Where: note 6:21 ("this verse is their only appearance in any Christian Bible"; `p`: "the one place cats appear in any Bible"). `sources-checked.md` says it was checked by a scripted search of `texts/en/*.json`. That covers the 66, the site's Apocrypha and a few other English texts, not "any Christian Bible" (Ethiopian, Slavonic and others). Fix: "cats are named nowhere else in the 66 books or the KJV Apocrypha" (what the search covered), or find a source for the wider claim. The Herodotus details in the same note (eyebrows shaved, embalming at Bubastis, 2.66-67) are right; I read them in the Gutenberg Macaulay text.

10. **Major. Drift toward NETS at three places.** The NETS text is printed in the PDF the writer cites, so I could compare. Describing, not reproducing: (a) 6:47, the Plain's "For they have left behind lies and disgrace for the generations after them" shares eight words in a row with NETS ("for they have left behind lies and disgrace for"). The Greek `κατέλιπον γὰρ ψεύδη καὶ ὄνειδος τοῖς ἐπιγινομένοις` does not force the same wording ("lies and disgrace" and "left behind" are natural, but the whole run is the NETS sentence). The drift audit lists 6:47 as "changed". (b) 6:42, the Plain's sentence shape ("The women put cords around themselves and sit in the streets, burning bran like incense") follows NETS's clause by clause (cords around themselves, sit in the streets, burning bran as incense). The audit lists 6:42 as changed. (c) 6:58 "better to be a king who" and 6:6 "is with you, and he himself is" are shorter matches, probably forced by the Greek. Fix: recast (a) and (b), for example 6:47 "They leave their lies and shame behind for those who come later"; 6:42 "The women wrap cords around their waists, sit by the roads, and burn bran as their incense" (check "waists": the Greek only says `περιθέμεναι`).

11. **Major. A note quote does not match the current Plain of Isaiah 6:4.** Where: note 6:20 (spec): "Isaiah saw 'the house was filled with smoke' (Isaiah 6:4)". The site's Plain of Isaiah 6:4 reads "smoke kept filling the house". The build did not flag it. Fix: quote the Plain exactly or paraphrase without quotation marks.

## Minor

12. **Minor. The gods/idols decision is right but not recorded as a decision.** The brief said "gods" for Babylonian speech and "idols" otherwise, decided at 6:4. The writer rendered every `θεοί` as "gods" and used "idols" only for `εἴδωλα` (6:72). This is better than the brief: with "idols" the refrain would say "they are not idols", and the argument that they cannot be called gods (6:39, 44, 56, 63) would fall apart. But (a) there is no entry in `content/proposals/works/terms.json` for it (checked) and the brief required a decision with a note; the note is at 6:3 not 6:4; (b) the note's claim that the neuter `auta` versus masculine `autous` "shows the scorn" is an interpretation stated as fact (the Greek neuter simply agrees with the neuter plural `ξύλινα` and so on) and the Plain's single "them" hides it; (c) a young reader meeting "silver gods, gold gods and wooden gods" at 6:3, in the voice of the God/Jeremiah who says they are not gods, may not hear the irony; a gloss at 6:3 would help. Fix: add the term and the reason to the works terms proposal; soften the pronoun claim; add a short gloss.

13. **Minor. The refrain note miscounts and misplaces.** Note 6:14 `p`: "Ten times the letter ends a section"; the note lists five command forms and three question forms (eight), plus three "compare" verses. It says the question "closes ... 6:56", but at 6:56 the question opens the verse and the verse goes on (thieves). The question refrain also has no "do not fear" (so "they are not gods, so do not fear them" is not true of all). "So do not keep on fearing them" (6:22): a present imperative after `μή` can mean "stop" or just a general ban; "keep on" builds a contrast with the aorist forms that the Greek does not clearly make. Note the same wording is used in `chapters.sum` and the idolatry topic ("Ten times"). Fix: say "in some ten places"; correct "closes 6:56"; consider "So do not fear them" for 6:22 with the form noted.

14. **Minor. 6:24 "bought at a price" drops `πάσης`.** `ἐκ πάσης τιμῆς` is "at every price / at full cost" (Vulgate `ex omni pretio`; NETS "at all cost"). The note then builds on it ("The gods bought 'at a price'"). Fix: "bought at any price" or "at great cost".

15. **Minor. 6:25 "they show the whole world".** `τοῖς ἀνθρώποις` is "to people"; "the whole world" is added. `φέρονται` is passive ("are carried"); "ride on people's shoulders" makes the statues the actors, against the point that they are carried.

16. **Minor. Small additions and shifts in the Plain.** 6:13-14 "cannot" for a future "will not" (`οὐκ ἀνελεῖ`, `οὐκ ἐξελεῖται`); 6:19 "licked hollow" (the Greek `ἐκλείχεσθαι` is "licked out") and "no different from" for `ὥσπερ`; 6:26 "whenever one tumbles" for `μή ποτε ... πέσῃ` (the sense is "so that it never falls"); 6:28 "may handle" turns "touch" into permission; 6:29 drops `ὅτι` (the ironic "Because women ..."); 6:36 "again" is added; 6:40 "bring Bel out" adds "out" (`προσενεγκάμενοι`); 6:54 "the beams in the middle" is unclear (the Greek means in the midst of the fire); 6:69 and 6:70 move "wooden, gold-plated, silver-plated gods" to the front, which is a legitimate recast and should be listed as a departure of order only. Fix at the writer's discretion; none changes the sense.

17. **Minor. 6:6 "looking after your lives".** `ἐκζητῶν τὰς ψυχὰς` means "seeking out" and, with `ἐκζητέω`, can mean "require an account of" (Douay "demand an account", Genesis 9:5 in Greek, as the variant's own `b` says). The Plain picks the caring sense "since the verse is meant to comfort", which is an argument from context, not from the word; NETS keeps "seeking out your souls". It is allowed, but the note should say that both senses are open. The entry is also filed as a variant of the text, but it is a difference between the Greek and the Latin (Swete notes no Greek variant); the claim that the Latin first person "is a translator's reading" is unsourced.

18. **Minor. The 6:56 variant has a wrong cross-reference and an unchecked base.** `why` says `διασῴζω` "matches 6:54 and 6:57": 6:57 has no such verb (6:49, 6:54 and 6:58 do). The entry also assumes Vaticanus itself reads `διαθῶσιν`; the part only has Swete's text and note. The departure is sound (Q first hand `διασωθῶσιν`, Alexandrinus `διασώσουσιν`, Vulgate `se liberabunt`, NETS "will not be safe"), but check what B reads in Adams's Vaticanus commentary (`adams-baruch-commentary`, in the book's sources) or Ziegler before saying "Vaticanus". 6:69: the entry is correct that Swete prints Q's `σικυηράτῳ` and notes B `συκηράτῳ` and A `συκηεράτῳ`; the "departure" there is only a word-order recast (finding 16), not a text choice.

19. **Minor. Unsourced generalizations in notes.** 6:26 "Many ancient peoples set food and drink out for the dead"; 6:33 "In Babylon the gods were thought to choose the king"; 6:52 "Rain was the test of a god in the ancient world"; 6:19 "Kardia ... also means the core or inside of a thing" (a lexical claim with no lexicon cited); 6:28 "The verse shows that the letter's first readers were Jews who kept the purity laws" (the verse shows the writer's standard; the settings repeat it as a fact about readers); 6:43 "The cord broken by the man is a sign that the woman has done her duty" (a reading of Herodotus stated as fact); 6:43 "The letter's scorn is aimed less at the women than at a worship". Each needs a source in an opened page, a hedge-free rewording as the writer's observation, or removal.

20. **Minor. Reading level.** Idioms and words above grade 8: "take the hint", "put their heads together", "run through it on themselves", "tight spot", "pickle", "dethroning", "scepter", "fortify", "artisans", "tarnish". Several are glossed (6:17, 6:27, 6:36, 6:45), the rest are not. 6:17 is one long sentence with two comparisons. No em-dashes found.

21. **Minor. Lens balance and a possible missing card.** `views.P` is only the Church of England (Article VI, quoted correctly in the part); Reformed and Lutheran positions are not given (for example the Westminster Confession's statement on the Apocrypha, not checked here). `views.C` says "Catholics have not read the letter as a ban on Christian images; see the card at Deuteronomy 4", with no source; the Deuteronomy 4 card exists, but it is not about this letter. I could not verify (a search found nothing) whether the Letter was a proof text in the iconoclasm debates or the Reformation; the writer should check before deciding that no card is needed. `views.J` has Rashi only; Jerome's remarks on the absence of Baruch and the Letter from the Hebrew are in the book-level work.

22. **Minor. Manuscript sigla and small apparatus details.** Q = Codex Marchalianus, 6th century, contains Baruch and the Letter: confirmed (Wikipedia). Γ = Codex Cryptoferratensis was identified from search snippets; the only source I found (a study list of LXX uncials, not Swete) dates it 8th-9th century and calls it Grottaferrata; there is also an NT uncial 098 with the same nickname, which is a different manuscript. The part gives no date for Γ, which is correct; keep it that way. A search result also says Γ contains only 7b-24a of the Letter, which fits Swete's notes (`hiat Γ` at 6:12, Γ readings up to 6:22). Note 6:40 says Alexandrinus "adds 'then to speak'": Swete's note (`φωνῆσαι] τοτε λαλησαι A`) says A reads it instead of `φωνῆσαι`. Note 6:1 says the Vulgate numbers as Swete does (72 verses); the Vulgate has 72 verses but its internal divisions differ (its 6:53 holds the Greek crows of 6:54).

23. **Minor. Glosses and settings.** Gloss 6:2 "seven generations" = "many lifetimes" settles the debated question the note 6:2 leaves open; gloss 6:27 adds "meat"; gloss 6:40 identifies Bel as the statue of Marduk without saying that this is the usual identification. Settings: `readers` states that the first readers "knew Jeremiah in Hebrew (6:69)" as fact (6:69 shows what the writer knew, as Wright says); the first setting's `why` gives the purpose inside the letter's fiction and does not separate it from the purpose for the real Jewish readers, who are in `readers`. A debated audience should be labeled once in `why`.

24. **Minor. Source credits.** Herodotus quotes are from Macaulay's translation (the note says so), but `herodotus-histories` does not credit the translator. Tertullian's English is the New Advent (ANF) text, which I confirmed word for word, and Rashi is the Judaica Press text; neither translation is credited in the part. `sources.json` in the part carries only `wright-nets-letjer`; the other keys come from other books (`djd3` from Lam, `budin-myth` from 1 Kings, `tertullian-scorpiace` from John). That works in the build but see findings 2 and 8 on what they claim to have been read.
