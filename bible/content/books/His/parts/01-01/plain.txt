## Why Tacitus is writing
1:1 I begin my work with the year when Servius Galba was consul for the second time, with Titus Vinius as his colleague. The earlier 820 years since Rome was founded have had plenty of writers, and as long as they were telling the story of the Roman people, they wrote with equal eloquence and freedom. After the war at Actium, when peace made it necessary for all power to go to one man, those great minds disappeared. Truth suffered in many ways too. First came ignorance of public affairs, which now seemed to belong to someone else. Then came the urge to flatter, or else hatred of the men in power. So neither side cared what later generations would think, since one was hostile and the other servile. We turn away easily from a writer who flatters, but we listen eagerly to spite and malice, because flattery carries the ugly charge of servility, while malice looks like honesty. Galba, Otho and Vitellius did me no favors and no harm, so I have no personal reason to praise or blame them. I won't deny that Vespasian started my career, Titus raised it, and Domitian took it further. But anyone who promises untouched honesty has to speak of every man without affection and without hatred. If I live long enough, I've saved for my old age a richer and less worrying subject: the reign of the divine Nerva and the rule of Trajan, a rare and happy time when you can think what you like and say what you think.
## An age of disasters and a few good people
1:2 The work I'm starting is rich in disasters, terrible in its battles, torn by revolts, and savage even in peace. Four emperors were killed by the sword. There were three civil wars, more wars with foreign enemies, and often wars that were both at once. Things went well in the East and badly in the West. Illyricum was in turmoil, the Gauls wavered, Britain was completely conquered and then immediately let go. The Sarmatae and the Suevi rose up against us together, the Dacians made a name for themselves through losses suffered on both sides, and the Parthians very nearly went to war, fooled by a fake Nero. Italy too was crushed by new disasters, or by old ones that came back after a long run of centuries. Cities on the richest coast of Campania were swallowed up or buried. Rome was ravaged by fires, its oldest temples were burned down, and the Capitol itself was set on fire by the hands of citizens. Sacred rites were defiled, and there were great adulteries among the elite. The sea was full of exiles, and the cliffs were stained with murders. In the city the cruelty was even worse. Being noble, rich, or having held an office or turned one down was enough for a charge, and virtue was the surest way to be destroyed. The informers' rewards were as hated as their crimes. Some of them took priesthoods and consulships as spoils, others took posts as procurators and places of inside power, and they stirred everything up with hatred and terror. Slaves were bribed against their masters and freedmen against their patrons, and men who had no enemy were ruined by their friends.
1:3 Still, the age was not so barren of virtue that it gave no good examples. Mothers went with their sons into flight, and wives followed their husbands into exile. There were bold relatives and steady sons-in-law, and slaves whose loyalty held out even under torture. Famous men faced their last hours bravely, and their ends matched the praised deaths of the ancients. Besides all the many reversals of human life, there were signs in the sky and on the earth, warnings from thunder, and forecasts of what was coming, cheerful or gloomy, uncertain or plain. Never was it proved more clearly, by worse disasters for the Roman people or by fairer signs, that the gods care nothing for our safety, only for punishing us.
## Rome and the armies when Nero died
1:4 Before I set out what I've planned, I think I should go back and describe the state of the city, the mood of the armies, the condition of the provinces, and what was strong and what was sick in the whole world. That way we can understand not only the turns and outcomes of events, which are mostly chance, but also the reason and causes behind them. Nero's end was welcome at first and set off a burst of joy, but it also stirred up all sorts of feelings, not only in the city among the senators, the people and the city's soldiers, but in every legion and every commander. The secret of the empire was out: an emperor could be made somewhere other than Rome. The senators were glad, and they used their new freedom more boldly because the emperor was new and away from Rome. The leading Roman Knights shared the senators' joy. The respectable part of the people, tied to the great houses, along with the dependents and freed slaves of condemned and exiled men, were full of hope. The dirty crowd that lived at the circus and the theater, the worst of the slaves, and men who had run through their money and lived off Nero's shameful excesses, were gloomy and hungry for rumors.
1:5 The city's soldiers had long been bound by their oath to the Caesars. They had been led to abandon Nero by scheming and pressure more than by their own choice. Now they saw that the bonus promised in Galba's name wasn't being paid, that in peacetime there was less room than in war for great services and great rewards, and that the favor of an emperor made by the legions had already been spoken for. So they were ready for change, and the crime of their prefect Nymphidius Sabinus, who was after the throne for himself, stirred them up further. Nymphidius was crushed in the very act, but even with the head of the revolt gone, many soldiers still felt guilty, and there was no shortage of talk about Galba's old age and greed. His strictness, once praised and celebrated in the army's reputation, wore on men who turned up their noses at the old discipline. For fourteen years under Nero they had grown used to loving their emperors' vices as much as they had once respected their virtues. On top of that came a saying of Galba's, noble for the state but dangerous for himself: "I pick my soldiers, I don't buy them." His other acts were not in line with it.
## Galba's bloody road to power
1:6 Titus Vinius and Cornelius Laco, one the worst of men and the other the most spineless, were wrecking the weak old man. He carried the hatred for their crimes and the contempt for their laziness. Galba's journey to Rome had been slow and bloody. He had Cingonius Varro, the consul-elect, and Petronius Turpilianus, a man of consular rank, put to death: Varro as a partner of Nymphidius, Turpilianus as a general of Nero's. Both died without a hearing or a defense, as if they were innocent men. Galba entered the city after thousands of unarmed soldiers had been cut down. It was a bad omen, and even the men who had done the killing were afraid of it. He brought the Spanish legion in, while the one Nero had raised from the fleet was still there, so the city was full of unfamiliar troops. On top of that there were many detachments from Germany, Britain and Illyricum. Nero had picked them and sent them ahead to the Caspian Gates for the war he was preparing against the Albani, and then recalled them to put down Vindex's uprising. That was plenty of material for a revolution. It didn't lean toward any one man, but it was ready for anyone bold enough.
1:7 It happened that the killings of Clodius Macer and Fonteius Capito were reported at the same time. Macer was clearly stirring up trouble in Africa, and the procurator Trebonius Garutianus killed him on Galba's order. Capito was trying the same thing in Germany, and the legion commanders Cornelius Aquinus and Fabius Valens killed him before any order came. Some believed that Capito, though foul and stained with greed and lust, had kept away from any thought of revolution. They said the legates themselves urged him to go to war, and when they couldn't push him into it, they invented a charge and a trick against him. And they said Galba approved it either from a changeable mind or to avoid digging deeper, since whatever had been done could not be undone. Either way, both killings were taken badly. Once a ruler is hated, whatever he does, good or bad, brings the same resentment. Everything was for sale, the freedmen had too much power, and the slaves grabbed at quick gains, hurrying to get rich with an old man for a master. The new court had the same evils as the old, just as heavy and less easy to excuse. Galba's very age drew laughter and disgust from people used to Nero's youth, who, as the crowd does, compared their emperors by their looks and good figure.
## The provinces and the armies
1:8 That was the mood in Rome, as far as you can say it of such a crowd. Among the provinces, Spain was governed by Cluvius Rufus, a skilled speaker and good at the arts of peace but with no experience of war. The Gauls were tied to Galba beyond their memory of Vindex by the recent gift of Roman citizenship and the promise of lighter tribute in the future. But the Gallic states nearest the German armies had not been given the same honor, and some had even had land taken away. These states measured other people's gains against their own injuries with equal bitterness. The German armies were worried and angry, which is the most dangerous mood for so much strength. They were proud of their recent victory and afraid because they seemed to have backed the other side. They had been slow to break from Nero, and Verginius had not declared for Galba at once. It was unclear whether he had refused to rule, though everyone agreed the soldiers had offered him the empire. Even people who couldn't call Capito's killing unjust were angry about it. The army had no leader, since Verginius had been called away on the pretext of friendship with the emperor. That he wasn't sent back, and was even put on trial, they took as an accusation against themselves.
1:9 The army of Upper Germany looked down on its legate, Hordeonius Flaccus, who was weak from old age and lame feet, with no steadiness and no authority. Even when the soldiers were calm he couldn't control them, and when they were in a frenzy his weak hold only made them hotter. The legions of Lower Germany had gone a long time without a commander of consular rank, until Aulus Vitellius arrived by Galba's appointment. He was the son of the Vitellius who had been censor and three times consul, and that seemed to be enough. The army in Britain was not angry at all. No other legions behaved more blamelessly through all the shocks of the civil wars, whether because they were far away and cut off by the Ocean, or because frequent campaigns had taught them to hate the enemy instead. Illyricum was quiet too, although the legions Nero had called up from there had sent messengers to Verginius while they waited around in Italy. But the armies were separated by long distances, which is the best protection for keeping soldiers loyal, so they did not mix either their vices or their strength.
## The East and the rest of the empire
1:10 The East hadn't moved yet. Licinius Mucianus governed Syria and its four legions, a man equally famous for good luck and bad. As a young man he had eagerly courted the friendship of important people. Later his money ran out and his position was shaky. He also suspected that Claudius was angry with him, so he withdrew to a remote part of Asia, as close to an exile as he later was to an emperor. He was a mix of luxury and hard work, of friendliness and arrogance, of good and bad skills. He gave himself over to pleasure when he had spare time, but whenever it was useful he showed great ability. In public you could praise him; in private he had a bad reputation. Still, with his subordinates, his close associates and his fellow officers, he had power through many kinds of charm. It would have been easier for him to hand over power than to hold it. Flavius Vespasian was running the war in Judea with three legions (Nero had chosen him as commander). Vespasian had no wish or intention against Galba. He had even sent his son Titus to pay his respects and show his loyalty, as I'll tell in its place. As for the hidden decrees of fate, the omens and the oracles that marked out the throne for Vespasian and his sons, we believed in them only after his success.
1:11 From the time of the divine Augustus on, Roman Knights have governed Egypt in place of kings, together with the forces that keep it in check. It was thought best to keep at home a province that was hard to reach, rich in grain, and restless and excitable through superstition and wildness, a people who knew nothing of laws and nothing of magistrates. Its governor then was Tiberius Alexander, a native of the country. Africa and its legions, with Clodius Macer dead, would accept any emperor at all, having tried a lesser master first. The two Mauretanias, Raetia, Noricum, Thrace and the other provinces held by procurators were pulled toward friendship or hostility by the nearest army, since they felt the touch of stronger neighbors. The unarmed provinces, and Italy above all, were open to any kind of slavery and were going to go to the winner as the prize of the war. That was the state of the Roman world when Servius Galba, consul for the second time, and Titus Vinius, his colleague, began a year that was the last for them and nearly the last for the state.
## Mutiny in Upper Germany and Galba's search for an heir
1:12 A few days after the first of January, a letter came from the procurator Pompeius Propinquus in Belgica. The legions of Upper Germany had broken the respect due to their oath, were demanding a different emperor, and left the choice to the Senate and people of Rome, so that the revolt would be taken more gently. This hurried Galba's plan, since he had been thinking over an adoption for some time, with himself and with his closest men. All through those months the whole city talked of nothing else, first because people felt free to say such things, and later because Galba was old and worn out. Few people had judgment or love of the state. Many, out of foolish hopes, named this man or that as the future heir, according to whose friend or dependent they were, in rumors meant to win favor. They also did it out of hatred for Titus Vinius, who grew more powerful every day and for the same reason more hated. Galba's very easiness made his friends' greed stronger, since they were gaping at so much good fortune. With a man so weak and so trusting, wrongdoing carried less fear and more reward.
## Rival candidates: Vinius, Laco, Icelus and Otho
1:13 The power of the emperor's office was split between Titus Vinius, the consul, and Cornelius Laco, the prefect of the Praetorian Guard. Icelus, a freed slave of Galba's, had just as much favor. He had been given the gold rings of a Roman Knight and went by the knightly name Marcianus. These men were at odds, each pushing his own interests in smaller matters, and over the choice of a successor they split into two factions. Vinius backed Marcus Otho. Laco and Icelus agreed, not so much on backing one man as on backing someone else. Galba knew about the friendship between Otho and Titus Vinius. Rumors, from people who let nothing pass in silence, had the two as son-in-law and father-in-law, because Vinius had a widowed daughter and Otho was unmarried. I believe Galba also felt concern for the state, which would have been rescued from Nero for nothing if it ended up in Otho's hands. Otho had a neglected boyhood and a reckless youth, and had won Nero's favor by competing with him in luxury. That is why Nero had put Poppaea Sabina, his favorite mistress, in Otho's care, as a man who knew his secrets, until Nero could get rid of his wife Octavia. Soon Nero suspected Otho with Poppaea too, and sent him off to the province of Lusitania under the title of governor. Otho ran the province with kindness. He was the first to go over to Galba's side, was not lazy, and while the war lasted he was the most impressive of the men on the scene. The hope of being adopted, which he had formed right away, grew sharper every day. Many of the soldiers favored him, and Nero's court was drawn to him as a man like Nero.
## Galba chooses Piso
1:14 After the news of the mutiny in Germany, Galba was uneasy about where the armies' force would break out, though nothing was yet certain about Vitellius. He didn't even trust the soldiers in the city. So he turned to what he thought was the only remedy and held the election of an emperor. Besides Vinius and Laco, he brought in Marius Celsus, the consul-elect, and Ducenius Geminus, the prefect of the city. After saying a few words about his old age, he ordered Piso Licinianus to be sent for. Whether he did this by his own choice, or, as some believed, because Laco pressed him, is not clear. Laco had formed a friendship with Piso in the circle of Rubellius Plautus. But Laco was clever and backed him as if Piso were a stranger to him, and Piso's good name lent weight to the advice. Piso was the son of Marcus Crassus and Scribonia, noble on both sides. In looks and manner he was a man of the old kind. To people who judged him fairly he was stern; to people who judged him less kindly he seemed gloomy. That side of his character, the more it worried the anxious, the more it pleased the man adopting him.
## Galba's speech to Piso
1:15 They say Galba took Piso by the hand and spoke like this: "If I were a private man adopting you by the law of the Curiae before the pontiffs, as our custom is, it would be an honor to me to bring a descendant of Gnaeus Pompeius and Marcus Crassus into my house, and it would be a distinction for you to add the Sulpician and Lutatian names to your own noble line. As it is, I have been called to power by the agreement of gods and men. Your fine character and my love of country move me to offer you, a man at peace, the supreme power our ancestors fought over and I won by war. I follow the example of the divine Augustus, who set in the place next to his own first his sister's son Marcellus, then his son-in-law Agrippa, then his grandsons, and last Tiberius Nero, his stepson. But Augustus looked for a successor in his own house, and I look for one in the state. It isn't that I lack relatives or comrades from the war, but I did not get power by scheming either. Let my choice show its own proof: not only my relatives, whom I put behind you, but yours too. You have a brother as noble as you and older, who would deserve this place if you weren't the better man. You are at an age that has already left the cravings of youth behind, and you have lived so that nothing in your past needs an excuse. So far you have only borne bad fortune. Good fortune tests the spirit with sharper stings, because misery can be put up with, but success corrupts us. Loyalty, freedom and friendship, the best things in the human spirit, you will hold on to with the same steadiness, but others will wear them down by pleasing you. Flattery will break in, and smooth talk, and the worst poison for real affection, everyone's own self-interest. Even if you and I speak to each other today in perfect honesty, the rest will gladly speak to our fortune rather than to us. Telling a ruler what he ought to do takes great effort, but flattering any ruler at all is done without any feeling.
1:16 If the huge body of the empire could stand and keep its balance without a ruler, I would have been the right man to start a republic. As it is, we have long since reached the point where my old age can give the Roman people nothing better than a good successor, and your youth nothing better than a good emperor. Under Tiberius, Caligula and Claudius we were, so to speak, the inheritance of one family. That we have begun to be chosen will stand in place of freedom. Now that the house of the Julii and Claudii is finished, adoption will find the best man. Being born to emperors is chance, and nothing more is made of it. In adoption the judgment is free, and if you want to choose, general agreement points out the man. Keep Nero before your eyes, swollen with the pride of a long line of Caesars. It was not Vindex with his unarmed province, or I with my one legion, who took him off our necks, but his own savagery and his own luxury. And there had been no earlier case of an emperor condemned. We were brought in by war and by those who judged us, so we will meet resentment however good we are. But don't be frightened if two legions are still not calm in this shaking of the world. I myself did not come to power in a safe time, and once they hear of your adoption, people will stop thinking of me as old, which is the one thing held against me now. Nero will always be missed by the worst men. You and I have to make sure he isn't missed by the good as well. To go on advising you doesn't suit this moment, and my whole plan is fulfilled if I have chosen you well. The most useful and shortest test of good and bad policies is to think about what you yourself would have wanted or not wanted under another emperor. Here it isn't like peoples under kings, with one ruling house and everyone else slaves. You will rule men who can't stand being total slaves and can't handle total freedom." Galba said all this and more, as if he were making an emperor; the others spoke to Piso as if he were already one.
## Piso is announced to the camp and the Senate
1:17 They say that Piso showed no sign of a troubled or delighted heart, either when everyone looked at him at once or afterward when all eyes were fixed on him. His words to Galba, as his father and emperor, were respectful, and about himself he was modest. Nothing changed in his face or manner, like a man who was more able to rule than eager to. They then discussed whether to announce the adoption from the Rostra, in the Senate, or in the camp. They decided to go to the camp. It would be an honor to the soldiers, and although their favor would be shameful to buy with bribes and scheming, it was worth having if it could be won honorably. Meanwhile the public, waiting for news, had gathered around the Palace, impatient for the great secret. Those who tried to hold back the badly guarded rumor only made it bigger.
1:18 January 10 was an ugly day, and thunder, lightning and threats from the sky were worse than usual. In ancient times this was taken as a reason to break up an assembly. It did not stop Galba from going to the camp. He either despised such things as accidents or believed that what fate holds can't be avoided even when it is signaled. He spoke to the packed assembly of soldiers as briefly as an emperor does. He announced that he was adopting Piso, as the divine Augustus had done and as soldiers do when one picks a comrade. So that hiding the mutiny wouldn't make it look bigger, he added on his own that the 4th and 22nd legions, led by a few ringleaders, had done no more than speak and shout, and would soon be back in line. He added no flattery to his speech and no payment. Still, the tribunes, the centurions and the soldiers nearest him gave answers that pleased. The rest were gloomy and silent, as if the war had cost them the bonus they had come to count on even in peacetime. It is agreed that their feelings could have been won over by even a small gift from the stingy old man. His old-fashioned hardness hurt him, and so did his extreme strictness, which we can no longer stand.
1:19 Then in the Senate Galba's speech was no more polished and no longer than the one to the soldiers. Piso's speech was gracious, and the senators were on his side. Many were for him from the heart, those who had not wanted him were more lavish, and the middle group, who were the most, were ready with quick compliments while chasing their own hopes and caring nothing for the public good. In the four days between his adoption and his killing, Piso said and did nothing more in public. News of the revolt in Germany was coming in more often each day, and the city was quick to take in and believe any new thing as long as it was bad, so the senators voted to send envoys to the German army. It was discussed in private whether Piso should go too, giving the mission a grander cover: they would carry the Senate's authority and he the dignity of Caesar. It was also thought wise to send Cornelius Laco, prefect of the Praetorian Guard, with them, but he blocked the plan. The Senate had left the choosing of envoys to Galba, and in naming them, excusing some and replacing others, he showed a shameful lack of firmness, giving in to anyone who pushed to stay or to go out of fear or hope.
## Money, and officers dismissed
1:20 Money was the next concern. Looking into everything, it seemed fairest to take it back from the people who were the reason for the shortage. Nero had poured out two thousand two hundred million sesterces in gifts. Galba ordered the recipients called up one by one, leaving each of them a tenth of what he had been given. But they barely had a tenth left, having run through other people's money the same way they had run through their own, until the most greedy and ruined had no land and no loans, only the equipment for their vices. Thirty Roman Knights were put in charge of collecting. It was a new kind of office, made heavy by lobbying and by the numbers. Everywhere there were auction spears and buyers, and the city was unsettled by lawsuits. Still, there was great joy that those Nero had given to were as poor as those he had robbed. Around those days, some tribunes were dismissed: Antonius Taurus and Antonius Naso from the Praetorian Guard, Aemilius Pacensis from the city cohorts, and Julius Fronto from the watch. It did nothing for the rest and only made them afraid that the government was using cunning and fear to remove one man at a time, with everyone under suspicion.
## Otho decides to act
1:21 Meanwhile Otho, who had no hope if things stayed settled and whose every plan depended on turmoil, was pushed by many things at once: a luxury that would have embarrassed even an emperor, a poverty hardly bearable even for a private man, anger at Galba, and envy of Piso. He even pretended to be afraid, to whip up his own desire. He told himself that he had been too heavy a burden to Nero, and that he shouldn't expect another Lusitania, another honor of exile. Rulers always suspect and hate the man named as next in line. That had hurt him with the old emperor, and it would hurt him more with a young man who was savage by nature and had been made fierce by a long exile. Otho could easily be killed. So he must act and dare now, while Galba's authority was shaky and Piso's had not yet taken hold. Times of change were good for great attempts, and there was no use in delay when sitting still was more dangerous than recklessness. Death comes equally to everyone by nature, but it is marked by being forgotten or by glory among later generations. If the innocent and the guilty meet the same end, a man of spirit at least deserves to die for something.
1:22 Otho's spirit was not as soft as his body. His closest freedmen and slaves, who were treated with more license than in any private home, kept bringing up Nero's court and its luxury, its adulteries, its marriages and the other pleasures of absolute power. To a man hungry for such things, they showed them off as his if he dared, and scolded him, if he stayed still, as belonging to others. The astrologers pushed him too, claiming from their star-watching that new upheavals were coming and a glorious year for Otho. That kind of man is untrustworthy to the powerful and deceives the hopeful, and in our city he will always be forbidden and always kept. Many of them had been in Poppaea's private circle, the worst tools of the imperial marriage. One of them, Ptolemaeus, had been with Otho in Spain and had promised that Otho would outlive Nero. After events gave his words credit, he used guesswork and the talk of people who set Galba's age beside Otho's youth to persuade him he would be called to rule. But Otho took the predictions as the fruit of skill and a warning from the fates, with the human mind's eagerness to believe in what is hidden. And Ptolemaeus did not hold back. He was now pushing him toward the crime itself, which is the easiest step from a wish like that.
1:23 It is not clear whether the thought of crime came suddenly. Otho had long been courting the soldiers, either hoping to succeed Galba or preparing some deed. On the road, in the column and at the guard posts he would call the oldest soldiers by name, and by recalling Nero's tours he would call them his old comrades. Some he recognized, others he asked about, and he helped them with money or influence. He kept dropping complaints and ambiguous remarks about Galba into his talk, and anything else that would stir up the crowd. The hard marches, the lack of supplies and the harshness of command seemed all the more cruel to men who were used to sailing to the lakes of Campania and the cities of Achaia, and who now struggled under arms over the Pyrenees, the Alps and endless stretches of road.
## Otho buys the soldiers
1:24 The soldiers' minds were already on fire, and Maevius Pudens, one of Tigellinus's inner circle, added fuel to the flames. He drew in the most fickle by temper and anyone short of money and headed toward new cravings, and little by little he went so far that, under the pretense of a banquet, whenever Galba dined at Otho's he handed out a hundred sesterces to every man of the cohort on guard duty. Otho added to this public gift with private rewards to individuals, and he was so bold a corrupter that when a guardsman, Cocceius Proculus, had a boundary dispute with his neighbor, Otho bought the neighbor's whole field with his own money and gave it to him. All this was helped by the carelessness of the prefect, who missed what was out in the open as well as what was hidden.
1:25 Then Otho put his freedman Onomastus in charge of the coming crime. Onomastus brought in Barbius Proculus, who gave out the watchword to the bodyguard, and Veturius, the deputy of the same unit. When he had learned in various conversations that they were clever and bold, he loaded them with payments and promises, and gave them money to sound out more men. So two common soldiers took on the job of transferring the empire of the Roman people, and they did transfer it. Only a few were let in on the secret. They worked on the wavering minds of the rest in different ways: on the more distinguished soldiers by saying that Nymphidius's favors made them suspect, and on the crowd and the others by their anger and despair over the donative being put off so many times. Some were set on fire by memories of Nero and longing for the old license. All of them shared a fear of being sent to serve somewhere else.
## The plot nearly breaks out
1:26 The infection spread to the legions and auxiliaries too, whose minds were already shaken once it was known that the German army's loyalty was wavering. The disloyal were so ready for mutiny, and even the loyal so quiet about it, that on the day after the Ides they would have seized Otho as he came home from dinner, if they hadn't feared the uncertainty of the night, the soldiers' camps scattered all over the city, and the difficulty of getting drunk men to agree. It wasn't concern for the state, which they were sober enough to plan to stain with their emperor's blood. They were afraid that in the dark some man shown to the soldiers of the Pannonian or German armies would be picked instead of Otho, whom most of them didn't know. Many signs of the coming outbreak were smothered by those in the secret. Some reached Galba's ears, and the prefect Laco brushed them off. He had no idea how soldiers think, and he opposed any plan, however good, that he hadn't brought himself, and stubbornly stood against men who knew more.
## Otho is made emperor
1:27 On the eighteenth day before the first of February, as Galba was sacrificing in front of the temple of Apollo, the reader of entrails Umbricius told him that the entrails were gloomy, that plots were closing in, and that there was an enemy at home. Otho heard it, since he was standing right next to Galba, and he took it the opposite way, as cheerful and favorable to his own plans. Not long after, his freedman Onomastus told him that the architect and the contractors were waiting for him. That had been agreed as the sign that the soldiers were gathering and the conspiracy was ready. Otho left, and when people asked why, he said he was buying some property that he suspected was unsound because of its age, and so had to inspect it first. Leaning on his freedman, he went through the house of Tiberius into the Velabrum, and from there to the golden milestone below the temple of Saturn. There twenty-three bodyguards hailed him as emperor, and as he trembled at how few they were, they quickly put him in a chair, drew their swords and carried him off. About as many more soldiers joined along the road, some because they knew of the plot, most from astonishment, some with shouts and swords, some in silence, planning to take their feelings from how it turned out.
1:28 The tribune on duty in the camp was Julius Martialis. He was stunned by how big and sudden the crime was, or he feared that so many of the troops were corrupted that resisting would mean death, and he made many suspect he was part of it. The other tribunes and centurions also put what was safe now ahead of what was uncertain and honorable. The mood was such that a few dared the worst crime, more wanted it, and everyone let it happen.
## Piso speaks to the palace guard
1:29 Meanwhile Galba knew nothing and was busy with his sacrifice, wearing out the gods on behalf of an empire that was already someone else's. A rumor reached him that some senator, no one knew who, was being carried off to the camp. Soon it was said that the man being carried off was Otho. At the same time people came from all over the city, each one who had met the procession, some making the danger bigger out of fear, others making it smaller than the truth, not forgetting to flatter even then. After they talked it over, they decided to test the mood of the cohort on guard at the Palace, but not through Galba himself, whose authority was being kept whole for bigger remedies. Piso called the soldiers together in front of the steps of the house and spoke to them like this: "Comrades, this is the sixth day since I was made Caesar by adoption, not knowing what was coming, or whether this name was something to wish for or to fear. It is in your hands now what happens to our house and to the state. It isn't that I dread the darker outcome for my own sake. I have known bad fortune, and right now I am learning that good fortune is no less dangerous. I grieve for my father Galba, for the Senate and for the empire itself, if we must either die today or do what is just as wretched in the eyes of good men, which is to kill. In the last upheaval we had this comfort, that the city stayed free of blood and power changed hands without a fight. My adoption was thought to have made sure there would be no room for war even after Galba.
1:30 I will claim nothing for my noble birth or my self-control, since there is no need to list virtues when comparing myself with Otho. The vices are the only thing he boasts of, and they brought the empire down even when he was only the emperor's friend. Is he going to earn the empire by his bearing and his walk, or by that womanish finery? Those people are fooled whom luxury tricks with the look of generosity. He will know how to waste, but he will not know how to give. Right now he is turning over in his mind his debaucheries, his drinking parties and his gatherings of women. These he thinks are the prizes of ruling, with the lust and pleasure all his own and the shame and disgrace for everyone else. No one has ever used power won by shameful means for good. The agreement of the whole human race named Galba Caesar, and Galba, with your agreement, named me. Even if the state, the Senate and the people were empty names, it would still be in your interest, comrades, that the worst men not make the emperor. We have sometimes heard of legions rising against their commanders. Your loyalty and your good name have stayed unharmed to this day. Even Nero deserted you, and you did not desert Nero. Are fewer than thirty runaways and deserters, whom no one would let choose a centurion or tribune for themselves, going to hand out the empire? Do you accept this example, and by staying quiet make the crime yours too? This lawlessness will pass into the provinces, and the results of the crimes will fall on us, but the results of the wars will fall on you. And you won't get any more for the murder of your emperor than what is given to the innocent, but you will get a bonus from us for your loyalty, just as you would get one from others for your crime."
## The troops divide
1:31 The bodyguards drifted away, but the rest of the cohort, who hadn't shown any disrespect to the speaker, took up their standards. They did it on the spur of the moment, as often happens in a disturbance, with no plan yet, and not, as people later believed, out of treachery or a plan to deceive. Marius Celsus was sent to the picked troops of the Illyrian army, who were camped in the Portico of Vipsanius. Amullius Serenus and Domitius Sabinus, former chief centurions, were ordered to bring the German soldiers from the Hall of Liberty. No one trusted the legion from the fleet, which was hostile because of the killing of its comrades, whom Galba had cut down the moment he entered the city. The tribunes Cetrius Severus, Subrius Dexter and Pompeius Longinus also went to the camp of the Praetorian Guard, hoping that the mutiny, which was only starting and not yet grown, could be turned by better advice. The soldiers attacked the tribunes Subrius and Cetrius with threats. They held Longinus by force and disarmed him, because he was not an officer of the ordinary service but one of Galba's friends, loyal to his emperor and so more suspect to the rebels. The legion from the fleet joined the Praetorians without any delay. The picked Illyrian troops drove Celsus away with hostile javelins. The German detachments wavered for a long time, since their bodies were still weak and their feelings were friendly. Nero had sent them ahead to Alexandria, and they had come back sick from the long voyage, and Galba had restored them with extra care.
## Galba's advisers argue
1:32 The whole crowd, with slaves mixed in, was already filling the Palace and shouting in discord for Otho's death and the destruction of the conspirators, as if they were asking for a show in the circus or the theater. They had no judgment and no honesty, since on that same day they would demand the opposite with equal energy. It was the traditional way of flattering any emperor with wild applause and empty enthusiasm. Meanwhile two opinions were holding Galba back. Titus Vinius thought he should stay inside the house, set the slaves against the enemy, strengthen the entrances, and not go out to angry men. He should give the guilty time to regret it and the loyal time to come together. Crime grows strong by rushing, good plans by waiting. In the end he could still go out later, if there was reason, but a retreat, if he regretted going, would be in someone else's power.
1:33 The others thought they should hurry before the still weak conspiracy of a few men could grow. Otho himself would panic, they said. He had slipped away secretly, been carried in before people who knew nothing, and was now learning to play the emperor through the delay and sluggishness of men who were wasting time. They shouldn't wait until he had set up the camp and burst into the Forum and walked up to the Capitol while Galba watched, and meanwhile our excellent emperor and his brave friends would shut the door of the house as far as the threshold, ready, of course, to sit out a siege. And what a splendid help the slaves would be, if the agreement of that huge crowd and its first burst of anger, which counts for so much, were allowed to fade. What is not honorable is not safe. Even if they had to fall, they should go meet the danger. That would bring more blame on Otho and be honorable for themselves. When Vinius argued against this view, Laco attacked him with threats, urged on by Icelus, who stubbornly kept up a private hatred to the public's ruin.
## A false rumor of Otho's death
1:34 Galba didn't hesitate any longer and went with the advisers who made the better show. Piso was sent ahead to the camp, as a young man with a great name, whose popularity was recent, and who was an enemy of Titus Vinius, either because he really was, or because angry men wanted it that way, and hatred is easily believed. Piso had hardly gone out when a rumor spread, vague and uncertain at first, that Otho had been killed in the camp. Soon, as happens with big lies, some claimed they had been there and seen it, and the report was believed easily by people who were glad or didn't care. Many thought the rumor had been made up and fed by the Othonianists, who by then were mixed in with the crowd and had spread the false good news to draw Galba out.
1:35 At that, not only the people and the ignorant crowd burst into applause and wild enthusiasm, but most of the Knights and many senators, careless now that their fear was gone, broke down the doors of the Palace, rushed in and showed themselves to Galba, complaining that their revenge had been snatched from them. The biggest cowards, the ones who, as events proved, would do nothing when real danger came, had the loudest voices and the fiercest tongues. Nobody knew anything and everybody swore to it. In the end, with no true news, and beaten down by the agreement of everyone who was mistaken, Galba put on his breastplate. He was too old and weak to stand against the crowd rushing in, so he was lifted onto a chair. In the Palace he was met by a bodyguard, Julius Atticus, who held up a bloody sword and shouted that he had killed Otho. "Fellow soldier," Galba said, "who ordered it?" His spirit was remarkable at keeping the soldiers' lawlessness in check. Threats did not frighten him, and flattery did not bend him.
## Otho in the camp
1:36 There was no longer any doubt about the mood of the whole camp. The soldiers were so fired up that, not content with surrounding Otho with their ranks and bodies, they put him on the platform where the gilded statue of Galba had stood a short time before, and ringed him with banners among the standards. Tribunes and centurions had no room to come near. The ordinary soldier even ordered them to keep a watch on their officers. Everything rang with shouting, uproar and the soldiers urging each other on. It was not like the shouting of an assembly of citizens or a mob, where the voices vary and the flattery is lazy. As soon as they saw any soldier coming in, they grabbed him by the hands, hugged him in his armor, stood him beside Otho, led him in the oath, and recommended the emperor to the soldiers and the soldiers to the emperor. Otho did his part. He held out his hands, bowed to the crowd, blew kisses, and acted the slave in every way to become the master. When the entire legion from the fleet had sworn the oath to him, he trusted his strength. He decided that the men he had so far worked on one by one should be set on fire all together, and he stood before the camp's rampart and began like this.
1:37 "Comrades, I can't say in what character I've come before you. I can't call myself a private citizen now that you've named me emperor, and I can't call myself emperor while someone else is ruling. Your own name will be uncertain too, as long as it's in doubt whether you have the emperor of the Roman people in your camp or an enemy. Do you hear how my punishment and your execution are demanded together? It's that plain: we can't die or be saved except together. And Galba, with his usual mercy, has perhaps already promised our deaths, just as he killed so many thousands of innocent soldiers when nobody had even asked him to. I shudder every time I remember that deadly entry, Galba's only victory, when before the eyes of the city he ordered the surrendered men, who had begged for mercy and been taken under his protection, to be decimated. That was the omen he came into the city with. What glory has he brought to the throne except the killing of Obultronius Sabinus and Cornelius Marcellus in Spain, Betuus Chilo in Gaul, Fonteius Capito in Germany, Clodius Macer in Africa, Cingonius on the road, Turpilianus in the city, and Nymphidius in the camp? Is there a province or a camp anywhere that isn't bloodied and stained, or, as he puts it himself, cleansed and set right? What others call crimes he calls cures, and with false names he calls cruelty strictness, greed thrift, and your punishments and insults discipline. It's only seven months since Nero's end, and already Icelus has grabbed more than the Polycleti, the Vatinii and the Aegiali ever wasted. Titus Vinius would have been less greedy and less lawless if he were emperor himself. As it is, he has treated us as his own subjects and held us cheap as if we were someone else's. That one house is enough to pay the bonus that you never get and are reminded of every day.
1:38 And so that there would be no hope even in Galba's successor, he calls back from exile the man he judged most like himself in gloom and greed. You saw, comrades, how even the gods showed with a notable storm that they turned away from that ill-fated adoption. The Senate and the Roman people feel the same way. They are waiting for your courage. In you lies all the strength of honorable plans, and without you even the best of them are powerless. I'm not calling you to war or to danger: the weapons of every soldier are on our side. Right now not even a single cohort in civilian dress is defending Galba. It's holding him in place. When it sees you and gets my signal, the only contest will be who can put me most in his debt. There is no room for delay in a plan that can only be praised once it's done." Then he ordered the armory opened. The weapons were snatched up at once, without any military custom or order, so that a Praetorian or a legionary could not be told apart by his own gear. They mixed in auxiliary helmets and shields, with no tribune or centurion urging them on, each man his own leader and instigator. And the worst men were spurred on all the more because the good ones were sad.
## Galba in the Forum
1:39 Meanwhile Piso was frightened by the roar of the growing mutiny and by the shouts echoing all the way to the city. He caught up with Galba, who had gone out and was nearing the Forum. Marius Celsus had already brought back bad news. Some advised returning to the Palace, some going to the Capitol, and most taking the Rostra. More just argued against what others said, and, as happens in doomed councils, the plans whose time had passed seemed the best. It is said that Laco, without Galba knowing, thought about killing Titus Vinius, either to calm the soldiers' feelings with his punishment, or because he believed Vinius was in on Otho's plot, or in the end just from hatred. The time and place made him hesitate, because once a killing begins it is hard to stop. His plan was also thrown into confusion by frightened messengers and by the desertion of the men nearest to him, as the eagerness of everyone who had first been quick to show loyalty and courage faded.
1:40 Galba was pushed this way and that by the shifting waves of the crowd, and the basilicas and temples all around were filled with a mournful sight. There was no voice from the people or the common crowd, only stunned faces and ears turned to every sound. It wasn't an uproar and it wasn't calm: it was the silence of great fear and great anger. Still, Otho was told that the crowd was being armed. He ordered his men to rush ahead and meet the danger. And so Roman soldiers charged as if they were going to throw Vologesus or Pacorus off the ancestral throne of the Arsacids, not to kill their own emperor, unarmed and old. They scattered the crowd, trampled the Senate underfoot, and broke into the Forum with their terrible weapons, at a gallop. Neither the sight of the Capitol, nor the sacredness of the temples towering above them, nor past or future emperors, kept them from committing a crime that whoever comes next will avenge.
## The killing of Galba
1:41 When the column of armed men came into view, the standard-bearer of the cohort escorting Galba (they say it was Atilius Vergilio) tore down Galba's image and threw it to the ground. At that signal it was plain that all the troops were for Otho. The Forum was emptied by the people fleeing, and weapons were drawn against those who hesitated. Near the Lake of Curtius, Galba's bearers panicked and he was thrown out of his chair and rolled on the ground. His last words have been told differently, depending on whether people hated him or admired him. Some say he asked in a pleading voice what wrong he had done and begged for a few days to pay the bonus. More say he offered his throat to the killers on his own, telling them to do it and strike, if that seemed best for the state. What he said made no difference to the men killing him. It isn't well established who struck the blow. Some say Terentius, a veteran called back to service, others Laecanius. The more common story is that Camurius, a soldier of the 15th legion, killed him by driving his sword into his throat. The other soldiers mangled his legs and arms, since his chest was covered, and added many more wounds out of cruelty to the already headless body.
## The deaths of Vinius and Piso
1:42 Next they attacked Titus Vinius. About him too it is disputed whether his rising fear took away his voice, or whether he cried out that Otho had not ordered him killed. If he made that up out of fear, or if he confessed that he knew of the plot, his life and reputation make me think he was in on the crime that he himself certainly caused. He fell in front of the temple of the divine Julius, first struck behind the knee, then run through from side to side by a legionary, Julius Carus.
1:43 Our age saw a remarkable man that day in Sempronius Densus. He was a centurion of a Praetorian cohort, and Galba had attached him to Piso as a guard. He ran at the armed men with his dagger drawn, shouting that what they were doing was a crime, and by turning the killers on himself, now with his hand and now with his voice, he gave Piso, though wounded, a way to escape. Piso made it to the temple of Vesta. A public slave took him in out of pity and hid him in his own room. It was not the holiness of the place or its rites but the hiding place that put off the coming death. Then, sent by Otho and named for the job because they were burning to kill him, Sulpicius Florus of the British cohorts, a man Galba had recently made a citizen, and Statius Murcus, a bodyguard, arrived. They dragged Piso out and killed him in the doorway of the temple.
## The heads, and the Senate comes to Otho
1:44 They say Otho welcomed no killing with greater joy and looked over no head with such insatiable eyes. Either his mind, relieved of every worry for the first time, was finally free for gladness, or the memory of Galba's majesty and of his friendship with Titus Vinius clouded even that hard heart with a sad picture, while he thought it right and lawful to be glad at the death of Piso as an enemy and a rival. The heads were stuck on spears and carried among the standards of the cohorts, next to the legion's eagle. Those who had killed, those who had been there, and those who boasted, truly or falsely, of a fine and memorable deed, competed to show off their bloody hands. Vitellius later found more than a hundred and twenty petitions from people asking a reward for some notable service that day. He ordered all of them hunted down and killed, not to honor Galba, but in the old custom of rulers, as protection for the present and punishment for later.
1:45 You would have thought it was a different Senate and a different people. Everyone rushed to the camp, tried to pass those nearest, fought with those running ahead, cursed Galba, praised the soldiers' judgment and kissed Otho's hand. The more false it all was, the more of it they did. Otho did not turn anyone away, and he held back the soldiers' greedy and threatening mood with his voice and face. They demanded that Marius Celsus, the consul-elect and Galba's loyal friend to the very end, be taken to execution, hostile to his energy and honesty as if they were bad skills. It was plain that they were looking for a start to killing and plunder and ruin for every good man. But Otho didn't yet have the authority to prevent a crime, though he could already order one. So he pretended to be angry, had Celsus put in chains, and swore he would pay a bigger penalty later. That saved him from instant death.
## Otho's first appointments
1:46 After that everything was done at the soldiers' pleasure. The Praetorians chose their own prefects: Plotius Firmus, once a common soldier, then commander of the watch, who had taken Otho's side while Galba was still alive. Licinius Proculus was added, suspected of backing Otho's plans through his close friendship with him. They made Flavius Sabinus prefect of the city, following Nero's choice, under whom he had held the same post, though many were looking to his brother Vespasian in this. They then demanded that the exemptions normally paid to the centurions be abolished. The ordinary soldier paid them like a yearly tax. A quarter of a company would be scattered on leave or wandering around the camp itself, as long as each paid the centurion his fee. No one cared how heavy the load was or how the money was earned. The soldiers bought their military idleness with robbery and theft or with slave jobs. Then the richest soldier was worn out with labor and cruel treatment until he bought his exemption. When he had run out of money and gone slack with laziness, he came back to the company poor instead of rich, and slow instead of strong. Then another and another, all corrupted by the same poverty and license, rushed into mutinies and quarrels and finally civil wars. But Otho, so that his gift to the crowd wouldn't turn the centurions against him, promised that his own treasury would pay the yearly exemptions. It was clearly a useful reform, and good emperors later made it permanent in the discipline of the army. Laco, the Praetorian prefect, who was supposedly being sent away to an island, was stabbed by a veteran whom Otho had sent ahead to do it. Marcianus Icelus, as a freedman, was executed in public.
## The Senate's decree and the burials
1:47 A day spent in crime ended with the worst of its evils: rejoicing. The city praetor called the Senate, and the other magistrates competed in flattery. The senators ran to the meeting, and they voted Otho the power of a tribune, the name Augustus and every honor of an emperor. Everyone worked to erase the insults and abuse that had been thrown at him freely, and that no one could tell had stayed in his mind. Whether he had dropped his grievances or only put them off was left uncertain by the shortness of his reign. With the Forum still bloody, Otho was carried through the carnage of the fallen to the Capitol, and from there to the Palace. He allowed the bodies to be given to burial and cremated. Piso's wife Verania and his brother Scribonianus laid him to rest, and Vinius's daughter Crispina did the same for Vinius, after they found and bought back the heads, which the killers had kept for sale.
## Piso, Vinius and Galba remembered
1:48 Piso was completing his thirty-first year, with a better name than fortune. His brothers, Magnus and Crassus, had been killed by Claudius and Nero. He himself spent many years in exile and was Caesar for four days. Galba's hasty adoption gave him only this advantage over his older brother: that he died first. Titus Vinius lived fifty-seven years with a changeable character. His father was from a family of praetors, and his mother's father was one of the proscribed. His first military service was disgraceful. He served under the legate Calvisius Sabinus, whose wife, driven by a bad desire to see the layout of the camp, went in at night dressed as a soldier. She tried out the same recklessness on the night watches and the other camp duties, and then dared to commit adultery in the headquarters itself. Titus Vinius was charged as the man in the crime. On Caligula's order he was loaded with chains and soon released when times changed. His career went on without trouble, and after his praetorship he was put in command of a legion and approved. Then he was splashed with a slave's disgrace: he was accused of stealing a gold cup at a dinner of Claudius's, and the next day Claudius ordered that Vinius alone of all the guests be served on clay dishes. Yet as proconsul he governed Gallia Narbonensis strictly and honestly. Dragged to the edge of a cliff by his friendship with Galba, he was bold, shrewd and quick, and, depending on where he turned his mind, wicked or hard-working, with the same force. Vinius's will was set aside because of his great wealth. Piso's last wishes were upheld by his poverty.
1:49 Galba's body lay neglected for a long time and was abused in many ways under the license of the dark. His steward Argius, once one of his senior slaves, covered it with a humble burial in Galba's own gardens. The head, which camp followers and servants had stuck on a pole and mangled, was found only the next day in front of the tomb of Patrobius, one of Nero's freedmen whom Galba had punished. It was put with his body, which had already been burned. That was the end of Servius Galba, who in seventy-three years passed through the reigns of five emperors with good fortune, and was luckier under other men's rule than under his own. His family had old nobility and great wealth. His own talent was middling, more free of vices than full of virtues. He did not ignore his reputation and he did not parade it. He did not crave other people's money, he was sparing with his own, and greedy with the public's. With friends and freed slaves, if he fell in with good ones, his patience could not be blamed; if they were bad, he was blind to a fault. But his famous birth and the fear of the times served as cover, so that what was laziness was called wisdom. In the prime of his life he was famed for his military record in Germany. As proconsul he governed Africa moderately, and in his later years he held Nearer Spain with the same justice. He seemed greater than a private man while he was one, and by everyone's agreement he would have been judged fit to rule if he had never ruled.
## Rome fears both men
1:50 The city was already alarmed by the horror of these new crimes and by the old record of Otho. Now fresh news about Vitellius terrified it even more. That news had been hushed before Galba was killed, so that it would seem only the army of Upper Germany had revolted. People saw that two men, the worst of all mortals in shamelessness, laziness and luxury, had been picked, as if by fate, to destroy the empire. Not only the Senate and the Knights, who had some share in the state and some concern for it, but the common people too openly grieved. They no longer talked about the recent examples of a savage peace but went back to the memory of the civil wars. They spoke of a city captured so many times by its own armies, of Italy laid waste and the provinces plundered, of Pharsalia, Philippi, Perusia and Mutina, familiar names of public disasters. The world was almost turned upside down when worthy men fought for power, yet the empire survived the victories of Julius Caesar and Augustus, and the republic would have survived under Pompey and Brutus. Now are we to go to the temples for Otho or for Vitellius? Prayers for either would be impious and vows for either hateful, when all you can learn from their war is that the winner will be the worse of the two. Some predicted Vespasian and the armies of the East. Vespasian was better than either of them, but they shuddered at another war and more disasters. And the reports about Vespasian were uncertain. He was the only emperor before himself who changed for the better.
## How Vitellius's revolt began
1:51 Now I will lay out the beginnings and causes of Vitellius's revolt. After Julius Vindex was destroyed with all his forces, the German army was bold with plunder and glory, as men are who have won a very rich war without labor or danger. It wanted campaigns and battles, and preferred prize money to pay. They had long put up with a service that was unprofitable and harsh because of the nature of the place and the climate and the strictness of discipline. That discipline is merciless in peace, but civil strife loosens it, since there are people ready to corrupt on both sides and treachery goes unpunished. They had men, weapons and horses to spare, for use and for show. But before the war they had known only their own companies and squadrons, because the armies were kept apart by the borders of the provinces. Then the legions were pulled together against Vindex, and having tested themselves and the Gauls, they looked for arms again and new quarrels. They no longer called the Gauls allies, as in the past, but enemies and the beaten. The part of the Gauls that lives along the Rhine had also taken the same side, and was then the sharpest in stirring them up against the Galbianists, the name they had given the party in contempt of Vindex. So they were angry first at the Sequani and Aedui, and then at other states in proportion to their wealth, and they feasted in their minds on storming cities, ravaging fields and plundering homes. On top of the greed and arrogance that are the main faults of the stronger side, they were stung by the insolence of the Gauls, who boasted, as an insult to the army, that Galba had cut a fourth of their tribute and given them public gifts. There was also a rumor, cleverly spread and recklessly believed, that the legions were being decimated and the boldest centurions dismissed. Terrible reports came from every side, and the news from the city was bad. The colony of Lugdunum was hostile, and its stubborn loyalty to Nero made it a breeding ground for rumors. But the army itself held the most material for invention and belief, through hatred, fear and, whenever they looked at their own strength, confidence.
1:52 Just before the first of December of the year before, Aulus Vitellius had entered Lower Germany and visited the winter camps of the legions with care. Many men had their ranks restored, disgraces were canceled, and marks against them were lightened. He did more of this to win favor and some from real judgment, and in this he honestly changed the stinginess and greed of Fonteius Capito in taking away and handing out ranks in the army. Everything he did was taken as bigger than the measure of a consular legate. To strict judges Vitellius seemed a man of low tastes, but those who favored him called it friendliness and goodness that he gave away his own property without limit or judgment and spent other people's. In their hunger for rule they also read his very vices as virtues. In both armies there were many who were modest and quiet, and many who were bad and energetic. But the legates of the legions Alienus Caecina and Fabius Valens stood out for their overflowing greed and remarkable recklessness. Of these, Valens was angry at Galba, who, in his view, had repaid with ingratitude his exposing of Verginius's hesitation and his crushing of Capito's plans. He pushed Vitellius, pointing to the soldiers' fire. He told him his name was famous everywhere, that Hordeonius Flaccus would be no obstacle, that Britain would be with him, and that the German auxiliaries would follow. The provinces were disloyal, the rule of an old man was only on loan and would soon pass to someone else. He only had to spread his arms and meet Fortune as she came. It was right for Verginius to hesitate, he said, being from a knightly family with a father nobody had heard of: he would have been unequal to power if he had taken it, and safe if he had refused. But Vitellius's father's three consulships, his censorship and his partnership with Caesar had long since put on him the standing of an emperor and taken from him the safety of a private man. This shook a lazy character, so that he wanted the throne more than he hoped for it.
1:53 In Upper Germany, though, Caecina had won over the soldiers. He was handsome in his youth, huge in body, immoderate in spirit, a clever talker, and he walked with his head high. Galba had put this young man in charge of a legion, after Caecina, as quaestor in Baetica, had eagerly come over to his side. Soon it was discovered that he had taken public money, and Galba ordered him prosecuted as an embezzler. Caecina took this badly and decided to stir everything up and cover his private wounds with the state's troubles. There was also no shortage of seeds of discord in the army itself, since all of it had fought in the war against Vindex, had gone over to Galba only after Nero was killed, and in that very oath had been beaten to it by the troops of Lower Germany. The Treveri and Lingones, and any other states that Galba had struck with harsh edicts or loss of land, were also mixed in closer with the legions' winter camps. That led to seditious talk, and the soldier was more corrupted among the country people. There was also a leaning toward Verginius that would help anyone else.
1:54 The people of the Lingones, following an old custom, had sent the legions gifts of right hands, the sign of hospitality. Their envoys, dressed up in rags and sorrow, went through the headquarters and the tents, complaining now of their own injuries, now of the rewards given to neighboring states. When the soldiers' ears were open to them, they complained of the army's own dangers and insults and stirred up their feelings. The legions were not far from mutiny when Hordeonius Flaccus ordered the envoys to leave, and so that their departure would be more hidden, to leave the camp at night. From this came a terrible rumor, with many claiming that they had been killed, and that unless the soldiers looked out for themselves, the keenest of them, who had complained about present conditions, would be killed in the dark while the others knew nothing. The legions bound themselves to each other with a silent pact, and the auxiliary soldiers were brought in. At first they were suspected of preparing, with cohorts and cavalry wings drawn around, an attack on the legions, but soon they were even keener on the same plans. Bad men find it easier to agree on war than to live in harmony in peace.
## The New Year mutiny in Germany
1:55 Still, the legions of Lower Germany swore the usual oath to Galba on January 1, but only after long hesitation, and only a few men in the front ranks said the words. The rest kept quiet, each waiting for his neighbor to be bold, in the natural way of mortals, who quickly follow what they hate to start. But the legions themselves differed in feeling. The 1st and 5th were so unruly that some of them threw stones at Galba's images. The 15th and 16th dared nothing beyond grumbling and threats, and watched for a chance to break out. In the Upper army, though, the 4th and 22nd legions, in the same winter camp, smashed Galba's images on the very first of January, the 4th with more readiness, the 22nd after hesitating, and then with the others' agreement. So as not to look as if they had thrown off all respect for the empire, they called on the names of the Senate and people of Rome, names that had long gone out of use, in their oath. None of the legates or tribunes struggled for Galba, and some, as happens in a riot, stirred up trouble more openly. Still, nobody spoke in the manner of an assembly or from a platform, since there was as yet no one to credit it to.
1:56 Hordeonius Flaccus, the consular legate, was there as a spectator of the outrage. He did not dare to hold back those rushing in, or to keep the doubtful in line, or to encourage the good. He was slow and frightened, and innocent only through laziness. Four centurions of the 22nd legion, Nonius Receptus, Donatius Valens, Romilius Marcellus and Calpurnius Repentinus, were trying to protect Galba's images when a rush of soldiers seized them and put them in chains. After that no one kept any loyalty or memory of the earlier oath. As happens in mutinies, whichever side had more men became everyone's side. On the night after the first of January, the eagle-bearer of the 4th legion came to Colonia Agrippinensis and told Vitellius, who was at dinner, that the 4th and 22nd legions had thrown down Galba's images and sworn by the Senate and people of Rome. That oath looked empty. They decided to seize the shaky fortune of the moment and offer an emperor. Vitellius sent messengers to the legions and legates to announce that the army of Upper Germany had revolted from Galba. So either they must fight the rebels, or, if they preferred harmony and peace, they must make an emperor, and it was less dangerous to take a ruler than to look for one.
## Vitellius is proclaimed
1:57 The nearest winter camp was that of the 1st legion, and the most eager of the legates was Fabius Valens. The next day he entered Colonia Agrippinensis with the cavalry of the legion and of the auxiliaries and hailed Vitellius as emperor. The legions of the same province followed with huge competition, and the Upper army dropped the fine names of the Senate and people of Rome and joined Vitellius on the third day before the nones of January. You could tell that in the two days before, it had not really been the army of the state. The people of Colonia Agrippinensis, the Treveri and the Lingones matched the armies' fire, offering auxiliaries, horses, weapons and money, each as he was strong in body, wealth or talent. And it was not only the leaders of the colonies and camps, who had plenty at hand and great hopes from a victory already won, but the companies and the common soldier too. They handed over their travel pay, their belts, and the silver-trimmed ornaments of their weapons in place of money, from impulse and passion and greed.
1:58 Vitellius praised the soldiers' eagerness. He assigned to Roman Knights the services of the emperor's household that freedmen normally carried out, and paid the centurions' exemption fees out of the treasury. Most of the time he approved the soldiers' savagery when they demanded punishment for many men, and rarely got around it by pretending to put them in chains. Pompeius Propinquus, the procurator of Belgica, was killed at once. Julius Burdo, commander of the German fleet, Vitellius cleverly pulled out of danger. The army's anger had flared against him on the charge that he had first made up the accusation against Fonteius Capito and then laid the trap. They were fond of Capito's memory, and with soldiers in this mood you could put a man to death in the open, but you could only pardon one by trickery. So Burdo was kept in custody and let go only after Vitellius's victory, when the soldiers' hatred had faded. In the meantime a centurion, Crispinus, was handed over as an offering. He had stained himself with Capito's blood, and so he was more obviously guilty to those demanding it and cheaper to the one who punished him.
## Vitellius gathers support, from Britain too
1:59 Next Julius Civilis, a man of great power among the Batavians, was pulled out of danger, so that the high-spirited nation would not be turned against them by his execution. There were eight Batavian cohorts in the territory of the Lingones, auxiliaries of the 14th legion, which had split off from the legion in the quarrels of the time. Whichever way they leaned, they would be a heavy weight as allies or enemies. Vitellius ordered the centurions Nonius, Donatius, Romilius and Calpurnius, of whom I spoke earlier, to be put to death. They were convicted of the crime of loyalty, the worst crime among rebels. Valerius Asiaticus, legate of the province of Belgica, whom Vitellius soon made his son-in-law, joined the cause, and so did Junius Blaesus, governor of Lugdunensian Gaul, with the Italian legion and the Taurian cavalry wing, which were stationed at Lugdunum. The Raetian troops didn't delay in joining at once, and not even in Britain was there any hesitation.
1:60 Trebellius Maximus was in charge there, a man despised and hated by the army for his greed and meanness. Roscius Caelius, the legate of the 20th legion, made the hatred worse. He had long been at odds with Trebellius, and the chance of civil war made them break out more savagely. Trebellius accused Caelius of mutiny and of wrecking army discipline, and Caelius accused Trebellius of robbing the legions and leaving them poor. In the meantime the army's good order was ruined by these shameful fights between the legates. It went so far that Trebellius was driven out by the shouts of the auxiliary soldiers too, and when the cohorts and cavalry wings sided with Caelius, he was left alone and fled to Vitellius. Even so, the province stayed calm, though the consular governor was gone. The legates of the legions ruled it, equal in law, though Caelius had more power because he dared more.
## Vitellius sends two armies toward Italy
1:61 With the army of Britain on his side, Vitellius was huge in strength and resources, and he planned two commanders and two routes for the war. Fabius Valens was ordered to win the Gauls over if he could, and if they refused, to ravage them, and then to break into Italy over the Cottian Alps. Caecina was ordered to take the nearer crossing and come down over the Pennine Alps. Valens was given the picked troops of the Lower army with the eagle of the 5th legion and cohorts and cavalry wings, about forty thousand armed men. Caecina led thirty thousand from Upper Germany, whose strength was the 21st legion. Both were also given German auxiliaries, and with them Vitellius, who was to follow with the full weight of the war, filled out his own forces.
1:62 The difference between the army and the emperor was remarkable. The soldiers pressed on and demanded arms, while the Gauls were trembling and Spain was holding back. Winter and the delays of a cowardly peace should not stand in the way. They had to invade Italy and seize the city. In civil strife nothing is safer than speed, since action is needed more than planning. Vitellius was sluggish. He was enjoying the fortunes of power ahead of time in idle luxury and lavish feasts, drunk at midday and heavy with food. Even so, the soldiers' own fire and energy filled the commander's duties for him, as well as if the emperor had been there to give the energetic hope and the lazy fear. Lined up and ready, they demanded the signal to march. The name Germanicus was added to Vitellius's at once, but he forbade being called Caesar even after his victory. On the very day the march began, a happy omen came to Fabius Valens and the army he was leading to war: an eagle flew ahead of the column in a gentle glide, just as the column moved, like a guide for the road. For a long stretch the soldiers shouted for joy, and the bird stayed so calm and unafraid that it was taken as a sure omen of a great and successful venture.
## Disaster at Divodurum
1:63 They went into the land of the Treveri without a worry, as among allies. At Divodurum, a town of the Mediomatrici, though they had been received with every courtesy, a sudden panic frightened them. They grabbed their weapons all at once to slaughter an innocent town, not for loot or out of greed for spoils, but in a rage and frenzy with causes unclear, and so all the harder to heal. Finally the pleas of their commander softened them and they held back from destroying the town, but about four thousand people were killed. Terror spread through Gaul so widely that soon whole states with their magistrates came out to meet the column, praying, with women and children laid out along the roads, and whatever else might calm an enemy's anger was held out, not in war but to buy peace.
1:64 Fabius Valens got the news of Galba's murder and Otho's rule in the territory of the Leuci. The soldiers weren't moved to joy or fear. They were turning war over in their minds. The Gauls lost their hesitation. Their hatred for Otho and Vitellius was equal, but with Vitellius there was fear too. The next state was that of the Lingones, who were loyal to the cause. The army was kindly received, and the two sides competed in good behavior. But the joy was short, because of the wildness of the cohorts that had split from the 14th legion, as I said above, and that Fabius Valens had added to his army. First came quarrels, then a brawl between the Batavians and the legionaries, and as the soldiers' sympathies gathered around one side or the other, it almost flared into a battle. Valens stopped it by punishing a few men and reminding the Batavians of the command they had by then forgotten. A cause for war against the Aedui was sought in vain. Ordered to bring money and weapons, they even supplied provisions for free. What the Aedui did from fear, the people of Lugdunum did with joy. But the Italian legion and the Taurian cavalry wing were taken away. It was decided to leave the 18th cohort at Lugdunum in its usual winter quarters. Manlius Valens, legate of the Italian legion, had served the cause well but got no honor from Vitellius. Fabius had slandered him in secret accusations he knew nothing about, and praised him openly so that he would be tricked more easily.
## Lugdunum, Vienna and Valens
1:65 The old quarrel between Lugdunum and Vienna had been set on fire by the recent war. They had inflicted many losses on each other, too often and too bitterly for them to have been fighting only over Nero and Galba. Galba, in his anger, had turned Lugdunum's revenues over to the treasury, while Vienna got great honor. So there was rivalry and envy, and a hatred joined the two cities, separated by a single river. The people of Lugdunum began to work on individual soldiers and push them to destroy Vienna. They reminded them that Vienna had besieged their colony, had supported Vindex's attempts, and had recently raised legions to protect Galba. When they had set out these causes of hatred, they showed how much plunder there was. It was no longer secret urging but public pleading. The soldiers should go as avengers and wipe out the seat of the Gallic war, since everything there was foreign and hostile. As for themselves, they were a Roman colony, a part of the army, and partners in good times and bad, so if fortune turned against them, the soldiers should not leave them to an angry enemy.
1:66 With these arguments and many more like them, they pushed the army until even the legates and leaders of the party thought the soldiers' anger could not be put out. But the people of Vienna knew very well their own danger. They carried out veils and wool bands of suppliants, and as the column came, they clung to the soldiers' weapons, knees and feet, and so bent their hearts. Valens added three hundred sesterces for each soldier. Then the age and rank of the colony counted for something, and the words of Fabius, who urged them to spare the safety and lives of the people of Vienna, were heard fairly. Still, they were fined their arms in public, and helped the soldiers with supplies from private and shared resources. But a steady report said that Valens himself had been bought for a large sum. Long poor, he had suddenly become rich and hid the change in his fortunes badly. Long want had inflamed his cravings, and he was extravagant, a man who had been poor in youth and was now a spender in old age. The army was led slowly through the lands of the Allobroges and the Vocontii, and the general sold the very length of the marches and the changes of camp, in ugly deals against landowners and city magistrates. He was so threatening that at Lucus, a town of the Vocontii, he held torches to it until he was calmed with money. Whenever there was no money to be had, he was won over with sexual favors and adultery. In this way they came to the Alps.
## Caecina and the Helvetii
1:67 Caecina took in more plunder and blood. The Helvetii, a Gallic people once famous for their fighting men and later for the fame of their name, provoked his restless temper. They knew nothing of Galba's murder, and they rejected Vitellius's rule. The war began with the greed and haste of the 21st legion, which seized money sent to pay a fortress garrison that the Helvetii had long held with their own soldiers and their own pay. The Helvetii took it badly. They intercepted letters that were being carried in the name of the German army to the Pannonian legions, and held the centurion and some soldiers in custody. Caecina, hungry for war, went out to avenge each offense as it came, before the offender could regret it. He moved camp quickly and ravaged the countryside. He plundered a place that had been built up like a town during the long peace and was busy with visitors who came for the pleasant use of its healing waters. Messengers were sent to the Raetian auxiliaries to attack the Helvetii from behind once they turned on the legion.
1:68 The Helvetii were bold before the danger and frightened in it. Though at the first outbreak they had picked Claudius Severus as their leader, they didn't know how to handle weapons, keep ranks, or plan together. A battle against veterans meant ruin, and a siege was risky with walls crumbling from age. On one side was Caecina with a strong army, on the other the cavalry wings and cohorts of Raetia and the Raetians' own young men, used to arms and trained in military ways. There was ravaging and slaughter on every side. The Helvetii wandered between the two, threw away their weapons, and a large part of them, wounded or scattered, fled to Mount Vocetius. A cohort of Thracians was sent in at once and drove them off, and with the Germans and Raetians chasing, they were cut down in the woods and in their hiding places. Many thousands were killed and many thousands were sold into slavery. After everything was torn down, as the army moved in hostile column on Aventicum, the capital of the people, envoys were sent to give up the city, and the surrender was accepted. Caecina punished Julius Alpinus, one of the chiefs, as the one who stirred up the war. He left the rest to Vitellius's mercy or cruelty.
1:69 It's hard to say whether the envoys of the Helvetii found the emperor or the soldiers less ready to be calmed. The soldiers demanded the destruction of the state, and thrust their weapons and fists in the envoys' faces. Even Vitellius did not hold back his threats, until Claudius Cossus, one of the envoys, a man known for his eloquence but who hid his skill in speaking behind a fitting show of fear, and so was all the more effective, softened the soldiers' mood. As the crowd always is, they were changeable on the spot, and as ready for pity as they had been wild with anger. They burst into tears, kept asking for better terms with more steadiness, and won pardon and safety for the state.
## Caecina crosses the Alps
1:70 Caecina stayed a few days among the Helvetii, waiting to learn Vitellius's decision and getting ready to cross the Alps. There he received good news from Italy: the cavalry wing of Silius, stationed around the Padus, had sworn allegiance to Vitellius. Those soldiers had had Vitellius as proconsul in Africa. Nero had then called them up to send ahead to Egypt, and recalled them because of Vindex's war. They were still in Italy when their decurions, who knew nothing of Otho and were tied to Vitellius, praised the strength of the legions on their way and the fame of the German army. So they went over to the Vitellianists, and as a gift for the new emperor, they added the strongest towns across the Padus: Mediolanum, Novaria, Eporedia and Vercellae. Caecina learned this from the soldiers themselves. Because the widest part of Italy could not be defended by the guard of a single cavalry wing, he sent ahead cohorts of Gauls, Lusitanians and Britons, detachments of Germans, and the Petrian cavalry wing. He himself hesitated a little over whether to turn through the Raetian ridges into Noricum against Petronius Urbicus the procurator, who had called up auxiliaries, broken the bridges over the rivers, and was thought to be loyal to Otho. But he was afraid of losing the cohorts and cavalry already sent ahead. He also reasoned that there was more glory in holding Italy, and that wherever the fight was settled, Noricum would go with the other prizes of victory. So he led the soldiers who fight under the standards, the heavy column of the legions, over the Pennine route while the Alps were still in winter.
## Otho keeps Marius Celsus
1:71 Meanwhile Otho, against everyone's expectation, was not going soft in pleasure and laziness. He put off his pleasures, hid his luxury, and arranged everything for the dignity of the empire. That made his false virtues and his coming-back vices all the more frightening. He had Marius Celsus, the consul-elect, whom he had kept from the soldiers' cruelty by pretending to chain him, summoned to the Capitol. He was after a name for mercy, from a distinguished man who was on the other side. Celsus admitted the charge of staying faithful to Galba and even claimed the example as a credit. Otho did not treat him as a man being pardoned. He called the gods as witnesses to their mutual reconciliation, took him straight into his circle of closest friends, and soon chose him as one of his generals for the war. And Celsus kept the same loyalty to Otho, as if by fate, a loyalty as faultless as it was unlucky. His rescue pleased the leading men of the city and was celebrated by the people, and it did not displease even the soldiers, who admired the very virtue they had been angry at.
## The end of Tigellinus
1:72 An equal burst of joy, from a different cause, followed when Tigellinus was finally destroyed. Tigellinus was born of obscure parents, had a shameful boyhood and a shameless old age. He got the command of the watch and of the Praetorian Guard, and other rewards that belong to virtue, by vice, because it was quicker. Then he practiced cruelty, then greed, the crimes of grown men. He corrupted Nero to every kind of crime, dared some things without Nero knowing, and in the end abandoned and betrayed him. So no one was demanded for punishment more stubbornly, from opposite feelings, by those who hated Nero and those who missed him. Under Galba he had been defended by the power of Titus Vinius, who claimed that Tigellinus had saved his daughter. He certainly had saved her, not out of mercy, since he had killed so many, but to keep a way out for the future. For every worst man, distrusting the present and afraid of change, prepares private favor against public hatred. So none of them care about innocence, only about taking turns at going unpunished. The people were all the more hostile, with the recent hatred of Titus Vinius added to the old hatred of Tigellinus. They ran together from all over the city into the Palace and the forums, and pouring into the circus and theaters, where the crowd has the most freedom, they roared with rebellious shouts. Finally at the springs of Sinuessa, Tigellinus received word that his last hour had come. Amid the embraces and kisses of his mistresses and other shameful delays, he cut his throat with a razor and stained his infamous life further with a late and dishonorable death.
## Calvia Crispinilla
1:73 Around the same time, people demanded the execution of Calvia Crispinilla. Otho used various tricks to save her, and drew a bad name for being two-faced about it. She had been Nero's teacher in lust, had crossed to Africa to push Clodius Macer into taking up arms, and had plotted openly to bring famine on the Roman people. Later she won the favor of the whole community, supported by a marriage to a man of consular rank, and went unharmed under Galba, Otho and Vitellius. Soon she was powerful through her money and her childlessness, which count just as much in good times and bad.
## Letters between the rivals
1:74 Meanwhile Otho sent Vitellius frequent letters tinged with womanish flattery, offering money, favor and any quiet place he cared to choose for a life of luxury. Vitellius made the same show of offers, at first more gently, with foolish and undignified pretending on both sides. Soon, as if quarreling, they threw debauchery and shameful acts in each other's faces, and neither was lying. Otho recalled the envoys Galba had sent and sent others in the name of the Senate to both German armies, to the Italian legion and to the troops at Lugdunum. The envoys stayed with Vitellius so willingly that no one could say they were being held. The Praetorians whom Otho had attached to the envoys under the pretense of an honor were sent back before they could mix with the legions. Fabius Valens also added letters, in the name of the German army, to the Praetorian and city cohorts, boasting about the strength of his side and offering peace. He even reproached them for turning to Otho the power that had been given to Vitellius so long before.
1:75 So the Praetorians and city cohorts were approached with promises and threats at once: they couldn't win a war, but they would lose nothing in peace. Still, the loyalty of the Praetorians did not change. But plotters were sent by Otho into Germany and by Vitellius into the city, and both failed. Vitellius's men got away unharmed, since in such a huge crowd, with no one knowing anyone, they passed unseen. Otho's men were given away by their unfamiliar faces, where everyone knew each other. Vitellius wrote to Titianus, Otho's brother, threatening death to him and his son unless Vitellius's mother and children were kept safe. And both families survived. Under Otho it is unclear whether that was from fear. Vitellius, the winner, got the glory of mercy.
## The provinces choose sides
1:76 The first thing that gave Otho confidence was news from Illyricum that the legions of Dalmatia, Pannonia and Moesia had sworn to him. The same came from Spain, and Cluvius Rufus was praised in an edict, but right away it was learned that Spain had turned to Vitellius. Not even Aquitania, though bound by Julius Cordus to Otho's oath, held for long. Nowhere was there loyalty or love: men swung this way and that from fear and necessity. That same fear turned the province of Narbonensis to Vitellius, an easy switch to the nearer and stronger side. The distant provinces and all the armies divided by the sea stayed with Otho, not from zeal for his cause. The name of the city and the pretense of the Senate counted for a lot, and what they heard first had taken hold of their minds. Vespasian had the army in Judea and Mucianus the legions in Syria swear Otho's oath, and Egypt and all the provinces facing the East were held in his name. Africa showed the same obedience, starting at Carthage without waiting for the authority of the proconsul Vipstanus Apronianus. Crescens, a freedman of Nero's (for in bad times even these men make themselves a part of the state), gave the common people a banquet to celebrate the new reign, and the people rushed to do most things without limit. The other cities followed Carthage.
## Otho's honors and favors
1:77 With the armies and provinces divided this way, Vitellius needed war to take hold of the fortune of power, while Otho went about the duties of the empire as in deep peace. Some of what he did suited the dignity of the state, but most was against what was fitting, hurried along by the need of the moment. He made himself consul with his brother Titianus until the first of March. He set aside the next months for Verginius, as a soothing gift for the German army. Pompeius Vopiscus was joined with Verginius under the pretext of an old friendship, though most people read it as an honor to the people of Vienna. The other consulships stayed as Nero or Galba had planned: Caelius and Flavius Sabinus until the first of July, and Arrius Antoninus and Marius Celsus until September. Not even Vitellius, after his victory, interfered with those honors. Otho added priesthoods and augurships as a crown of dignity to elderly men who had already held high office, and he comforted young nobles just back from exile by restoring to them the priesthoods of their grandfathers and fathers. Cadius Rufus, Pedius Blaesus and Saevinius Pomptinius had been convicted of extortion under Claudius and Nero, and they were restored to their place in the Senate. Those who wanted to pardon them decided to change the name of the charge and make what had really been greed look like treason, a charge so hated then that it made even good laws worthless.
1:78 With the same generosity Otho went after the goodwill of cities and provinces too. He gave the people of Hispalis and Emerita additional families, gave Roman citizenship to all the Lingones, and gave the province of Baetica the states of the Moors. He gave Cappadocia new rights and Africa new rights, more for show than to last. In the midst of this, which the pressure of the moment and the anxieties on him could excuse, he still did not forget his old love affairs, and by a decree of the Senate he put Poppaea's statues back. He was even believed to have thought about honoring Nero's memory, hoping to win over the crowd. And there were people who set up images of Nero. On some days even the people and the soldiers shouted to Otho "Nero Otho," as if adding rank and honor. He himself left it hanging, afraid to forbid it or ashamed to accept it.
## War with the Roxolani in Moesia
1:79 With minds turned to the civil war, foreign affairs went unwatched. That made the Roxolani, a Sarmatae people, bolder. They had wiped out two cohorts the winter before, and with high hopes they broke into Moesia, about nine thousand horsemen, more set on plunder than on fighting, because of their pride and their success. They were scattered and careless when the 3rd legion with auxiliaries attacked them suddenly. The Romans were ready for battle in every way. The Sarmatae were spread out, or weighed down by the baggage they'd grabbed in their greed for loot, and the slippery roads took away the speed of their horses, so they were cut down as if they were tied. It's amazing how all the courage of the Sarmatae is, as it were, outside themselves. Nothing is so cowardly in a fight on foot, but when they come charging in squadrons, hardly any line can stand against them. But on that damp day with the frost thawing, neither their lances nor their swords, which are so long they have to wield them with both hands, were of any use to them, with the horses slipping and the weight of their armor. That armor is worn by their chiefs and by every man of high rank, made of iron plates or very hard leather. It can't be pierced by blows, but when its wearer is knocked down by an enemy charge, it is hard to get up in. They also kept sinking in the deep soft snow. The Roman soldier, light in his breastplate, darted in with throwing spears or lances, and when needed, stabbed the Sarmatae warrior, who had no defense (they don't use shields), with his short sword at close range. A few who survived the battle hid in the marshes. There they died from the harshness of the winter and from their wounds. When this was learned at Rome, Marcus Aponius, who held Moesia, was given a triumphal statue, and Fulvius Aurelius, Julianus Titius and Numisius Lupus, legates of legions, were given the honors of consuls. Otho was delighted and drew the glory to himself, as if he too had been successful in war and had enlarged the state through his generals and his armies.
## A night riot in Rome
1:80 Meanwhile a small start, from which nothing was feared, set off a riot that nearly destroyed the city. Otho had ordered the 17th cohort brought into Rome from the colony of Ostia. The task of arming it was given to Varius Crispinus, a tribune of the Praetorians. To carry out the order with less disturbance while the camp was quiet, he opened the armory and ordered the cohort's wagons loaded at nightfall. The hour raised suspicion, the reason raised accusation, and his try for quiet turned into an uproar. The sight of weapons among drunk men woke a desire to use them. The soldiers grumbled and charged the tribunes and centurions with treachery, saying that the slaves of the senators' households were being armed to destroy Otho. Some did not know better and were heavy with wine, the worst men were looking for a chance to plunder, and the crowd, as always, wanted any new commotion, and the obedience of the better soldiers was lost in the dark. They cut down the tribune who stood against the riot and the strictest of the centurions. They grabbed weapons, bared their swords, and mounted on horses headed for the city and the Palace.
1:81 Otho was giving a crowded dinner for the leading women and men. They were frightened. Was it a chance fit of the soldiers or the emperor's trick? Would it be more dangerous to stay and be caught or to run and be scattered? At one moment they pretended to be steady, at another their fear gave them away, and all the while they watched Otho's face. As happens when minds are leaning toward suspicion, since Otho was afraid, he was feared. But he was no less alarmed by the danger to the Senate than by his own. He had immediately sent the Praetorian prefects to calm the soldiers' anger, and he ordered everyone to leave the dinner quickly. Then all over, magistrates threw off their badges of office and avoided their crowds of attendants and slaves. Old men and women hurried through the dark by different routes across the city. Few went home. Most went to friends' houses, or, each according to his humblest dependent, to uncertain hiding places.
1:82 Not even the doors of the Palace held back the soldiers from bursting into the dinner. They demanded that Otho be shown to them, and wounded the tribune Julius Martialis and Vitellius Saturninus, prefect of a legion, as they stood in the way of the rush. There were weapons and threats on every side, now against the centurions and tribunes, now against the whole Senate. Their minds were frenzied with blind terror, and since they couldn't pick any one person for their anger, they called for license against everyone. Finally Otho, against the dignity of his office, stood up on a couch and with prayers and tears barely held them back. They went back to the camp unwillingly and not innocent. The next day the houses were shut as if the city had been captured, there were few people on the streets, and the crowd was gloomy. The soldiers kept their eyes on the ground, with more sadness than regret. The prefects Licinius Proculus and Plotius Firmus spoke to the companies one by one, each gentler or rougher according to his own character. The speeches ended with 5,000 sesterces being paid to each soldier. Then Otho dared to enter the camp. The tribunes and centurions surrounded him, having thrown away the badges of their service, asking for rest and safety. The soldier felt the reproach, and, brought back to obedience, even demanded of his own accord the punishment of the ringleaders of the riot.
## Otho speaks to the soldiers
1:83 The country was in turmoil and the soldiers' feelings were divided. The best of them demanded a cure for the present lawlessness, while the crowd and most of the others delighted in riots and in a rule resting on popularity, and could more easily be pushed into civil war through disorder and plunder. Otho also reflected that power won by crime could not be kept by a sudden modesty and old-fashioned seriousness. But he was anxious about the danger to the city and the peril to the Senate, and in the end he spoke like this: "Comrades, I haven't come to fire up your love for me, or to urge you on to courage. You have plenty of both. I've come to ask you to hold your bravery in check and to keep some limit on your affection for me. The start of this latest uproar was not greed or hatred, which have driven many armies into discord, and it was not even refusing or fearing danger. Your devotion was too great and fired you up more fiercely than wisely. Often honorable causes are followed by ruinous results if you don't use judgment. We are marching to war. Do the nature of events and the speed of opportunities allow every message to be heard in the open and every plan to be discussed with everyone present? Soldiers need to be kept in the dark about some things just as much as they need to be told others. The authority of commanders and the strictness of discipline are such that in many matters it's best that even centurions and tribunes only receive orders. If each man may ask why he is being ordered, obedience dies, and command with it. Will you grab your weapons in the middle of the night out in the field, too? Are one or two worthless drunks (I can't believe there were more caught up in that last frenzy) going to stain their hands with the blood of a centurion or a tribune, and break into their emperor's tent?
1:84 You did that for me, I know. But in the rushing about and the darkness and the confusion of everything, a chance can open up even against me. If Vitellius and his attendants were given the chance to choose, what attitude and what thoughts would they pray we had? What would they wish for but mutiny and discord, so that the soldier wouldn't obey the centurion or the centurion the tribune, and we would all rush, foot and horse together, into our own destruction? Comrades, military affairs are held together by obeying, not by quizzing the orders of commanders. And the army that is bravest in the danger is the one that was calmest before it. You keep your weapons and your courage. Leave me the planning and the guiding of your bravery. A few were at fault, and two will be punished. Let the rest wipe out the memory of that terrible night. Let no army anywhere hear such shouts against the Senate again. To call for the destruction of the head of the empire and the pride of all the provinces, by heaven, not even those Germans whom Vitellius is stirring up against us right now would dare to do it. Would sons of Italy, the real youth of Rome, shout for the blood and slaughter of the order whose splendor and glory throw the squalor and obscurity of Vitellius's party into the shade? Vitellius has taken hold of a few peoples. He has a kind of picture of an army. The Senate is with us. And so it comes about that on one side stands the state and on the other the enemies of the state. What? Do you think this beautiful city stands on houses and roofs and heaps of stone? Those things are dumb and lifeless, and they can be torn down and rebuilt alike. The lasting life of the empire, the peace of nations, and my safety along with yours rest on the safety of the Senate. This order was founded under good auspices by the father and founder of our city, has gone on unbroken and undying from the kings to the emperors, and as we received it from our ancestors, so let us hand it on to those after us. For as senators are born from you, so emperors are born from senators."
## Fear in the city and the Senate
1:85 The speech, meant to rebuke and soothe the soldiers, and the mildness of the punishment (he had ordered no more than two to be punished), were gratefully received, and for the moment those who could not be forced were calmed. Still, quiet had not returned to the city. There was the clatter of weapons and the look of war. The soldiers, though they were causing no general disturbance, were scattered through private houses in disguise and kept a malicious watch on everyone whom high birth, wealth or some famous distinction had exposed to damaging talk. Many also believed that Vitellius's soldiers had come into the city to find out where people's loyalties lay. So everything was full of suspicion, and even the privacy of the home was hardly free of fear. But the greatest alarm was in public. With each piece of news that rumor brought, people changed their expression and mood, so as not to look doubtful about uncertain news or too little glad about good. When the Senate was called together into the Senate House, it was hard to keep a safe balance in everything. Silence looked defiant and frankness looked suspicious, and Otho, who had lately been a private man and had used the same language, knew what flattery was. So they turned the proposals over and twisted them this way and that. They called Vitellius an enemy and a traitor to his country. The cautious stuck to common insults, though some threw out true accusations, but only amid shouting where many voices rose together, or else they drowned themselves out in a jumble of words.
## Omens and the flood
1:86 Omens, reported by different sources, added to the terror. It was said that in the porch of the Capitol the reins had slipped from the two-horse chariot on which Victory stood. A form larger than a human had burst out of the shrine of Juno. The statue of the divine Julius on the island in the Tiber had turned from west to east on a clear and still day. An ox had spoken in Etruria. Animals had given birth to strange young. And many other things that, in rough ages, were noticed even in peace, and are now heard of only in times of fear. But the biggest fear, because it brought present loss together with worry about the future, came from a sudden flood of the Tiber. The river rose enormously, broke the wooden bridge, was thrown back by the pile of wreckage in its way, and filled not only the low and flat parts of the city but places thought safe from such events. Many were swept off the streets, and more were cut off in their shops and bedrooms. Hunger came to the common people, since there was no work to earn from and food was scarce. The foundations of the apartment blocks were rotted by the standing water and then collapsed when the river went back. As soon as people's minds were free of the danger, the very fact that the Campus Martius and the Flaminian Way, Otho's road to the war, were blocked as he was preparing his campaign, by accident or natural causes, was turned into a prodigy and a sign of coming disasters.
## Otho's war plan
1:87 After publicly purifying the city and weighing his plans for the war, Otho decided to attack Gallia Narbonensis, since the Pennine and Cottian Alps and the other approaches to Gaul were closed by Vitellius's armies. His fleet was strong and loyal to his cause. He had enrolled in the ranks of a legion the survivors of the slaughter at the Mulvian Bridge, whom Galba's cruelty had kept in custody, and he had given the others hope of honorable service in the future. To the fleet he added city cohorts and many of the Praetorians, men who were the strength of the army and both advisers and watchers for the commanders themselves. The overall command of the expedition was given to Antonius Novellus and Suedius Clemens, former chief centurions, and Aemilius Pacensis, to whom Otho had given back the tribunate that Galba had taken away. The freedman Moschus kept charge of the ships, unchanged, to watch the loyalty of men more honorable than himself. Suetonius Paullinus, Marius Celsus and Annius Gallus were named to lead the infantry and cavalry. But Otho had the most trust in Licinius Proculus, prefect of the Praetorian Guard. Proculus was energetic at city soldiering and unused to wars. By running down Paullinus's authority, Celsus's drive and Gallus's maturity, each according to what he had, which is the easiest thing to do, the crooked and cunning man got ahead of the good and modest.
## Rome prepares
1:88 In those days Cornelius Dolabella was sent away to the colony of Aquinum, in custody that was neither strict nor secret. It was for no crime, but because his old name and his kinship to Galba had pointed him out. Otho ordered many magistrates and a large part of the ex-consuls to go with him on the campaign, not as partners or aides in the war but as an escort. Among them was Lucius Vitellius, who got the same treatment as the others, neither as the emperor's brother nor as an enemy. The city was shaken with worry. No rank was free of fear or danger. The leading senators were weak with age and idle from a long peace. The nobility were sluggish and had forgotten war. The Knights knew nothing of military service. The harder they tried to hide and bury their fear, the more plainly afraid they looked. On the other hand, there were some who, out of silly showing off, bought splendid weapons and handsome horses, and some who bought dinner-party luxuries and temptations to vice as equipment for the war. The sensible thought about peace and the good of the state. The emptiest and those who could not see ahead were puffed up with false hope. Many whose credit had been shattered in peacetime were anxious then, and were lively in the disorder and safest in the uncertainty.
## The war reaches the people
1:89 The crowd, and the people at large, whom the sheer size of public affairs shut out of any share in them, began little by little to feel the evils of war, as all the money was turned to the soldiers' use and food prices went up. These had not worn the common people down as much in Vindex's uprising, when the city was safe and the war was in the provinces and, being between the legions and the Gauls, felt like a foreign war. Since the divine Augustus built up the power of the Caesars, the wars of the Roman people had been fought far away and had brought worry or glory to one man alone. Under Tiberius and Caligula only the troubles of peace affected the state. Scribonianus's attempt against Claudius was heard of and crushed in the same moment. Nero was driven out more by messages and rumors than by arms. Now the legions and fleets were in action, and with them, as rarely before, the Praetorian and city soldiers had been led into the line. The East and West and all the strength on both sides lay behind them, so with other commanders the war could have been long. Some brought Otho, as he set out, delays and a religious scruple, that the sacred shields had not yet been put back in their place. He rejected every delay as having been fatal to Nero too, and Caecina, who had already crossed the Alps, pressed him on.
## Otho leaves Rome
1:90 On March 14, after entrusting the state to the senators, Otho gave to those recalled from exile what was left of Nero's confiscations and had not yet been paid into the treasury. It was a very fair gift and splendid in appearance, but useless in practice because the collecting had been rushed long before. Soon he called an assembly and held up the greatness of the city and the agreement of the people and Senate on his side. He spoke of Vitellius's party with moderation, blaming the legions for ignorance rather than boldness, and did not mention Vitellius at all. Either that restraint was his own, or the writer of the speech, afraid for himself, avoided insults to Vitellius, since Otho was believed to use the talent of Galerius Trachalus in city business, just as he used Suetonius Paullinus and Marius Celsus in military planning. And there were people who recognized the very style of speaking, well known from his frequent pleading in the courts, broad and ringing to fill the ears of a crowd. The shouts and cries of the people, in the usual way of flattery, were excessive and false. They competed in zeal and good wishes as if they were seeing off the dictator Caesar or the emperor Augustus. They did it not from fear or love but from a sheer love of servitude. As in a private household, each had his own reasons, and the public honor already counted for nothing. Otho left, and put the peace of the city and the cares of the empire in the hands of his brother Salvius Titianus.
