1:1 Here's where my story starts. It's the year Servius Galba was consul for the second time, with Titus Vinius as his fellow consul. By then Rome was 820 years old, and writers had covered it in crowds. While they wrote about the Roman people, they wrote with equal skill and equal freedom. Then came the war at Actium. Peace meant one man held all the power. The great minds were gone. Truth took a beating too, from every side. First, people stopped knowing what went on in public affairs, as if the government belonged to somebody else. Then came the urge to flatter the men in power, or else to hate them. Nobody cared what future generations would think. One side was hostile and the other was bowing and scraping. We push a flatterer away, right? But spite and malice? People gobble that up. Flattery carries the ugly stain of acting like a servant. Malice passes for honesty. Galba, Otho and Vitellius never did me a favor or a wrong, so I owe them no praise and no blame. Vespasian started my career, and I won't pretend otherwise. Titus lifted it higher, and Domitian carried it further still. But promise total honesty, and you have to talk about every man without liking him or hating him. Cold. Fair. No favorites. If I live long enough, I've saved a richer, calmer subject for my old age. It's the reign of Nerva, called divine because he was dead, and the rule of Trajan. Rare, happy days, when you could think what you wanted and say what you thought.

1:2 Brace yourself. This story is packed with disasters. The battles are terrible, revolts tear it apart, and even the peace is savage. Four emperors died by the sword. Three civil wars. Then came more wars with foreign enemies, and often both kinds hit at once. Things went well in the East and badly in the West. Illyricum was in chaos. The Gauls wobbled, and Britain was completely conquered, then let go right away. The Sarmatae and the Suevi rose up against us. The Dacians made a name for themselves, but only through losses on both sides. The Parthians nearly went to war, fooled by a fake Nero. Yes, a fake. Think about that. Italy got crushed too. New disasters hit, and so did old ones that came back after centuries of quiet. Cities on Campania's richest coast were swallowed up or buried. Rome burned, and its oldest temples burned with it. The Capitol (Rome's holy hill) went up in flames, set on fire by citizens' own hands. Sacred rites were ruined, and adultery ran wild among the elite. The sea was packed with exiles. The cliffs were stained with murder. In the city, the cruelty was even worse. Being noble could get you charged with a crime. So could being rich, or holding a public office, or even turning one down. Being good was the surest way to get destroyed. The rewards for informers (men who turned others in) were hated just as much as their crimes. Some informers grabbed priesthoods and consulships like loot. Others got jobs as procurators (the emperor's money managers) and places of inside power. They turned everything upside down with hatred and terror. Slaves were bought off to turn on their masters. Freedmen (former slaves) turned on the patrons who had freed them. Men with no enemy at all were brought down by their own friends.

1:3 Still, the age had some goodness left in it, and you could find examples if you looked. Mothers went along with their children when they fled. Wives followed their husbands into exile. Some relatives were brave, and sons-in-law stood firm. Slaves stayed loyal, even under torture. Famous men faced their last hours with bravery, and their deaths matched the famous deaths of the old days. On top of all the twists of life, came signs in the sky and on the ground. Thunder gave warnings. Some forecasts were cheerful, some gloomy, some vague, some plain. Picture it. No disasters had ever hit the Roman people harder. No signs had ever been clearer. Together they proved it: the gods didn't care about keeping us safe. They cared about punishing us.

1:4 Before I get to my plan, I have to go back. Here's the state of the city, the mood of the armies, the condition of the provinces. What was strong across the whole world? What was sick? Knowing that gives you more than the twists of events, which are mostly luck. It gives you the reasons and causes behind them. Nero's death was welcome at first, and joy burst out. But it riled up every kind of feeling too. It hit the city hard: the senators, the common people, the city's soldiers. It hit every legion and every commander. The big secret was out: an emperor could be made somewhere besides Rome. The senators were glad. Of course they were. They grabbed their new freedom right away and used it boldly, since the emperor was new and away from Rome. The top Roman Knights (Rome's second rank after the senators) were just as happy. The respectable part of the people, the ones tied to the great houses, were full of hope. So were the freed slaves and followers of men who had been convicted and exiled. Then there was the dirty crowd that lived for the circus and the theater. The worst of the slaves were in there. So were men who had burned through their money and fed off Nero's wild living. That last crowd was gloomy and hungry for rumors.

1:5 What about the soldiers in Rome? They had been bound by their oath to the Caesars for a long time. Schemers and pressure had pushed them to drop Nero. Their own choice counted for less. Now they looked around and saw three problems. The bonus promised in Galba's name still hadn't been paid. In peacetime there were fewer chances than in war to earn big rewards for big services. And the favor of the emperor the legions had raised up was already taken. So they were ready for a change. Then their commander, a prefect named Nymphidius Sabinus, tried to grab the throne for himself. His crime riled them up even more, like fresh wood on a fire. Nymphidius was crushed right in the middle of his attempt. That ended fast. But with the leader of the revolt gone, plenty of soldiers still felt guilty. And the complaining about Galba's old age and greed never stopped. His strictness had once been famous in the army, and the army had praised it. Now it grated on men who turned up their noses at the old discipline. Fourteen years under Nero had taught the soldiers to love the emperors' bad habits. They had once respected the emperors' good qualities just as much. Then Galba came out with a saying. It was good for the state but dangerous for himself: "I pick my soldiers, I don't buy them." His other acts said otherwise.

1:6 Two men were wrecking the weak old Galba: Titus Vinius and Cornelius Laco. One was among the worst people alive. The other was the most spineless. Galba carried the hatred for their crimes and the contempt for their laziness. His trip to Rome was slow and bloody. He had two men put to death. Cingonius Varro was a consul-elect (a man picked to be consul but not in office yet). Petronius Turpilianus was a former consul. The reasons given: Varro had been Nymphidius's partner, and Turpilianus had been one of Nero's generals. Neither got a hearing or a chance to defend himself. They were killed like innocent men. Then Galba went into the city after thousands of unarmed soldiers had been cut down. A bad sign, and even the men who did the killing were scared of it. He brought in the Spanish legion too. The one Nero had raised from the fleet was still there, so the city was packed with troops nobody knew. Plenty of groups from Germany, Britain and Illyricum came as well. Nero had picked them and sent them ahead to the Caspian Gates for the war he was planning against the Albani. Then he called them back to crush Vindex's uprising. All of it added up to a stack of dry wood, ready for a revolution. It backed no single man, but it was ready for anyone bold enough.

1:7 Then two reports landed at once: Clodius Macer and Fonteius Capito were dead. Macer had clearly been stirring up trouble in Africa. The procurator (an official who ran money and property for the emperor) Trebonius Garutianus killed him on Galba's order. Capito tried the same in Germany. Two legates, the men in charge of legions, Cornelius Aquinus and Fabius Valens, killed him before any order came. Was Capito really plotting? Some people said no. Sure, he was foul, stained with greed and lust. But they said he had stayed far from any thought of revolution. The legates pushed him to go to war. When he wouldn't budge, they made up a charge and a trick against him. And Galba signed off on it, these people said. Maybe his mind changed like the wind. Or maybe he didn't want to dig deeper. Either way, what was done could not be undone. People took both killings badly. Once a ruler is hated, whatever he does, good or bad, earns the same anger. Everything was for sale. Freed slaves held too much power, and slaves grabbed for fast cash, hurrying to get rich under an old man. Gross, right? The new court had the same evils as the old one, just as heavy and harder to excuse. And Galba's age? People used to Nero's youth laughed and turned up their noses. They compared their emperors by looks and good figure, the way crowds do, like judging a beauty contest.

1:8 That was Rome's mood, as far as a crowd has a mood. On to the provinces. Cluvius Rufus ran Spain. He was a great speaker and good at the arts of peace, but he had never fought a war. The Gauls were loyal to Galba, on top of remembering Vindex. They had recently been given Roman citizenship, and lighter taxes had been promised for the future. But the Gallic states closest to the German armies hadn't gotten that honor. Some even lost land. They set what others got beside their own wrongs, and they were just as bitter. The German armies were worried and angry. That is the most dangerous mood for so much strength. They were proud of their recent victory. They were also afraid, since it looked as if they had backed the other side. They'd been slow to drop Nero. Verginius hadn't declared for Galba right away. Did he refuse to rule? Nobody knows for sure. Everyone agreed the soldiers had offered him the empire, though. Even people who couldn't call Capito's killing unjust were angry about it. The army had no leader, because Verginius had been called away under a fake show of friendship with the emperor. He wasn't sent back, and he was even put on trial. The soldiers took that as a charge against themselves.

1:9 Upper Germany's army looked down on its legate, Hordeonius Flaccus. Why? He was old, weak, and lame, with no steadiness and no authority. Even calm soldiers slipped out of his control. Angry ones? His weak grip only made them hotter. Lower Germany's legions had gone a long time without a former consul in charge. Then Galba sent Aulus Vitellius, and he took over. He was the son of Vitellius, a censor and three-time consul. That seemed like enough. Britain's army wasn't angry at all. No other legions behaved better through the shocks of the civil wars. Maybe they were far off and cut off by the Ocean. Or maybe frequent campaigns had taught them to hate the enemy instead. Illyricum was quiet too, although the legions Nero had raised there had sent messages to Verginius while they waited around in Italy. Still, long distances kept the armies apart. That is the best way to keep soldiers loyal. So they never mixed their bad habits or their strength. Distance did the job.

1:10 The East hadn't made a move yet. Licinius Mucianus ran Syria and its four legions. He was famous for good luck and bad luck alike. Talk about a mixed bag. As a young man, he worked hard to make friends with important people. Then his money ran out, and his position got shaky. He suspected Claudius was angry with him. So he pulled back to a remote part of Asia. He was about as close to exile then as he would later be to the throne. The man was a mix of everything. Luxury. Hard work. Friendliness. Arrogance. Good skills and bad ones. With free time, he went for pleasure. When it was useful, he showed huge ability. In public you could praise him, but in private he had a bad name. Still, with the men under him, his close friends and his fellow officers, he had power through every kind of charm. Handing over power would have been easier for him than holding on to it. Flavius Vespasian was running the war in Judea with three legions, and Nero had picked him as commander. Vespasian had no wish or plan against Galba, and he had even sent his son Titus to pay his respects and show loyalty. I'll tell that in its place. And the hidden plans of fate? The signs and prophecies that marked the throne for Vespasian and his children? We believed in those only after he succeeded. Easy to believe by then.

1:11 On to Egypt. Since the divine Augustus, Roman Knights have governed it instead of kings. They also command the troops that keep it in line. Why? Egypt was hard to reach, it grew tons of grain, and its people were restless, stirred up by superstition and wild living. They knew nothing of laws or officials. So it was thought best to keep the province close to home. Its governor then was Tiberius Alexander, a native of the country. With Clodius Macer dead, Africa and its legions would take any emperor at all. They had already tried a lesser master. The two Mauretanias, Raetia, Noricum, Thrace and the other provinces run by procurators went where the nearest army pulled them. Friendship or hatred, it depended on who was close. They felt the pull of stronger neighbors. The unarmed provinces were wide open to any kind of slavery. Italy most of all. All of them were headed for the winner, like a prize at the end of a war. That was the state of the Roman world when Servius Galba, consul for the second time, and Titus Vinius, his partner, began a year. It was the last year for both of them. For the state, nearly the last.

1:12 A few days after the first of January, a letter arrived from Pompeius Propinquus, the procurator in Belgica. The legions of Upper Germany had thrown away the respect they owed their oath. They wanted a different emperor, and they left the choice to the Senate and the Roman people, so the revolt would go down easier. That hurried Galba's plan along. For a while he had been thinking about adopting someone, talking it over with himself and his closest men. The whole city talked about it through those months. First, people felt free to say such things. Later, Galba was old and worn out. Few people had judgment or love of the state. So who was thinking straight? Hardly anyone. Full of foolish hope, lots of people pushed this man or that man as the heir, depending on whose friend or follower they were. They spread rumors to win favor. Some did it to spite Titus Vinius too. He grew more powerful every day, and for the same reason, more hated. Galba's own easygoing nature only fed his friends' greed. They were gaping at so much good fortune. With a man that weak and that trusting, wrongdoing came with less fear. And a bigger reward.

1:13 So who really ran the empire? Two men. Titus Vinius, a consul, held one of Rome's top jobs. Cornelius Laco commanded the Praetorian Guard. Then there was Icelus, a freed slave of Galba's, who stood just as high in favor. He wore the gold rings of a Roman Knight now, and people called him by the knightly name Marcianus. These men did not get along. Not a happy team. In the smaller matters, each one pushed his own interests. Over a successor, they split into two camps. Vinius backed Marcus Otho. Laco and Icelus agreed on one thing: not Otho, just someone else. Galba knew Otho and Titus Vinius were friends. Gossip spread, from people who let nothing pass in silence. The two would soon be son-in-law and father-in-law, they said. Vinius had a widowed daughter, and Otho was single. I believe Galba worried about the state too. Picture it: all that rescue from Nero, wasted if the state landed in Otho's hands. Otho's childhood was careless and his youth reckless. He won Nero's favor by trying to outdo him in luxury. So Nero put Poppaea Sabina, his favorite mistress, in Otho's care, since Otho knew his secrets. That lasted until Nero could get rid of his wife Octavia. Before long, Nero suspected Otho of an affair with Poppaea too. So he shipped Otho off to the province of Lusitania as governor. Otho ruled it kindly, and he was the first to switch to Galba. He wasn't lazy, either, and while the war lasted, nobody on the scene impressed more. Right away, Otho had hoped to be adopted, and that hope sharpened every day. Plenty of soldiers backed him. Nero's court leaned his way too, toward a man just like Nero. Of course it did.

1:14 Then bad news: a mutiny in Germany. Not good. Galba was uneasy about where the armies' power would break out, though nothing was certain yet about Vitellius. He didn't even trust the soldiers in the city. Would you? Of course not. He saw only one remedy, so he held the vote for an emperor. Vinius and Laco were already there. He called in Marius Celsus, a consul-elect (a man already picked for a coming consulship). Ducenius Geminus, the prefect of the city, came too. After a few words about his age, Galba had Piso Licinianus called in. Was this his own choice, or, as some believed, did Laco push him? Nobody knows. Laco had become friends with Piso in the circle of Rubellius Plautus. But Laco was clever, and he pushed Piso as though they had never met. And Piso's good reputation gave the advice weight. He was the son of Marcus Crassus and Scribonia. His family was noble on both sides, and he looked and acted like a man from the old days. Fair judges called him strict and harsher ones called him gloomy. That gloom made nervous people uneasy. It made Galba like him more.

1:15 Here's what they say Galba said, holding Piso by the hand. "If I were a private man, I would adopt you before the pontiffs, by the law of the Curiae, the way it's done. That would be an honor for me. I'd bring a descendant of Gnaeus Pompeius and Marcus Crassus into my house. And you would add the Sulpician and Lutatian names to your noble line. But that's not my situation. That's not me at all. The gods and men agreed to call me to power. Your great character and my love for my country push me to offer you, a man at peace, the top power. Our ancestors fought over it. I won it in war. I'm following the example of the divine Augustus. He put his sister's son Marcellus right below himself. Then his son-in-law Agrippa, then his grandsons, and last of all came Tiberius Nero, his stepson. But Augustus looked for a successor in his own house, and I look in the state. It isn't that I lack relatives or comrades from the war. But I didn't get power by scheming either. Let my choice prove itself. I'm putting my relatives behind you, and yours too. You have an older brother, just as noble as you. He would deserve this spot if you weren't the better man. You're past the cravings of youth. Your past needs no excuses. So far, you've only faced bad luck. Good luck is a tougher test, with sharper stings. Misery can be endured. Success corrupts us. Loyalty, freedom, friendship: the best things a person has. You will hold them with the same steadiness as before. Others will wear them down by trying to please you. Flattery will break in. So will smooth talk. And the worst poison for real affection is everyone's own advantage. Say we speak with total honesty to each other today. Everyone else will still happily talk to our position instead of to us. Telling a ruler what to do takes hard work. Flattering any ruler? That takes no feeling at all.

1:16 If the huge empire could stand and keep its balance without a ruler, I would be the right man to start a republic. But we passed that point long ago. An old man like me can give the Roman people nothing better than a good successor. Your youth can give them nothing better than a good emperor. Under Tiberius, Caligula and Claudius, we were, so to speak, one family's inheritance. Being chosen will have to stand in for freedom. Now that the Julii and Claudii families are done, adoption will find the best man. Being born to emperors is luck. That's all. Adoption leaves the choice open. And if you want the best, general agreement points him out. Think of Nero, puffed up with pride from a long line of Caesars. Who took him off our necks? It wasn't Vindex and his unarmed province, and it wasn't me with my one legion. His own brutality did it, and his own wild living. Until now, no emperor had ever been judged guilty. War and the judgment of others brought us in, so people will be against us however good we are. But don't be scared if two legions still aren't calm in this shaking of the world. I didn't get power in a safe time either. And once people hear of your adoption, I'll stop looking old. Right now, that's the one thing they hold against me. The worst people will always miss Nero. You and I must make sure the good people don't miss him either. This isn't the time for more advice from me. My whole plan is done if I picked you well. Here's the quickest, most useful test of good and bad rule: think about what you would have wanted or not wanted under another emperor. This isn't like the peoples who live under kings. There one family rules as masters, and everyone else is a slave. You will rule people who can't stand being total slaves, and who can't handle total freedom either." Galba said all this and more, as if he were the one making an emperor. The others talked to Piso as if he already was one.

1:17 By all accounts, Piso gave nothing away. No worry showed. No joy either. Every eye hit him at once, and then stayed glued there. With Galba, his father and his emperor, he was respectful, and about himself he was modest. His face and manner never budged. He looked like a man better at ruling than hungry for it. Then they talked over where to announce the adoption: the Rostra, the Senate, or the camp. They picked the camp, since that would honor the soldiers. Buying their favor with bribes and scheming was shameful, but winning it honestly was worth having. Outside, the public waited for news. People crowded around the Palace, desperate for the big secret. Nobody guarded the rumor well, and the people who tried to hold it back? They only fed it. Hiding it made it bigger.

1:18 January 10 was an ugly day. Thunder, lightning and threats from the sky came down harder than usual. In the old days, that was reason enough to break up an assembly. Galba headed for the camp anyway. Maybe he looked down on signs like that as plain accidents. Or maybe he believed fate's plans can't be avoided, even when fate sends a warning. The soldiers packed in to hear him. He kept it short, the way emperors do. He was adopting Piso. The divine Augustus had done the same, he said, and this is how one soldier picks another. Then he added a point of his own, so that hiding the mutiny wouldn't make it look bigger. The 4th and 22nd legions had only talked and yelled. A few ringleaders had led them, and they would be back in line soon. No flattery. No money. The tribunes, the centurions and the soldiers closest to him gave answers that sounded good. Everyone else sat gloomy and silent, like men the war had cost a bonus they had come to expect, even in peacetime. Everyone agrees a small gift from the stingy old man could have won them over. It never came. His old-fashioned toughness hurt him. So did his extreme strictness. We just can't put up with that anymore.

1:19 Galba's speech in the Senate was no fancier and no longer than the one to the soldiers. Piso's speech was smooth, and the senators liked him, and plenty truly backed him. Some who hadn't wanted him now gushed over him. The biggest group sat in the middle. They tossed out quick compliments, chased their own hopes and didn't care one bit about the public good. That was all Piso did or said in public during the four days between his adoption and his killing. Meanwhile, reports of the revolt in Germany came in more often each day. The city believed every new piece of bad news. So the senators voted to send envoys to the German army. Should Piso go too? People whispered about it. The trip would look grander, with the Senate's authority on one side and Caesar's dignity on the other. Some wanted Cornelius Laco, prefect of the Praetorian Guard, to go along as well. Laco blocked that. The Senate had left the choice of envoys to Galba. He named them, excused some, replaced others. No backbone at all. He gave in to anyone who pushed, whether they wanted to stay or go, driven by fear or hope. Shameful.

1:20 Next came the money. Galba looked into everything and decided the fairest plan was to take it back from the people who had caused the shortage. Nero had given away two thousand two hundred million sesterces. Galba called in the recipients one at a time and let each keep a tenth. They barely had a tenth left. They had burned through other people's money the same way they had burned through their own. By the end, the greediest and most broke had no land and no loans. All they had left were the tools of their vices. Thirty Roman Knights got the job of collecting. It was brand new, and it was a headache, full of lobbying and headcounts. Auctions and buyers turned up everywhere. Lawsuits kept the city on edge. Still, people were thrilled. The men Nero had rewarded were now as broke as the men he had robbed. Around then, some tribunes lost their jobs: Antonius Taurus and Antonius Naso from the Praetorian Guard, Aemilius Pacensis from the city cohorts, and Julius Fronto from the watch. Did that calm everyone else? No. It only scared them. Galba seemed to be picking men off one at a time, with cunning and fear. Everyone was a suspect.

1:21 Meanwhile, Otho was in a tough spot. If things stayed calm, he had no hope at all, and every plan he had depended on chaos. Plenty pushed him at once. His love of luxury would weigh down even an emperor. His poverty was hardly bearable even for a private citizen. He hated Galba and envied Piso. He even pretended to be scared, just to whip up his own desire. He told himself he had been too heavy a burden for Nero. So he shouldn't expect another Lusitania, or another honor of exile. Whoever holds power always hates and suspects the man picked as heir. That had hurt him with the old emperor. With a young man, savage by nature and fierce from a long exile, it would hurt even more. Otho could easily be killed. So he had to act and dare now, while Galba's authority was shaky and Piso's had not yet taken hold. Times of change are perfect for big moves. Waiting was pointless, and sitting still was riskier than being reckless. Death comes to everyone the same way. What sets people apart is whether later generations forget them or remember them in glory. And if the innocent and the guilty meet the same end, a man with spirit at least ought to die for something.

1:22 Otho's spirit was not as soft as his body. His closest freedmen (former slaves) and his slaves were allowed to act looser than they ever would have in a private home. They kept bringing up Nero's court: the luxury, the adulteries, the marriages, all the pleasures of absolute power. If Otho dared, they said, it would all be his. If he sat still, they scolded, those pleasures would belong to other people. Then the astrologers piled on. They had watched the stars, they claimed, and new upheavals were coming. A glorious year, too, for Otho. That kind of person is untrustworthy to the powerful and tricks the hopeful. Our city bans them forever and keeps them around anyway. Many had been in Poppaea's private circle, the worst tools of the imperial marriage. One of them, Ptolemaeus, had been with Otho in Spain. He had promised that Otho would outlive Nero. Once events made him look right, he pushed the idea that Otho would be taken up to rule. His tools were guesswork and rumor, the kind that compared Galba's old age with Otho's youth. Otho treated the predictions like real skill and a warning from fate. People always rush to believe what is hidden. And Ptolemaeus did not hold back. He now pushed Otho toward the crime itself, the easiest step from a wish like that.

1:23 Did the idea of a crime hit him all at once? No one knows for sure. Otho had long worked hard to make friends with the soldiers. Maybe he hoped to take Galba's place. Maybe he was getting ready for some deed. On the road, in the marching column, at the guard posts, he called the oldest soldiers by name. He reminded them of Nero's tours with the royal party. He called them his old tentmates. Some he recognized, others he asked about, and some he helped with money or influence. Into his talk he kept slipping complaints and vague remarks about Galba, plus anything else that would rile up the crowd. The long marches hit harder, and so did the lack of supplies and the harsh orders. These men were used to sailing to the lakes of Campania and the cities of Achaia. Now they struggled under arms over the Pyrenees, the Alps and endless stretches of road. Quite a comedown.

1:24 Soldiers already on fire need only one more match. Maevius Pudens, one of Tigellinus's inner circle, supplied it. He went after the fickle, the broke, and anyone hungry for something new, and little by little he got bolder. Whenever Galba dined at Otho's, Pudens used the dinner as cover. He gave every man of the cohort on guard duty a hundred sesterces, Roman coins. Otho stacked private rewards for individual men on top of that public gift. He was a bold bribe-giver, and he proved it. A bodyguard, Cocceius Proculus, got into a boundary fight with his neighbor, so Otho bought the neighbor's whole field with his own money. Then he handed it to Proculus. Generous. Very. The prefect's carelessness helped all along. He missed what was in plain sight, and he missed what was hidden. Everything, in other words.

1:25 Next came the crime itself. Otho put his freedman Onomastus, a former slave, in charge of it. Onomastus brought in Barbius Proculus, the officer who gave out the watchword to the bodyguards, and Veturius, the deputy of the same unit. After many talks, he found out they were clever and bold, so he loaded them with money and promises. Then he gave them more cash to sound out other men. Picture it: two common soldiers took on the job of handing over the empire of the Roman people. And they did it. Only a few were let in on the secret. The rest were wobbling, so the plotters tried a different pitch on each group. The top soldiers heard that Nymphidius's favors made them look suspicious. The crowd and everyone else were worked on through their anger and despair, since the bonus had been put off again and again. Some burned with memories of Nero and a longing for the old wild freedom. Different pitches, one shared fear: being sent off to serve somewhere else.

1:26 The infection spread. Legions and auxiliaries caught it too, and their minds were already shaken, since everybody knew the German army's loyalty was wobbling. The disloyal were hungry for mutiny. Even the loyal kept quiet. On the day after the Ides, they would have grabbed Otho on his way home from dinner. Three things stopped them. The night was too uncertain. The camps were spread all over the city. And try getting drunk men to agree on anything. Don't mistake this for concern about the state. They were sober enough to plan to stain it with their emperor's blood. Their real fear was different. In the dark, some man shown to the soldiers of the Pannonian or German armies might get picked instead of Otho. Most of them didn't even know Otho. Plenty of signs of the coming revolt were smothered by people in on the secret. A few reached Galba's ears, and the prefect Laco brushed them off. He had no idea how soldiers think. He shot down any plan he hadn't thought up himself, however good, and he dug in against men who knew more. The warnings went nowhere.

1:27 Then came the day. It was the eighteenth day before the first of February, and Galba was sacrificing in front of the temple of Apollo. Umbricius, the reader of entrails, gave his verdict: the guts looked gloomy. Plots were closing in, and there was an enemy at home. Otho stood right next to Galba, so he heard every word. He took it the opposite way, as cheerful news and a good sign for his own plans. Cheerful. Sure it was. Soon his freedman Onomastus told him the architect and the contractors were waiting. That was the agreed signal: the soldiers were gathering and the plot was ready. Otho left. 1:28 And the camp? Julius Martialis was the tribune on duty there. Maybe the size and suddenness of the crime stunned him. Maybe he feared so many troops had gone bad that resisting meant death. Either way, he made many people suspect he was in on it. The other tribunes and centurions also chose what was safe now over what was uncertain and honorable. The mood was ugly: a few dared the worst crime, more wanted it, and everyone else let it happen.

1:29 Galba, meanwhile, knew nothing. He was busy with his sacrifice, wearing out the gods for an empire that already belonged to someone else. Not that it helped. So what now? They talked it over and chose to test the mood of the cohort on guard at the Palace. Galba himself would stay out of it, because his authority was being saved for bigger fixes. Instead Piso called the soldiers together in front of the house steps and spoke. "Comrades, today is the sixth day since I was made Caesar by adoption. I had no idea what was coming. Was this name something to wish for or to fear? I couldn't tell. What happens to our house and the state is in your hands now. I'm not afraid of the darker outcome for my own sake. I have known bad fortune. Right now I'm learning that good fortune is no less dangerous. My heart breaks for my father Galba, for the Senate, for the empire itself. We may have to die today. My adoption was thought to have made sure there would be no room for war, even after Galba.

1:30 "I won't boast about my noble birth or my self-control. Do I need to line up my good points against Otho's? No. His only bragging point is his bad habits. Those habits brought the empire down when he was just the emperor's friend. Will he earn the empire with his looks and his walk? Or with that womanish finery? Luxury dressed up as generosity fools people. Otho knows how to waste money. He doesn't know how to give it. The shame and disgrace land on everyone else. No one has ever used power won by shameful means for good. Not once. The whole human race agreed that Galba was Caesar. 1:31 The bodyguards drifted off. The rest of the cohort (a military unit) had shown the speaker no disrespect, yet they grabbed their standards anyway. It was a snap move, the kind a riot throws up, with no plan behind it. Later, people believed it was treachery and a trick. It was not. Marius Celsus was sent to the picked troops of the Illyrian army, camped in the Portico of Vipsanius. Amullius Serenus and Domitius Sabinus, both former chief centurions (the top centurion of a legion), had orders to fetch the German soldiers from the Hall of Liberty. And the legion from the fleet? Nobody trusted it. Galba had cut down its comrades the moment he entered the city, and it was hostile. Three tribunes, senior officers, headed for the camp of the Praetorian Guard: Cetrius Severus, Subrius Dexter and Pompeius Longinus. They hoped better advice could turn the mutiny around while it was young. Not a great hope. The soldiers threatened Subrius and Cetrius. Longinus they seized, pinned down and disarmed. Why him? He was no ordinary officer. He was a friend of Galba's, loyal to his emperor, and the rebels trusted him even less for it. The legion from the fleet did not wait. It joined the Praetorians at once. The picked Illyrian troops hurled javelins at Celsus and drove him off. The German detachments wobbled for a long time. Nero had sent them ahead to Alexandria, and they came back sick from the long voyage. Galba was nursing them back to health with extra care. Their bodies were still weak, and their feelings were friendly. So they wobbled.

1:32 The Palace was already filling with a crowd, slaves mixed in. They shouted, all out of step, for Otho to die and the plotters to be wiped out. Picture people begging for a show at the circus or the theater. They had no judgment and no honesty. That same day they would demand the opposite, just as loudly. It was the old way of flattering any emperor: wild cheering and empty excitement. Meanwhile, two opinions were tugging Galba in different directions. Titus Vinius said to stay inside. Set the slaves against the enemy, strengthen the entrances, and do not go out to angry men. Give the guilty time to regret it. Give the loyal time to rally. Crime grows strong by rushing. Good plans grow strong by waiting. Galba could still go out later, if he had a reason. But if he regretted going, the way back would not be his to choose. Retreat would be up to someone else.

1:33 The others said to hurry. The conspiracy was a few men and still weak, so stop it before it grew. Otho would panic, they said. He had slipped away in secret and been carried in before people who knew nothing. Now he was learning to play the emperor while Galba's side dragged their feet and wasted time. Why wait until he set up camp? Why wait until he burst into the Forum and walked up to the Capitol while Galba watched? Meanwhile, "our excellent emperor and his brave friends" would shut the door of the house, right up to the threshold. Ready for a siege, of course. And the slaves would be a splendid help, if the huge crowd's agreement and its first burst of anger were allowed to fade. That first burst counts for a lot. What is not honorable is not safe. Even if they had to fall, they should go and meet the danger. It would put more blame on Otho and be honorable for themselves. Vinius argued against all this, and Laco attacked him with threats. Icelus urged Laco on, nursing a private hatred. The whole public would pay for it.

1:34 Galba stopped hesitating. He went with the advisers who made the better show. Piso was sent ahead to the camp: a young man with a great name and a fresh popularity, and an enemy of Titus Vinius. Was that true, or did angry men just want it that way? Hatred is easy to believe. Piso had barely left when a rumor began to spread. At first it was vague. Otho had been killed in the camp. Soon, as with all big lies, some people swore they had been there and seen it. Those who were glad, or didn't care, swallowed it easily. Many thought the rumor had been made up and fed by the Othonianists, Otho's supporters, who were by then mixed into the crowd. They had spread false good news. The goal was to lure Galba out.

1:35 Cheers exploded. The people and the ignorant crowd went wild, and they were not alone. Most of the Knights (Rome's second rank after the senators) joined them, along with many senators. Fear gone, they stopped being careful. They smashed down the Palace doors, rushed in and made sure Galba saw them. Their complaint? Someone had snatched their revenge away. The worst cowards among them were the loudest. Events proved it: when real danger came, the men with the loudest voices and fiercest tongues would do nothing. Nobody knew anything. Everybody swore to it. There was no true news, only a whole crowd agreeing on the same mistake, and it wore Galba down. He put on his breastplate. Too old and weak to stand against the crowd pouring in, he was lifted onto a chair. Inside the Palace, a bodyguard named Julius Atticus met him. Atticus held up a bloody sword and shouted that he had killed Otho. "Fellow soldier," Galba said, "who ordered it?" Galba had a remarkable spirit for keeping the soldiers' lawlessness in check. Threats did not frighten him. Flattery did not bend him.

1:36 No more doubt about the camp. The soldiers had gone wild. Surrounding Otho with their ranks and bodies wasn't enough for them. They hauled him up onto the platform where a golden statue of Galba had stood a little while before. Then they ringed him with banners, right in the middle of the standards. The officers, the tribunes and centurions, couldn't get near him. Ordinary soldiers even ordered a watch kept on their own officers. Picture the noise. Shouting, uproar, soldiers egging each other on. It sounded nothing like a citizens' meeting or a mob, where the voices differ and the flattery is lazy. Any soldier who walked in got grabbed by the hands and hugged in his armor. They stood him beside Otho and led him through the oath. Then they praised the emperor to the soldiers and the soldiers to the emperor. Otho played his part. He held out his hands, bowed to the crowd, blew kisses, and acted like a slave in every way, all to become the master. Then the entire legion from the fleet swore the oath to him. He trusted his strength now. The men he had worked on one by one could be set on fire all at once. So he stood before the camp's rampart and began.

1:37 "Comrades, I don't know what to call myself. I'm no private citizen, not now that you've named me emperor. But I'm not emperor either, while someone else still rules. Your own name is in doubt too. Does your camp hold the emperor of the Roman people, or an enemy? Do you hear them? They demand my punishment and your execution in the same breath. It's that plain. We die together or we're saved together. Galba, with his usual mercy, has probably already promised our deaths. Sure he has. He's the man who killed so many thousands of innocent soldiers when nobody had even asked him to. I shudder every time I remember his deadly entry into the city. It was Galba's only victory. Before the eyes of the whole city, he ordered the surrendered men decimated. They had begged for mercy, and he had taken them under his protection. Decimated means every tenth man was put to death. That was the sign he came in on. So what glory has he brought to the throne? Killings. Obultronius Sabinus and Cornelius Marcellus in Spain. Betuus Chilo in Gaul. Fonteius Capito in Germany. Clodius Macer in Africa. Cingonius on the road. Turpilianus in the city. Nymphidius in the camp. Is there a province or a camp anywhere that isn't bloodied and stained? Or, as he puts it, cleansed and set right? What others call crimes, he calls cures. He gives cruelty a false name, strictness, and greed another, thrift. Your punishments and insults? Discipline. It's only seven months since Nero's end. Already Icelus has grabbed more than the Polycleti, the Vatinii and the Aegiali ever wasted. Titus Vinius would be less greedy and less lawless if he were emperor himself. As it is, he treats us like his subjects and holds us cheap, as if we belonged to someone else. That one house could pay the bonus you never get. Every day you're reminded of it.

1:38 Galba even called back from exile the man he judged most like himself in gloom and greed. That left no hope even for his successor. You saw it yourselves, comrades. A huge storm hit, and the gods showed they had turned away from that doomed adoption. The Senate and the Roman people think the same. They're waiting on your courage. Every honorable plan rests on you, and without you even the best ones go nowhere. I'm not calling you to war or to danger. Every soldier's weapon is on our side already. So who is defending Galba right now? Not even one cohort in civilian dress is defending Galba right now. It just holds him in place. Soldiers snatched up weapons at once, with no military custom and no order. You couldn't tell a Praetorian from a legionary by his gear. They mixed in auxiliary helmets and shields, and no tribune or centurion urged them on. Each man was his own leader and his own instigator. The worst men were spurred on all the more, because the good ones were sad.

1:39 Piso was scared stiff, and no wonder. The roar of the growing mutiny was bad enough, but the shouts echoing all the way to the city were worse. He caught up with Galba, who had gone out and was nearing the Forum. Marius Celsus had already brought back bad news. So what now? Everyone had an answer. Some said return to the Palace, some said the Capitol, and most said the Rostra. More people just argued against whatever the others said. In doomed councils it always goes this way: the plans whose time had passed looked best. Too late, as usual. Meanwhile, the story goes, Laco was weighing a killing of his own: Titus Vinius, and Galba knew nothing about it. Maybe punishing Vinius would cool the soldiers' anger. Maybe he believed Vinius was in on Otho's plot, or maybe, in the end, it was just hatred. The time and place made Laco hesitate. Once a killing begins, it's hard to stop. Then frightened messengers wrecked his plan, and the men closest to him deserted. Everyone who had been quick to show loyalty and courage at first? Their eagerness faded.

1:40 The crowd shifted like waves, and Galba got pushed this way and that. All around, the basilicas and temples were packed, a mournful sight. The people said nothing, and neither did the common crowd. You saw stunned faces and ears cocked toward every sound. No uproar, no calm. Just the silence of great fear and great anger. Otho still heard that the crowd was being armed. So he sent his men ahead at a run to meet the danger. Roman soldiers charged in as if they meant to knock Vologesus or Pacorus off the ancestral throne of the Arsacids. They did not. They were hunting their own emperor, unarmed and old. They scattered the crowd and trampled the Senate underfoot. Then they burst into the Forum at a gallop, with their terrible weapons. Nothing held them back: not the sight of the Capitol, not the sacred temples towering above them, not past or future emperors. They did the crime anyway. Whoever comes next will avenge it.

1:41 The armed column came into view. The standard-bearer of the cohort guarding Galba (they say his name was Atilius Vergilio) yanked down Galba's image and hurled it to the ground. That settled it. Every troop was plainly on Otho's side. The Forum emptied as people ran, and swords came out against anyone who stood frozen. Near the Lake of Curtius, Galba's bearers lost their nerve, and he tumbled from his chair onto the ground. What did he say at the end? The stories change with the teller, depending on whether the teller hated Galba or admired him. One version has him pleading to know what wrong he had done and begging for a few more days to pay the bonus. More people say he held out his throat to his killers himself. Strike, he told them, if that was best for the state. None of it mattered to the men killing him. As for who landed the blow, nobody has settled it. Some name Terentius, a veteran called back to service. Others name Laecanius. The common story says Camurius, a soldier of the 15th legion, drove a sword into his throat and finished him. The other soldiers hacked at his legs and arms, since his chest was covered. Out of cruelty, they piled many more wounds onto a body already headless.

1:42 Next came Titus Vinius, and nobody is sure what he said as he died. Did fear choke off his voice? Or did he scream that Otho never ordered him killed? Maybe terror made that up. Or maybe he admitted he knew about the plot. Either way, I read his life and reputation and I think he was in on it. He certainly caused the crime. He fell in front of the temple of the divine Julius. First a blow behind the knee. Then Julius Carus, a soldier from a legion, ran him through from side to side. Ugly.

1:43 Our age got to see one remarkable man that day: Sempronius Densus. He was a centurion, a company commander in charge of about eighty men, in a Praetorian cohort. Galba had told him to guard Piso. So Densus ran straight at the armed killers, dagger out, yelling that this was a crime. He kept dragging their attacks onto himself, sometimes with his hands, sometimes with his voice. It bought Piso, who was wounded, a way out. Piso reached the temple of Vesta. A public slave took pity on him, let him in, and hid him in his own room. Picture it. The holy place didn't save him, and neither did its rites. The hiding place only bought him time. Then two men arrived: Sulpicius Florus of the British cohorts, and Statius Murcus, a bodyguard. Otho had sent them. Their names were picked for the job because they were burning to kill Piso. Florus was a man Galba had recently made a citizen. They dragged Piso out and killed him in the doorway of the temple.

1:44 People say Otho had never loved a killing so much. No head had ever been studied with such hungry eyes. Maybe, for the first time, his mind was free of every worry, and it could finally enjoy being glad. Or maybe the memory of Galba's greatness, and his friendship with Titus Vinius, cast a sad shadow over even that hard heart. Either way, Piso had been his enemy and rival, so Otho thought celebrating his death was right and lawful. The heads went up on spears. They were carried through the cohorts' standards, next to the legion's eagle. Everyone raced to show off their bloody hands. Some had killed. Some had only been there. Others bragged about a great and memorable deed, true or not. Later, Vitellius found more than a hundred and twenty requests from people who wanted a reward for some notable service that day. He ordered every one of them hunted down and killed. Was he honoring Galba? No. He was doing what rulers had always done. Protection now. Payback later.

1:45 You would have sworn it was a different Senate and a different people. Everybody stampeded to the camp. They shoved whoever stood nearest and fought whoever ran ahead. They cursed Galba, praised the soldiers' good judgment, and kissed Otho's hand. The falser it all was, the more they piled on. Otho turned no one away. With his voice and his face, he held back the soldiers' greedy, threatening mood. Then the soldiers demanded Marius Celsus, to drag him off and execute him. Celsus was the consul-elect, chosen for the office but not yet in it. He had been Galba's loyal friend right to the end. The soldiers hated his energy and honesty, as if those were bad skills. They plainly wanted to start killing and looting and to ruin every good man. Otho had no authority yet to stop a crime. He could already order one, though. So he faked anger. He had Celsus put in chains and promised a bigger penalty later. That saved Celsus from instant death. For now.

1:46 From then on, the soldiers got everything their way. The Praetorians chose their own commanders, the prefects. First came Plotius Firmus. He had once been a regular soldier, then was put in charge of the watch. He had taken Otho's side while Galba was still alive. Next, Licinius Proculus. People suspected he had backed Otho's plans, since he was a close friend of Otho's. For prefect of the city, they made Flavius Sabinus the choice, following Nero's choice. Sabinus had held that same job under Nero. Plenty of people were looking at his brother Vespasian through him. Then the soldiers demanded an end to the usual exemptions for centurions. A regular soldier paid for them like an annual tax. A quarter of each company could be off on leave or wandering around the camp itself, as long as every man paid his centurion. Nobody cared how heavy the load was. Nobody cared how the money was made. The soldiers paid for their days off by robbing and stealing, or by doing jobs fit for slaves. Then the richest soldier got worked to the bone and treated cruelly, until he bought his own exemption. Once he ran out of money and went soft from laziness, he came back to his company. Poor instead of rich. Slow instead of strong. One soldier after another, all ruined by the same poverty and lack of discipline, rushed into mutinies, fights, and finally civil wars. Otho did not want the centurions turning on him over his gifts to the common soldiers. So he promised that his own treasury would pay the yearly exemptions. It was a useful reform, no doubt about it. Good emperors later made it permanent as part of army discipline. Laco, the Praetorian prefect, was supposedly being sent off to an island. Instead, a veteran Otho had sent ahead for the job stabbed him to death. Marcianus Icelus was executed in public, as a freedman.

1:47 A day of crime ended with the worst evil of all: a celebration. The city praetor called the Senate. The other magistrates competed to flatter. The senators rushed to the meeting. They gave Otho the power of a tribune, the protections of the people's officer. They gave him the name Augustus too, and every honor of an emperor. Everyone worked to wipe out the insults that had been flung at him left and right. Did any of it stick in his mind? Nobody could tell. Had he let those grievances go, or only put them off? Nobody knows. His reign was too short to say. The Forum was still bloody. Otho was carried through the bodies of the fallen to the Capitol, Rome's holy hill, and from there to the Palace. He let the dead be buried and burned. Piso's wife Verania and his brother Scribonianus buried him. Vinius's daughter Crispina did the same for Vinius. But first they had to search for the heads and buy them back, because the killers had been keeping them to sell.

1:48 Piso was almost thirty-one, with a better name than luck. Claudius and Nero had killed his brothers, Magnus and Crassus. Piso himself spent years in exile. Then he was Caesar for four days, because Galba had adopted him in a hurry. It gave him one edge over his older brother: he died first. Some edge. Now for Titus Vinius. He lived fifty-seven years, and his character kept changing. His father came from a family of praetors, and his mother's father was one of the proscribed. His first military job was a disgrace. He served under the legate Calvisius Sabinus. The legate's wife badly wanted to see the layout of the camp, so one night she slipped in dressed as a soldier. She tried the same recklessness on the night watches and the other camp jobs. Then she dared to commit adultery right in the headquarters. Who was charged as the man in that crime? Vinius. Caligula ordered him locked in chains, but when the times changed, he was let go soon after. His career then rolled on without trouble. He got command of a legion after his praetorship, and he was approved. Then came a disgrace fit for a slave. He was accused of stealing a gold cup at one of Claudius's dinners. The next day Claudius ordered that Vinius, alone of all the guests, be served on clay dishes. Ouch. Still, as proconsul, he governed Gallia Narbonensis with strict, honest hands. His friendship with Galba dragged him to the edge of a cliff. He was bold, shrewd and quick. Point that mind at wickedness or at hard work, and he threw the same force into either. Vinius's will was thrown out because he was so rich. Piso's last wishes stood because he was poor.

1:49 For a long time, nobody cared about Galba's body. In the dark, people did all kinds of cruel things to it. Then his steward Argius, once one of his senior slaves, gave it a plain burial in Galba's own gardens. The head was another story. Camp followers and servants had jammed it on a pole. They mangled it. It turned up only the next day, in front of the tomb of Patrobius. Patrobius had been one of Nero's freedmen (freed slaves), and Galba had punished him. The head was put with the body, which had already been burned. That was the end of Servius Galba. He was seventy-three, and he had lived through the reigns of five emperors, and with good fortune. Under other men's rule he was luckier than under his own. Think about that. His family had old nobility and great wealth, but his own talent was middling. He was more free of vices than full of virtues. He cared about his reputation. He never paraded it. He did not crave other people's money, and he was sparing with his own and greedy with the public's. And his friends and freed slaves? It depended. If he fell in with good ones, nobody could blame his patience. If they were bad, he stayed blind to their faults, so blind it became his own fault. His famous birth and the fear of the times gave him cover. So laziness got called wisdom. Sure it was. In his prime, Germany made his name. His military record there was famous. As proconsul he ran Africa with a moderate hand, and later he held Nearer Spain with the same fairness. While he was a private citizen, he seemed bigger than one. Everybody agreed he would have been judged fit to rule, if only he had never ruled.

1:50 Rome was already on edge. These new crimes had shaken it, and Otho's old record scared it too. Bad news, stacked on bad news. Then fresh news about Vitellius scared it even more. That news had been kept quiet until Galba was killed. The plan was to make it look as if only the army of Upper Germany had revolted. People looked at the two men and saw the worst of all men, a pair who matched in three things. Shamelessness. Laziness. Luxury. It was as if fate had picked them to wreck the empire. The Senate and the Knights had some share in the state, and some concern for it, so they grieved. But ordinary people grieved out loud too. People stopped talking about the recent savage peace and went back to the civil wars. They talked about a city captured again and again by its own armies. They talked about Italy laid waste and the provinces plundered. They named Pharsalia, Philippi, Perusia and Mutina. Everybody knew those names as public disasters. The world nearly flipped upside down when worthy men fought for power. Still, the empire survived the wins of Julius Caesar and Augustus. The republic would have survived if Pompey or Brutus had won. But now? People asked: are we to go to the temples and pray for Otho, or for Vitellius? Either prayer would be wicked, they said. Either vow would be hateful. All you can learn from their war is this: the winner will be the worse of the two. Some predicted Vespasian and the armies of the East, and Vespasian was better than either of them. Still, those people shuddered at another war and more disasters. And the reports about Vespasian were uncertain. Of all the emperors before him, he alone changed for the better.

1:51 So how did Vitellius's revolt get started? Here's what set it off. Julius Vindex and his whole army were gone, and the German army was flying high on loot and glory. Picture it: these men had won a very rich war without hard work or real danger. They felt great about themselves, and they wanted real campaigns and real battles. Prize money beat regular pay, every time. For years they had put up with a service that was rough and didn't pay off. The place was miserable, the weather was worse, and the discipline was brutal. In peacetime, that discipline shows no mercy, but a civil war loosens it. Plenty of people on both sides were ready to bribe others, and nobody got punished for betrayal. Wonderful. The army had men, weapons and horses to spare, for fighting and for showing off. Before the war, though, each soldier knew only his own company and squadron, because the borders of the provinces had kept the armies apart. Then the legions were pulled together against Vindex and tested themselves against the Gauls. Afterward they went hunting for weapons again, and for new fights. The Gauls had been allies once. Not anymore: now they were enemies, and beaten ones. The Gauls along the Rhine had picked the army's side, and they shouted the loudest, stirring the army up against the Galbianists. That was the name they gave Galba's party, as a jab at Vindex. So the army got angry at the Sequani and Aedui first. Then it got angry at other states, depending on how rich they were. In their heads, the soldiers feasted on storming cities, wrecking fields and plundering homes. Add the greed and arrogance that come with being the stronger side. And the Gauls made it worse. They bragged, to insult the army, that Galba had cut a fourth of their tribute and given them public gifts. Then came a rumor, cleverly spread and recklessly believed. The legions were being decimated (every tenth man killed), and the boldest centurions were getting kicked out. Bad news poured in from every direction, and the city had its own. The colony of Lugdunum was hostile, and its stubborn loyalty to Nero made it a hotbed of rumors. But the army itself was the best place for stories to grow. What fed them? Hatred. Fear. And confidence, every time the soldiers looked at their own strength.

1:52 Now go back a little. Right before the first of December of the year before, Aulus Vitellius had gone into Lower Germany. He toured the winter camps of the legions and paid close attention. A lot of men got their ranks back, disgraces were canceled, and marks against names were lightened. Most of this was to win favor. Some came from real judgment. He did undo the stinginess and greed that Fonteius Capito had shown in taking ranks away and handing them out. Still, it all looked like more than a legate (an army commander) who had been consul should be doing. Strict judges thought Vitellius had low tastes. His fans called it kindness and goodness. He gave away his own property with no limits and no judgment, and he spent other people's the same way. Men hungry for power even read his bad habits as good ones. Both armies had some quiet, modest men. They also had plenty of bad men full of energy. But the legates of the legions led the pack in greed and recklessness: Alienus Caecina and Fabius Valens. Valens was angry at Galba. In his view, Galba had repaid him with ingratitude. Valens had exposed Verginius's hesitation and crushed Capito's plans. And that was his thanks. He pushed Vitellius hard. He pointed to how fired up the soldiers were and told Vitellius his name was famous everywhere. Hordeonius Flaccus would be no problem. Britain would be on his side, and the German auxiliaries would follow along. The provinces were disloyal. Galba's rule was only on loan, and it would soon go to someone else. All Vitellius had to do was open his arms and grab the luck coming his way. Verginius had been right to hold back, Valens said. Verginius came from a knightly family, and his father was a nobody. If he had taken power, he would have been in over his head, but if he had turned it down, he would have stayed safe. Vitellius was a different case. His father had three consulships, a censorship and a partnership with Caesar. Those things had long since given Vitellius the standing of an emperor, and they had also taken away the safety of an ordinary private man. It shook the lazy man awake. He wanted the throne now, far more than he dared hope to get it.

1:53 Caecina was another story. He had already won over the soldiers in Upper Germany. As a young man he had been good-looking. He was huge, wild in spirit, a clever talker, and he walked with his head held high. Galba had put this young man in charge of a legion. Caecina had jumped to Galba's side early, back when he was quaestor in Baetica. Then it came out that he had taken public money, and Galba had him put on trial for it. Did Caecina shrug that off? No. He took it hard and decided to rile everything up. The state's troubles would hide his own private wounds. And the army had plenty of seeds of trouble. Every one of those soldiers had fought against Vindex, and they had switched to Galba only after Nero was dead. And the troops of Lower Germany had beaten them to that oath. Now the Treveri and Lingones were mixed in with the legions' winter camps. So were any other states Galba had hit with harsh orders or taken land from. That meant talk of rebellion. The locals were corrupting the soldiers even more, and there was goodwill toward Verginius. That would help whoever else came along.

1:54 The Lingones had an old custom. They sent gifts of right hands to the legions as a sign of hospitality. This time their envoys turned up at headquarters and the tents dressed in rags, looking miserable. First they complained about their own wrongs, then they complained about the rewards neighboring states got. The soldiers were happy to listen. So the envoys went on about the army's own dangers and insults, and they stirred up the men's feelings. The legions were close to mutiny. Hordeonius Flaccus ordered the envoys out. He made them leave camp at night, so nobody would notice. Then a terrible rumor spread. Many people said the envoys had been killed. The rumor warned that unless the soldiers looked out for themselves, the keenest of them would die in the dark. And the keenest were the men who had complained about present conditions. The others would not even know what happened. Chilling, right? The legions made a secret pact with each other, and the auxiliaries were brought in too. At first the auxiliaries were suspected of getting ready to attack the legions. They had cohorts and cavalry wings drawn up all around. But soon they were even keener on the same plans. Bad men agree on war far more easily than they get along in peace.

1:55 January 1 came, and the legions of Lower Germany swore to Galba. It was the usual oath, and it took them forever. Only a few men in the front ranks said the words. The rest stayed silent, each waiting for his neighbor to be brave first. People are like that. We rush to follow what we hate to start. Sound familiar? Not every legion felt the same, though. The 1st and 5th were so wild that some of their men threw stones at Galba's statues. The 15th and 16th dared nothing beyond grumbling and threats. They just watched for a chance to break loose. In the Upper army, the 4th and 22nd legions shared a winter camp. On the very first day of January, they smashed Galba's statues. The 4th did it eagerly, while the 22nd hesitated, then went along. They wanted to avoid looking like they had thrown away all respect for the empire. So their oath named the Senate and the people of Rome, names nobody had used in ages. None of the legates (top commanders) or tribunes (officers under them) fought for Galba. Some stirred up trouble in the open, the way people do in a riot. Still, nobody gave a speech, not like at an assembly and not from a platform. There was no one yet to pin it on.

1:56 Hordeonius Flaccus, a former consul serving as a legate, watched the whole disgrace. He did not dare stop the men rushing in. He did not keep the wobbly ones in line or cheer on the good ones. Slow. Scared. Not guilty only because he was lazy. Four centurions (officers who led companies of soldiers) of the 22nd legion tried to protect Galba's statues. Their names were Nonius Receptus, Donatius Valens, Romilius Marcellus and Calpurnius Repentinus. A rush of soldiers grabbed them and put them in chains. After that, nobody kept any loyalty to the earlier oath, or even remembered it. Mutinies work like that: whichever side has more men becomes everybody's side. The night after January 1, the eagle-bearer of the 4th legion (the soldier who carried the legion's eagle banner) reached Colonia Agrippinensis. The man told Vitellius, who was at dinner, that the 4th and 22nd had thrown down Galba's statues and sworn by the Senate and people of Rome. That oath looked empty. No surprise there. Still, the decision was made to grab the shaky luck of the moment and offer up an emperor. Vitellius sent messengers to the legions and legates. The message: the army of Upper Germany had broken away from Galba. So they had two choices. Fight the rebels. Or, if they wanted peace and harmony, make an emperor. Taking a ruler was safer than searching for one.

1:57 Fabius Valens moved first. He was the most eager of the legates, and the 1st legion's winter camp was the nearest. The next day he swept into Colonia Agrippinensis with cavalry from his legion and from the auxiliaries (troops from outside the legions). He hailed Vitellius as emperor. The other legions of the province jumped in, all racing to outdo one another. The Upper army dropped the fancy names of the Senate and the people of Rome. On January 3, it joined Vitellius. So much for the state. You could tell that for the two days before, this had not really been the army of the state. The people of Colonia Agrippinensis, the Treveri and the Lingones burned just as hot. They offered auxiliaries, horses, weapons and money. Each gave whatever he was strong in: body, wealth or talent. It was not only the leaders of the cities and camps, who had plenty on hand and big hopes from a victory already won. The companies and the common soldiers went further. They handed over their travel money, their belts and the silver-trimmed decorations on their weapons, in place of cash. Impulse. Passion. Greed.

1:58 Vitellius praised the soldiers for their eagerness. Then he started handing out favors. He gave Roman Knights the jobs in the emperor's household that freedmen (former slaves who had been set free) usually did. He paid the centurions' exemption fees out of the treasury. Most of the time, he went along with the soldiers' savagery when they demanded that many men be punished. Only rarely did he dodge it, by pretending to chain them up. Pompeius Propinquus, the procurator of Belgica (an official who handled money and property for the emperor), was killed at once. Julius Burdo, commander of the German fleet, was another story. Vitellius cleverly pulled him out of danger. The army was furious at Burdo. They charged him with making up the accusation against Fonteius Capito first, and then laying the trap. The soldiers loved Capito's memory. In that mood, killing a man in the open was easy. Sparing one took a trick. So Burdo was kept locked up. He was let go only after Vitellius won, when the soldiers' hatred had faded. Meanwhile, a centurion named Crispinus was handed over as an offering. He had stained himself with Capito's blood. To the men demanding a death, he was plainly guilty. To Vitellius, he came cheap.

1:59 Then came Julius Civilis, a powerful man among the Batavians. Vitellius got him out of danger too, because killing him might turn the fierce Batavian nation against them. Eight Batavian cohorts (units of auxiliary troops) were in the Lingones' territory. They were auxiliaries of the 14th legion, but in the chaos of the time they had split off from it. Whichever way they leaned, they would weigh a lot, as allies or as enemies. Vitellius ordered the centurions Nonius, Donatius, Romilius and Calpurnius, the four I mentioned earlier, put to death. They were convicted of loyalty. Loyalty, of all things. Among rebels, no crime was worse. Valerius Asiaticus, legate of the province of Belgica, joined the cause. Vitellius soon made him his son-in-law. Junius Blaesus, governor of Lugdunensian Gaul, joined too. He brought the Italian legion and the Taurian cavalry wing, which were stationed at Lugdunum, and the Raetian troops joined right away too. Not even Britain hesitated.

1:60 Britain's governor, Trebellius Maximus, was despised by his army. They hated him for his greed and his stinginess. Roscius Caelius, the legate of the 20th legion, made the hatred worse. He and Trebellius had been at odds for a long time, and the chance of civil war was like fuel on an old fire. Trebellius charged Caelius with mutiny and with wrecking army discipline. Caelius charged Trebellius with robbing the legions and leaving them broke. Meanwhile, the army's good order fell apart over these shameful fights between legates. It got so bad that the auxiliary soldiers shouted Trebellius down and drove him out too. The cohorts and cavalry wings sided with Caelius. Trebellius was left alone, and he fled to Vitellius. And the province? Calm. Even with its governor, a former consul, gone. The legates of the legions ran it together, equal under the law. Caelius had more power, though. He just dared more.

1:61 Vitellius had the army of Britain behind him now. His strength and resources were huge. So he split the war into two jobs. Two commanders and two routes. Fabius Valens took the first. His orders were to win the Gauls over if he could. If they refused, he was to ravage their land. After that he would break into Italy through the Cottian Alps. Caecina got the nearer crossing. He would come down over the Pennine Alps. Valens had the best of the Lower army: the eagle of the 5th legion, cohorts, and cavalry wings. That came to about forty thousand armed men. Not a small force. Caecina led thirty thousand from Upper Germany, and the 21st legion was his real muscle. Both men also got German helper troops. Vitellius would follow behind with the full weight of the war. He used those same helpers to fill out his own forces.

1:62 Army and emperor made a strange mismatch. The soldiers were pushing forward and demanding their weapons. The Gauls were trembling, and Spain was holding back. Winter should not stop them. Neither should the delays of a lazy peace. They had to invade Italy. They had to grab the city. In civil war, speed is safer than anything else, because action counts for more than planning. Vitellius? He dragged his feet. He was enjoying the perks of power before he had earned them, with lazy luxury and fancy feasts. He was drunk by midday and stuffed with food. Still, the soldiers' own fire did the commander's job for him. It was like having the emperor there, giving hope to the eager and fear to the lazy. They lined up, ready, and demanded the signal to march. The name Germanicus was added to Vitellius's right away. But he would not be called Caesar, even after a win. On the very day the march began, a lucky sign came for Fabius Valens and his army. An eagle flew out ahead of the column. It glided gently, moving as the column moved, like a guide for the road. For a long stretch the soldiers cheered. The bird stayed calm and unafraid. Everyone took it as a sure sign of a great and successful venture.

1:63 The army marched into the land of the Treveri with no worries, as if among friends. At Divodurum, a town of the Mediomatrici, the people welcomed them with every courtesy. Then panic hit the soldiers. Out of nowhere. They grabbed their weapons all at once and went after a town that had done nothing wrong. It was not loot, and it was not greed for plunder. It was wild fury, and nobody could name the cause. That made it all the harder to fix. At last their commander's pleas calmed them, and they held back from wiping the town out. But about four thousand people were already dead. Fear spread through Gaul fast. Soon whole communities came out to meet the column, with their leaders praying beside them. Women and children lay along the roads. Anything else that might soften an enemy's anger was held out too. None of it was for war. All of it was to buy peace.

1:64 Fabius Valens got the news in the land of the Leuci. Galba was murdered, and Otho was in charge. The soldiers felt no joy and no fear, but they were chewing over the war in their heads. The Gauls stopped hesitating. They hated Otho and Vitellius equally. With Vitellius, though, there was fear too. Next came the Lingones, who were on Vitellius's side. They gave the army a kind welcome, and both sides tried to outdo each other in good behavior. It didn't last. Why? The cohorts that had split off from the 14th legion were wild, as I said above, and Fabius Valens had added them to his army. First came quarrels. Then a brawl broke out between the Batavians and the legionaries. Soldiers picked sides, and it nearly blew up into a full battle. Valens stopped it. He punished a few men and reminded the Batavians of the command they had forgotten. Then he went looking for an excuse to fight the Aedui, and found none. They were ordered to bring money and weapons, and they even handed over supplies for free. The Aedui did it out of fear. The people of Lugdunum did it happily. Still, the Italian legion and the Taurian cavalry wing were pulled away. The 18th cohort was left at Lugdunum, in its usual winter quarters. Manlius Valens, legate (commander) of the Italian legion, had served the cause well. Vitellius gave him no honor anyway. Fabius had slandered him in secret accusations he knew nothing about. Fabius also praised him out loud. The point was to trick him more easily. Ouch.

1:65 Lugdunum and Vienna had an old fight, and the recent war set it on fire. The two cities had hurt each other again and again. Too often, and too bitterly, for it to be only about Nero and Galba. Galba had been angry at Lugdunum. He turned its money over to the treasury, while Vienna got great honor. So the rivalry and envy grew. Hatred tied the two cities together. One river split them. Picture neighbors glaring across a fence, except the fence was a river. The people of Lugdunum went to work on the soldiers one by one, pushing them to destroy Vienna. They reminded the men that Vienna had besieged their colony (a town of Roman settlers). It had backed Vindex's attempts. It had recently raised legions to protect Galba. After laying out every reason to hate, they pointed to the loot waiting there. Soon the whispering stopped. The begging got loud. The soldiers should go as avengers, they said. They should tear down the seat of the Gallic war, because everything there was foreign and hostile. As for themselves, they were a Roman colony, part of the army, and partners in good times and bad. If luck turned against them, the soldiers should not leave them to an angry enemy.

1:66 Arguments like these, and plenty more, pushed the army along. Even the legates and party leaders thought the soldiers' anger could never be put out. The people of Vienna knew exactly how much danger they were in. So they came out carrying veils and woolen bands, the way suppliants (people begging for protection) did. As the column marched up, they clung to the soldiers' weapons, knees and feet. It worked. The soldiers' hearts bent. Fabius Valens handed three hundred sesterces, small Roman coins, to each soldier. The age and rank of the colony counted for something. He urged them to spare the safety and lives of the people of Vienna, and he got a fair hearing. Still, Vienna paid a fine: its weapons, handed over in public. The city also gave the soldiers supplies from its own stores and from shared ones. But a steady rumor said Valens himself had been bought for a large sum. Why? He had been poor for a long time. Then he was suddenly rich, and he hid the change badly. Years of want had lit up his cravings. Poor in his youth, he was a big spender in old age. The army crawled through the lands of the Allobroges and the Vocontii. The commander sold the length of the marches and the moves between camps. His deals with landowners and city magistrates were ugly. He was so threatening that at Lucus, a town of the Vocontii, he held torches to the place until someone paid him off. When there was no money to be had, he could be bought with sex and adultery. That is how they reached the Alps.

1:67 Caecina went hunting for more loot and blood, and the Helvetii gave him his excuse. They were a Gallic people, once famous for their fighters and later for the fame of their name. Now they riled up his restless temper. They knew nothing about Galba's murder, and they refused to accept Vitellius as ruler. The war began with the greed and rush of the 21st legion. Those soldiers grabbed money meant to pay a fort's garrison. The Helvetii had long held that fort with their own soldiers and their own pay. They took it badly. Wouldn't you? The Helvetii intercepted letters that were on their way to the Pannonian legions in the name of the German army. They also held a centurion and some soldiers in custody. Caecina was hungry for war. For every offense, he hit back fast, before the offender could regret it. He moved camp quickly and wrecked the countryside. Then he plundered a place that had grown into a town during the long peace. People came there for the healing waters, and it was always busy. Not for long. He also sent messengers to the Raetian auxiliaries with orders to hit the Helvetii from behind once they turned on the legion. A trap was waiting.

1:68 Bold before the danger, terrified in the middle of it. That was the Helvetii. Their leader at the start was Claudius Severus, their own pick. Yet nobody knew how to use weapons, hold a line, or plan together. Fight veterans? Certain ruin. Sit through a siege? Risky, with walls crumbling from age. Great choices. On one side stood Caecina and his strong army. On the other stood the cavalry wings and cohorts of Raetia. Add the Raetians' own young men, used to weapons and trained in army ways. Killing and wrecking happened on every side. The Helvetii stumbled back and forth between the two armies and threw their weapons away. A large part of them, wounded or scattered, fled to Mount Vocetius. A Thracian cohort was sent in at once and drove them off. The Germans and Raetians kept chasing, through the woods and into every hiding place, and the Helvetii were cut down there. Many thousands were killed. Many thousands more were sold into slavery. After everything was torn down, the army marched on Aventicum, the capital of the people, in a hostile column. Envoys were sent out to hand over the city, and the surrender was accepted. Caecina punished Julius Alpinus, one of the chiefs, as the man who stirred up the war. The rest he handed over to Vitellius, for mercy or for cruelty. Their fate was in Vitellius's hands.

1:69 Which was harder to calm down, the emperor or the soldiers? Hard to say. The soldiers wanted the whole state destroyed, and they shoved their spears and fists in the envoys' faces. Vitellius himself kept up the threats. Then Claudius Cossus, one of the envoys, stepped up. He was known for his skill at speaking. But he hid it behind a fitting show of fear, and that made him even more effective. The soldiers' mood softened. Crowds are like weather vanes in a gale. They spin on the spot, as quick to pity as they were to rage. Tears came, and the envoys kept asking for better terms, more firmly, and they won pardon and safety for their state. That was close.

1:70 Caecina stayed a few days among the Helvetii, waiting for Vitellius to decide and getting ready to cross the Alps. Then good news came from Italy. The cavalry wing of Silius, stationed around the Padus, had sworn loyalty to Vitellius. Those riders knew him already. Back in Africa, he had been their proconsul. Nero had called them up to send ahead to Egypt, but he recalled them when Vindex's war broke out. They were still in Italy when their decurions (cavalry officers) went to work on them. The decurions had never heard a word about Otho. Their loyalty was to Vitellius. They praised the strength of the legions on the march and the fame of the German army. So the cavalry switched to the Vitellianists. They came with a gift for the new emperor: the strongest towns across the Padus. Mediolanum. Novaria. Eporedia. Vercellae. Caecina heard all this from the soldiers themselves. One cavalry wing could not guard the widest part of Italy. So he sent ahead cohorts of Gauls, Lusitanians and Britons, detachments of Germans, and the Petrian cavalry wing. Then he hesitated over a choice. Should he turn through the Raetian ridges into Noricum? There sat Petronius Urbicus, the procurator (an official who ran money and property for the emperor). Urbicus had called up auxiliaries and broken the bridges over the rivers, and people thought he was loyal to Otho. But Caecina feared losing the cohorts and cavalry he had already sent ahead. He also reasoned that holding Italy was the bigger glory. Wherever the fight was settled, Noricum would go to the winner along with the other prizes. So he led the soldiers who fight under the standards, the heavy column of the legions, over the Pennine route. The Alps were still in winter. Lovely. He went anyway.

1:71 Everyone expected Otho to go soft on pleasure and laziness. He didn't. He put off his pleasures, hid his luxury, and arranged everything for the dignity of the empire. Fake virtues, vices sure to come back: people found that frightening. Then came Marius Celsus, chosen to be consul. Otho had once kept him safe from the soldiers' cruelty by pretending to chain him. Now Otho had him summoned to the Capitol. Otho wanted a name for mercy, and Celsus was a distinguished man from the other side. Celsus admitted the charge of staying faithful to Galba and even claimed it as a credit to himself. Did Otho treat him like a man being pardoned? No. He called the gods to witness that the two were reconciled. Celsus went straight into his circle of closest friends, and soon Otho chose him as one of his generals for the war. And Celsus stayed loyal to Otho too, as if by fate. His loyalty was faultless. It was also unlucky. The leading men of the city were pleased by his rescue, and the people celebrated it. Even the soldiers didn't mind. They admired the same loyalty they had been angry at.

1:72 Another burst of joy followed, for a different reason: Tigellinus was finally dead. His parents were obscure, his boyhood was disgraceful, and his old age was shameless. Vice was quicker than virtue, so he used it. That got him command of the night watch and the Praetorian Guard, and other rewards that belong to virtue. Cruelty came next. Then greed, then the crimes of grown men. He pushed Nero into every kind of crime and did some things without Nero even knowing. In the end he deserted Nero and betrayed him. So nobody was demanded for punishment more stubbornly. People who hated Nero wanted it, and people who missed Nero wanted it too, for opposite reasons. Under Galba, the power of Titus Vinius had protected him, because Vinius claimed Tigellinus had saved his daughter. He had, no doubt about that. Out of mercy? Hardly, from a man who had killed so many. He saved her to keep a way out for the future. Every really bad man distrusts the present and fears change, so each one builds private favors to guard against public hatred. None of them cares about innocence. They only care about taking turns at going unpunished. The people were angrier than ever, with new hatred of Titus Vinius piled onto the old hatred of Tigellinus. They ran together from all over the city into the Palace and the forums. They poured into the circus and the theaters, where a crowd has the most freedom, and roared rebel shouts. Finally, at the springs of Sinuessa, Tigellinus got word that his last hour had come. His mistresses were hugging and kissing him, and he stalled in shameful ways. Then he cut his throat with a razor. A late death. A dishonorable one.

1:73 Around the same time, people demanded the execution of Calvia Crispinilla. Otho used all sorts of tricks to save her, and his reputation took a hit for being two-faced about it. She had been Nero's teacher in lust. She had crossed to Africa to push Clodius Macer into taking up arms, and she had plotted openly to starve the Roman people. Starve them. Openly. So how did she come through unharmed under Galba, Otho and Vitellius? Later she had won the favor of the whole community, and a marriage to a former consul backed her up. Soon she was powerful, thanks to her money and her having no children. Money and no children. Good times or bad, they still count.

1:74 Letters flew back and forth. Otho kept sending Vitellius letters dripping with sugary flattery. He offered money, favors and any quiet corner Vitellius wanted for a life of luxury. Vitellius offered the same, a bit more gently at first. Both were faking it, and it looked silly and undignified. Soon the sweetness soured. They started throwing each other's messy love lives and shameful acts back and forth like insults in a food fight. Neither one was lying. Ouch. Then came the messengers. Otho called back the ones Galba had sent. He sent new ones in the Senate's name to both German armies, to the Italian legion and to the troops at Lugdunum. Those messengers stayed with Vitellius, and so eagerly that nobody could say they were being held. Otho had also attached some Praetorians (the emperor's bodyguard troops in Rome) to the messengers, as a supposed honor. They were sent back before they could mix with the legions. Fabius Valens wrote letters too, in the name of the German army. They went to the Praetorian cohorts and the city cohorts (the troops that policed Rome). He bragged about how strong his side was and offered peace. Then he scolded them. Why had they handed the power to Otho, when it had been given to Vitellius so long before? Fighting words.

1:75 Now the soldiers in Rome. The Praetorians and the city cohorts got promises and threats in the same breath. The message: you can't win a war. In peace, you lose nothing. Did it work? Not on the Praetorians. Their loyalty held. Then both men tried spies, with Otho sending plotters into Germany and Vitellius sending his into the city. Both missions flopped. Vitellius's men slipped away safe, since nobody knew anybody in that huge crowd and they passed unseen. Otho's men had it the other way around. In a place where everyone knew everyone, their unfamiliar faces gave them away. Vitellius also wrote to Titianus, Otho's brother. He threatened Titianus and his son with death unless Vitellius's mother and children stayed safe. Both families survived. Did Otho spare Vitellius's family out of fear? Nobody knows. Vitellius, the winner, got the credit for mercy. Funny how that works.

1:76 Otho's first real boost of confidence came from Illyricum. A messenger reported that the legions of Dalmatia, Pannonia and Moesia had sworn loyalty to him. Spain sent the same news, and an official proclamation praised Cluvius Rufus. Then people learned that Spain had turned to Vitellius. Oops. Not even Aquitania held for long, though Julius Cordus had bound it to Otho's oath. Nowhere was there real loyalty or love. Fear and need swung people back and forth like a door in the wind. That same fear pulled the province of Narbonensis over to Vitellius, an easy switch to the closer, stronger side. The distant provinces stayed with Otho, along with every army the sea cut off. They didn't care about his cause. But the name of Rome counted for a lot. So did the Senate's name, used as a front. And whatever people heard first stuck in their heads. Vespasian made the army in Judea take Otho's oath. Mucianus did the same with the legions in Syria. Egypt and every province facing the East were held in Otho's name. Africa was just as loyal. Carthage led the way, without waiting for the proconsul, Vipstanus Apronianus (the governor of the province). Crescens, a freedman of Nero's (a former slave Nero had set free), threw the common people a feast to celebrate the new reign. In bad times, even men like him make themselves part of the state. The crowd dove into nearly everything with no restraint. The rest followed Carthage.

1:77 The armies and provinces were split. Vitellius needed a war to grab the power he wanted. Otho, meanwhile, ran the empire as if it were peacetime. Some of what he did suited the dignity of the state. Most of it didn't, because he rushed to handle whatever was in front of him. He took the consulship himself, with his brother Titianus, and they held it until the first of March. The next months he saved for Verginius, a soothing gift to the German army. Pompeius Vopiscus was added to Verginius, with an old friendship as the excuse. Most people saw it as an honor to the people of Vienna. Fair enough. The other consulships went as Nero or Galba had planned. Caelius and Flavius Sabinus served until the first of July, and Arrius Antoninus and Marius Celsus until September. Vitellius, even after winning, left those honors alone. Otho also handed priesthoods and augurships (religious posts) to old men who had already held high office, a crowning honor. Young nobles back from exile got a warm welcome, too. He gave them back the priesthoods of their grandfathers and fathers. Then came three men: Cadius Rufus, Pedius Blaesus and Saevinius Pomptinius. Under Claudius and Nero, they had been convicted of extortion. Now they got their Senate seats back. Some people wanted to let them off. So the charge got a new name. What had really been greed was made to look like treason. Nice trick. Treason charges were so hated back then that even good laws were dying out.

1:78 Gifts were Otho's tool for winning over cities and provinces. The people of Hispalis and Emerita got extra families, and all the Lingones became Roman citizens. Baetica was handed the states of the Moors. Cappadocia and Africa got new rights, more for show than meant to last. The pressure of the moment and his worries could excuse a lot of this. Still, he never forgot his old love affairs. By a decree of the Senate, he had Poppaea's statues put back up. People even believed he had thought about honoring Nero's memory, hoping to win over the crowd. Some people set up pictures of Nero. On some days, the people and the soldiers hailed him as "Nero Otho", as if two names together added rank and honor. Otho himself left it hanging. Was he afraid to ban it? Or too ashamed to accept it?

1:79 Meanwhile, nobody was watching the frontier. With everyone's minds on civil war, the empire's outside enemies got bolder. Take the Roxolani, a Sarmatae people. The winter before, they had wiped out two cohorts. Now, full of hope, they burst into Moesia with about nine thousand horsemen. Their wildness and their success had them fired up. They wanted plunder, not a fight. Then the 3rd legion and its auxiliaries struck them suddenly, while they wandered around not paying attention. The Romans were ready in every way. The Sarmatae were scattered. Some were weighed down by loot they had grabbed in their greed. The slippery roads robbed their horses of speed. They were cut down like men tied up. Strange but true: the Sarmatae's courage is, so to speak, outside of them. On foot, they fight like cowards. But when they come charging in squadrons, hardly any battle line can hold. That day was damp, and the frost was thawing. Their lances and swords were no help. Those swords are so long they need both hands. The horses kept slipping, and the armor was heavy. Their chiefs and every high-ranking man wear that armor, made of iron plates or very hard leather, and blows can't pierce it. But if an enemy charge knocks the wearer down, he can't get up. They also sank into the deep, soft snow. The Roman soldier was light in his armor. He rushed in with throwing spears or lances. When it was needed, he stabbed the Sarmatae at close range with a light sword. They had no defense, since they don't use shields. A few survived and hid in the marshes. There they died, worn down by the harsh winter and their wounds. Back in Rome, the news landed. Marcus Aponius, who held Moesia, was given a triumphal statue. Fulvius Aurelius, Julianus Titius and Numisius Lupus were legates of legions (the generals who commanded them). They were given the honors of consuls. Otho was thrilled. He grabbed the glory for himself, as if he too had won the war and made the state bigger through his generals and armies.

1:80 One small spark nearly burned Rome down. Nobody saw it coming. Otho had ordered the 17th cohort brought into Rome from the colony of Ostia. Varius Crispinus, a tribune of the Praetorians, got the job of arming them, and he wanted it done quietly while the camp was quiet. Then he opened the armory and loaded the cohort's wagons at nightfall. So much for quiet. The hour looked suspicious. The reason sounded like a charge. His try for quiet became an uproar. Weapons in front of drunk men are like matches near hay, and these men itched to use them. The soldiers grumbled that the tribunes and centurions were traitors. The slaves of the senators' households, they said, were being armed to destroy Otho. Some of the men were too drunk to know better. The worst were after loot. And the crowd, as always, wanted any new commotion. In the dark, the better soldiers stopped obeying. The rioters cut down the tribune who stood against them, and the strictest of the centurions. Then they grabbed weapons, bared their swords and mounted horses. They rode for the city and the Palace, swords out.

1:81 Picture a dinner party where everyone is terrified. Otho was hosting it, and the guests were the leading women and men of the city. Was this a sudden fit of the soldiers, or a trick by the emperor? Stay and get caught, or run and get scattered? Both choices were bad. For a while they pretended to be calm. Then their fear slipped out. All the while they watched Otho's face, because that is what suspicion does to a mind. Otho was afraid, so he was feared. But he was just as alarmed for the Senate as for himself. He had already sent the Praetorian prefects to calm the soldiers down. Now he told everyone to leave the dinner, fast. Magistrates all over the city threw off their badges of office and dodged their crowds of attendants and slaves. Old men and women hurried through the dark by different routes. Few went home. Most headed for friends' houses. The rest went to the poorest people who depended on them, and to hiding places that might not hold. Nowhere was certain.

1:82 The Palace doors did not stop them. The soldiers burst into the dinner and demanded that Otho be shown to them. The tribune Julius Martialis got in the way, and so did Vitellius Saturninus, a prefect of a legion. Both were wounded. Weapons and threats were everywhere. One moment the soldiers went after the centurions and tribunes. The next, the whole Senate. Their minds were wild with blind panic, and they could not pick out one person to be angry at. So they demanded a free hand against everybody. Finally Otho climbed up on a couch, against the dignity of his office. With prayers and tears he barely held them back. They went back to camp unwillingly, and not innocent. The next morning the city looked conquered. Houses were shut. Hardly anyone walked the streets. The common people were gloomy. The soldiers stared at the ground, with more sadness than regret. The prefects Licinius Proculus and Plotius Firmus spoke to the companies one by one. Some speeches were gentler, some rougher, each by the speaker's own character. They ended with 5,000 sesterces (a Roman coin) paid to each soldier. Then Otho dared to walk into the camp, and the tribunes and centurions crowded around him. They had thrown away their badges of service, and they begged him for rest and safety. The soldiers felt the sting. Back in line, they even demanded, on their own, that the ringleaders be punished. Nobody had to ask.

1:83 Rome was a mess, and the army was split. The best soldiers wanted the lawlessness cured right now. The crowd and most of the others loved riots, and they loved a rule built on popularity. Not a calm bunch. They were also the easiest to push into civil war, through disorder and plunder. Otho saw the problem. Power won by crime can't be held with a sudden show of modesty and old-fashioned seriousness. Still, the danger to the city and the Senate worried him. So in the end he spoke like this: "Comrades, I didn't come to fire up your love for me. I didn't come to push you toward courage either, because you have plenty of both. I came to ask you to rein in your bravery and put some limit on your affection for me. This latest riot didn't start from greed or hatred. Those have split many armies. It didn't start from running from danger, or fearing it. Your devotion was too big. It fired you up more fiercely than wisely. Good causes often end in ruin when you don't use judgment. We are marching to war. Can every message be heard in the open? Can every plan be talked over with everyone in the room? Not with events moving this fast. Soldiers must be kept in the dark about some things, just as they must be told others. Commanders have authority, and discipline is strict, for a reason. Often it's best if even centurions and tribunes only get orders. If every man can ask why he's being ordered, obedience dies. Command dies with it. And will you grab your weapons in the dead of night, out in the field, too? Are one or two worthless drunks going to stain their hands with a centurion's or a tribune's blood? Break into their emperor's tent? (I can't believe it was more than that.)

1:84 I know you did that for me. But in the running around and the dark and the confusion, a chance can open up even against me. Think about it. Suppose Vitellius and his followers got to choose our state of mind. What would they pray for? Mutiny and discord. Soldiers who won't obey centurions, and centurions who won't obey tribunes. Then every one of us, foot and horse, would rush to our own destruction. Comrades, an army holds together by obeying, not by quizzing its commanders. The army that is bravest in danger is the one that was calmest before it. Hang on to your weapons and your nerve. Planning is my job. I will steer your courage. Only a few were at fault. Only two will be punished. Everyone else, wipe that disgraceful night out of your memory. No army anywhere should ever hear shouts like that against the Senate again. Demand the downfall of the empire's head and every province's pride? By heaven, not even the Germans Vitellius is stirring up against us right now would dare. And would the sons of Italy, Rome's true youth, howl for blood and slaughter? Against the very order whose splendor and glory leave Vitellius's grubby, unknown party in the shade? Vitellius has taken hold of a few peoples. He has a kind of picture of an army. The Senate is with us. So the state stands on one side, and the enemies of the state stand on the other. Do you really think this beautiful city stands on houses and roofs and heaps of stone? Those things are silent and lifeless. They can be torn down and rebuilt just as easily. The empire's lasting life, peace among the nations, and my safety along with yours all rest on the Senate's safety. The father and founder of our city set this order up under good signs. It has lasted, unbroken and undying, from the kings to the emperors. We got it from our ancestors, and so let us hand it on to those who come after us. Senators come from you. Emperors come from senators."

1:85 Otho's speech was meant to shame the soldiers and calm them down. The punishment was mild, too: he had ordered no more than two men punished. People were grateful for both. For now, the unruly ones settled. But quiet? Not in Rome. Weapons still clattered, and the whole city had the look of war. The soldiers weren't rioting. They had simply scattered through private houses in disguise. From there they kept a nasty watch on anyone whose high birth, wealth or famous name had gotten them talked about. Plenty of people also believed that Vitellius's soldiers had sneaked in to learn where everyone's loyalties lay. Suspicion everywhere. Even your own home hardly felt safe. The worst fear, though, was out in public. Every new rumor changed people's faces and moods, because nobody wanted to look doubtful about the news or not glad enough about the good. Then the Senate met in the Senate House. Staying safe in there was almost impossible. Silence looked like defiance. Speaking freely looked suspicious. Otho knew flattery when he heard it, since he had been a private citizen not long ago and had talked that way himself. So the senators twisted their proposals every which way. They called Vitellius an enemy and a traitor to his country. The careful ones stuck to ordinary insults. A few tossed out true accusations, but only in the middle of shouting, when many voices rose at once. The rest drowned themselves out in a jumble of words. Safer to be loud than clear.

1:86 Then came the signs. Different sources reported them, and every one made the terror worse. At the Capitol (Rome's big hill, with the great temple of Jupiter), people said, the reins had slipped. They were the reins of the two-horse chariot where Victory stood. Something bigger than a human being had burst out of Juno's shrine. The statue of the divine Julius, on an island in the Tiber, had turned from west to east on a clear, still day. An ox had spoken in Etruria. Animals had given birth to strange young. In rough old times, people noticed such things even in peace. Now they only come up when everyone is scared. Funny how that works. But the biggest scare was real: the Tiber River suddenly flooded. It cost people something right away, and it made them dread what came next. The water smashed the wooden bridge. A pile of wreckage jammed in its path and shoved the river back, so it spilled everywhere. It filled the low, flat parts of the city, and places people thought were safe from floods. Many were swept off the streets. More were trapped in their shops and bedrooms. Then hunger hit ordinary people. No work meant no pay, and food was scarce. The standing water rotted the foundations of the apartment blocks, and when the river dropped, the buildings collapsed. Once the danger passed, people found something new to worry about. The Campus Martius and the Flaminian Way were blocked, just as Otho was getting ready for his campaign. That road was his route to the war. Maybe chance did it. Maybe nature did. People called it a sign of coming disaster anyway. Of course they did.

1:87 Time to plan the war. Otho purified the city with the proper rites, then thought hard about how to fight. His target was Narbonese Gaul, a region of Gaul. Why there? Vitellius's armies had shut the Pennine and Cottian Alps and the other ways into Gaul. Otho's fleet was strong, and it was loyal to him. He also had fresh soldiers. The survivors of the slaughter at the Mulvian Bridge, whom Galba had cruelly kept locked up, now filled a legion. The rest got hope of honorable service later. To the fleet he added city cohorts (Rome's own garrison) and many Praetorians (the emperor's bodyguard). These men were the strength of the army. They were also advisers and spies on their own commanders. The overall command went to three men: Antonius Novellus and Suedius Clemens, both former chief centurions, and Aemilius Pacensis. Otho had given Pacensis back the tribune's job that Galba took away. The freedman Moschus, a former slave, stayed in charge of the ships, just as before. His job was to watch men more honorable than himself. Suetonius Paullinus, Marius Celsus and Annius Gallus were named to lead the infantry and cavalry. But Otho trusted one man above them all: Licinius Proculus, prefect of the Praetorian Guard. Proculus was good at soldiering in the city. War was new to him. So how did he get ahead? By running down the others. Paullinus had authority, Celsus had drive, and Gallus had maturity, and each one gave him something to attack. Attacking is the easiest thing in the world. That is how a crooked, sly man beat good and modest ones. The sly beat the good.

1:88 Meanwhile Cornelius Dolabella was sent off to the colony of Aquinum. The custody was neither strict nor secret. Dolabella had committed no crime. His old name and his family tie to Galba had singled him out, and that was all. Otho also ordered many magistrates and a big share of the ex-consuls to join the campaign. Not as partners. Not as helpers in the war. As an escort. One of them was Lucius Vitellius, brother of the rival emperor. He got the same treatment as everyone else: not as the emperor's brother, and not as an enemy. Fear hung over the whole city, and no rank was safe from it. The leading senators were weak with age and soft from a long peace. The nobles were sluggish and had forgotten war. The Knights, Rome's wealthy class below the senators, knew nothing about military service. The harder they tried to bury their fear, the more it showed. Some people, from silly showing off, bought splendid weapons and handsome horses. Others bought dinner-party luxuries and temptations to vice, and called it war gear. Yes, really. The sensible thought about peace and the good of the state. The emptiest heads, the ones who couldn't see ahead, swelled up with false hope. And many whose credit had collapsed in peacetime were nervous, yet lively in the disorder. Chaos was the safest place for them.

1:89 What about ordinary people? The crowd had no say in any of this, because public affairs were simply too huge. Little by little, they began to feel the cost of war. All the money went to the soldiers, and food prices climbed. Vindex's uprising had not worn the common people down this much. Back then the city stayed safe, and the war was out in the provinces. Fought between the legions and the Gauls, it felt like a foreign war. Look at the record. Ever since the divine Augustus built up the Caesars' power, Rome's wars had been fought far away. They brought worry or glory to one man alone. Under Tiberius and Caligula, only the troubles of peace touched the state. Scribonianus's attempt against Claudius? Heard of and crushed in the same moment. Nero was driven out more by messages and rumors than by weapons. This time was different. The legions and fleets were on the move, and, as rarely before, the Praetorians and city troops had been led into the battle line. The East and the West and all the strength on both sides stood at their backs. With other generals, the war could have dragged on. Some people urged Otho to wait as he set out. One reason was religious: the sacred shields hadn't yet been put back in their place. He threw out every delay. Delay had been fatal to Nero too, he said. Caecina was already over the Alps, and Caecina kept pushing him.

1:90 On March 14, Otho handed the state over to the senators. Then came the gifts. He gave the people recalled from exile whatever was left of Nero's seized property, the part that had not yet been paid into the treasury. It was a very fair gift, and it looked splendid. Nice gesture. In practice it was useless, because the collecting had been rushed long before. Soon Otho called an assembly. He pumped up the greatness of the city, and the agreement of the people and the Senate, all of it on his side. About Vitellius's party he stayed calm, and he blamed the legions for not knowing better, not for daring too much. And he never named Vitellius at all. Was that restraint really his own? Maybe. Or the speechwriter was scared for himself and cut the insults to Vitellius. Remember, Otho was believed to use the talent of Galerius Trachalus in city business, and Suetonius Paullinus and Marius Celsus in military planning. Some people even recognized Trachalus's style, famous from all his court speeches. It was broad and ringing, made to fill a crowd's ears. Then the crowd joined in. The shouts and cries were the usual flattery, way too much and totally fake. They raced to show the most zeal and the best wishes, like people seeing off the dictator Caesar or the emperor Augustus. Fear? Love? Neither. They just loved being slaves. Everyone had private reasons, like members of one household, and public honor already counted for nothing. Otho left. His brother Salvius Titianus got the city's peace and the empire's worries.
