5:1 Titus Caesar had one job: finish conquering Judea. His father had picked him for it. Early that same year, Titus was already a well-known soldier, going back to when both of them were private citizens. Now he had more power and a bigger name, and provinces and armies competed to show how much they backed him. He wanted to be thought bigger than his position, so he acted the part. He stayed dignified and ready for action. He was friendly and easy to talk to, which got people's best effort out of them. He often joined the ordinary soldiers at work and on the march, and he never lost the respect a general was owed. Then the army gathered. Three legions met him in Judea: the 5th, the 10th and the 15th, Vespasian's veteran troops. He added the 12th from Syria, plus men of the 22nd and the 3rd, brought from Alexandria. Twenty allied cohorts came along, and eight cavalry squadrons. So did two kings, Agrippa and Sohemus. King Antiochus's troops were there too, with a strong force of Arabs, who hated the Jews the way neighbors usually hate each other. Many others showed up from Rome and from Italy. Why the rush? Each one hoped to win Titus's favor before anyone else did. Everybody wanted in. With this army, Titus crossed into enemy territory. His column marched in good order, he scouted every spot, and he stayed ready to fight at any moment. Then he made camp, not far from Jerusalem. The city was close.

5:2 I'm about to tell the last day of a famous city. So first, how it began. Some say the Jews were runaways from the island of Crete. They settled in the farthest part of Libya, around the time Jupiter's force drove Saturn out of his kingdom. Proof? They point to the name Judaei. Crete has a famous mountain called Ida. The neighbors there, the Idaei, supposedly got the longer, foreign-sounding name Judaei. Others say the Jews began in the reign of Isis. Egypt's overflowing crowds were emptied out into the nearby lands, led by Hierosolymus and Judas. Many say the Jews came from the Ethiopians. Fear and hatred of their neighbors pushed them out, back in King Cepheus's day. Still others call them Assyrian newcomers. Short of land, they took over part of Egypt. Soon they lived in cities of their own, in the Hebrew country and the lands closer to Syria. And some give the Jews a famous start. The Solymi were a nation celebrated in Homer's poems. They founded a city and named it Hierosolyma, after themselves. Five origin stories. Take your pick.

5:3 Now the part most writers agree on. Disease swept Egypt and horribly disfigured people's bodies. King Bocchoris asked the oracle of Hammon for a cure. The answer: purify your kingdom. Carry this kind of people, hated by the gods, off to other lands. So the crowd was hunted down and rounded up, then left in empty country. The crowd sat there numb, crying. Then one exile, Moyses, spoke up. Don't expect help from gods or from men, he warned, because both have abandoned you. Trust yourselves. Take as your heaven-sent leader whoever first gets you out of this misery. They agreed. Nobody knew the way, so they just set out. Nothing wore them down like thirst. Soon they lay all over the plains, close to death. Then a herd of wild asses wandered off its grazing and up to a shady rock, under trees. Moyses followed the asses. He guessed from the grassy ground where the water was, and he opened plenty of veins of it. Relief, at last. Ever set out with no idea where you were going? They did. They kept going for six days without a stop. On the seventh they drove out the people living there and took the land. There they dedicated a city and a temple.

5:4 Next came Moyses's rites. He wanted the nation loyal in the future. So he gave them new customs, the reverse of everyone else's. What we call sacred, they call unholy. What's forbidden to us is allowed to them. In their inner shrine they dedicated a statue of the animal that showed them the way. It ended their wandering and their thirst. They also sacrifice a ram, as if to insult Hammon. They sacrifice an ox too, since the Egyptians worship Apis. They avoid pork. Why? To remember the disaster. A skin disease once disfigured them, and pigs are prone to it. Frequent fasts admit their long-ago hunger. Jewish bread has no yeast, and it's kept as proof of the grain they grabbed in a hurry. They rest on the seventh day, they say, because it ended their labors. Then, charmed by idleness, they gave the seventh year to laziness too. That's the story. Others say the honor is for Saturn. Maybe the Idaei handed down the first elements of the religion. They're said to have been driven out along with Saturn and to have founded the nation. Or maybe it's the seven stars that rule mortal lives. Saturn's star travels in the highest orbit and has the greatest power. Most heavenly bodies run their paths and cycles in multiples of seven. Sevens, all over the sky.

5:5 Whatever started these rites, their age defends them. Their other customs are twisted and foul, but they drew their strength from that very wickedness. How did Jewish wealth grow? The worst people of other nations dropped their own religions and kept piling tribute and gifts onto the temple there. It also grew because the Jews are stubbornly loyal to each other and quick to pity each other. Everyone else gets only hostile hatred. They eat apart. They sleep apart. A people extremely given to lust, they still keep away from foreign women. Among themselves, though, nothing is forbidden. They set up circumcision on purpose, so the difference would mark them out. Anyone who crosses over to their ways takes up the same practice. The first lesson for these converts: despise the gods, give up your country, and count your parents, children and brothers as worthless. Still, they look after the growth of their numbers. Killing any of their newborn children is a crime. They think the souls of men killed in battle or by execution are eternal. That's where the love of having children comes from, and the contempt for dying. Like the Egyptians, they bury their bodies instead of burning them. They give the dead the same care and hold the same belief about the underworld. About heavenly things, though, they believe the opposite. The Egyptians worship many animals and images, put together in odd shapes. The Jews understand only with the mind, and only one divine power. Anyone who makes images of God in human form out of perishable materials is profane, they say. The supreme, eternal being can't be copied and will never die. So their cities have no statues, and their temples have none either. Kings get no such flattery from them, and emperors get no such honor. Then there's the ivy. Their priests sang to flutes and drums, wreathed in ivy, and a golden vine was found in the temple. So some have decided they worship Father Liber, the conqueror of the East. That's quite a leap. Their practices don't fit it at all. Liber set up festive, joyful rites. The Jewish way is absurd and sordid.

5:6 Start with the borders. Arabia presses up against the country's eastern edge, and Egypt lies to the south. Phoenicia and the sea lie to the west, while to the north the land looks a long way out over Syria's flank. The people are healthy and tough, built for hard work. Rain is rare, but the soil is rich, and crops grow thick, like ours. Besides crops, you get balsam and palms. The palm groves are tall and gorgeous. Balsam is a modest tree, and every branch swells with sap. Try cutting one with iron? The veins shrink back. You have to open them with a chip of stone or a piece of pottery, and doctors use the sap. The chief mountain is Libanus. Strangely, it rises into shade and snow you can count on, right in the middle of all that heat. It also feeds the river Jordan and sends it on its way. But the Jordan never reaches the sea. It runs whole through one lake, then another, and gets held in the third. That lake is huge and looks like a sea, only it tastes fouler. Its heavy smell is deadly to the people living nearby. Not a spot for a picnic. No wind stirs it, and it won't take fish or the water birds that are used to water. The sluggish water holds up whatever you throw on it, like solid ground. Swimmers and non-swimmers float up alike. At a set time each year, the lake throws up bitumen, a sticky black tar. Experience taught people how to gather it, the way it teaches other skills. By nature it's a black liquid, but sprinkle vinegar on it, and it thickens and floats. The men in charge take it by hand and drag it onto the top of the boat. Then it flows in by itself, with nobody helping, and loads the boat until someone cuts it off. Bronze won't cut it. Neither will iron. It runs from blood, and from any cloth stained with the blood women lose each month. That's what the old authors say. People who know the region tell it differently. They report that the bitumen floats in heaving masses, and hands push and haul it to the shore. Then the earth's heat and the sun's force dry it out, and axes and wedges split it, like logs or stone. Nothing magic about it.

5:7 Nearby lie some plains with a grim story. People say they were once rich, home to great cities, until lightning struck and burned them down. Traces remain, they say. The ground looks scorched, and it has lost its power to grow crops. Whatever comes up there turns black and empty and fades away like ash, whether it sprouts on its own or gets planted by hand. That goes for plants that stop at grass or flower and for plants that reach their usual form. So what really wrecked the land? Here's my view. Famous cities may well have burned with fire from heaven, but I think the lake's fumes poisoned the ground. The air above it is spoiled too. That's why the crops rot, and the autumn fruit with them, since soil and sky are equally heavy there. Now for the river Belus, which runs into the Jewish Sea. Around its mouth, people gather sand and melt it into glass, with nitre mixed in. The shore is tiny. The sand never runs out.

5:8 Judea is mostly villages scattered across the land, though it has towns too. Jerusalem is the capital, and it held a temple of immense wealth. Picture it from the outside in. You pass the city and its outer defenses first. Then comes the royal palace. The temple sits last, shut away in the innermost part. Only a Jew could walk up to its doors, and everyone except priests was kept back from the threshold. Now the history. Back when the Assyrians, Medes and Persians ruled the East, the Jews were the most despised of their conquered peoples. When the Macedonians took over, King Antiochus tried to strip away the Jews' superstition and push Greek ways on them. A war with the Parthians stopped him from improving this vilest of nations, because Arsaces had revolted at that moment. Poor timing. Later the Macedonians grew weak, the Parthians were not yet grown up, and the Romans were far away. So the Jews set up kings of their own. The moody crowd drove those kings out. The kings won their rule back by force of arms, and they dared everything. They exiled citizens, tore down cities, and killed brothers, wives and parents. They did the other things kings usually do. Through it all, they fostered the superstition, since the honor of the priesthood was the foundation of their power.

5:9 Pompey (Gnaeus Pompeius) was the first Roman to crush the Jews. Victory gave him the right to enter the temple, so in he went. Afterward, people said the place was an empty seat: no image of any god, and nothing in its secret rooms. Jerusalem's walls came down, but the sanctuary stayed standing. Fast forward to our civil war, when Marcus Antonius took charge of these provinces. Pacorus, king of the Parthians, grabbed Judea. Publius Ventidius killed him and drove the Parthians back over the Euphrates. Then Gaius Sosius conquered the Jews. Augustus won his own victory and made the kingdom Antony had given Herod larger. Herod died. A man named Simon didn't wait for the emperor, and took the royal title himself. Quintilius Varus, who ran Syria then, came down on him hard. With the nation held in check, Herod's children ruled it in three parts. Under Tiberius, things were quiet. Then Caligula ordered the Jews to put his statue in the temple, and they grabbed their weapons rather than do it. Caligula's death ended that unrest. By then the kings were dead or cut down to little. So Claudius gave Judea to Roman Knights or to freedmen (former slaves). One of them was Antonius Felix, who used every kind of cruelty and lust. He held a king's power with a slave's mind. Not a great combination. Felix had married Drusilla, granddaughter of Cleopatra and Antony, which made him Antony's grandson-in-law. Claudius was Antony's grandson. Tangled family tree.

5:10 Still, the Jews' patience lasted until Gessius Florus became procurator, an official who handled money and property for the emperor. Under him, the war broke out. Cestius Gallus, the legate of Syria, tried to put it down. He fought several battles, and most went badly. Then Cestius died, either from natural causes or from sheer disgust. Nero sent Vespasian next. Vespasian had luck, a good reputation, excellent officers and a victorious army. Within two campaign seasons, he held all the flat country and every city except Jerusalem. The next year was swallowed up by civil war, so for the Jews it passed quietly. Then peace came in Italy, and the foreign worries returned. Did that calm anyone down? No. It made us angrier that the Jews alone hadn't given in. Meanwhile, it seemed smart to keep Titus with the armies, in case anything happened under the new regime. Just in case.

5:11 Now for Jerusalem itself. Titus pitched his camp before the walls, as we said, and showed off his drawn-up legions. The Jews lined up right under the walls. They were ready to push farther if they won, with a refuge waiting if they lost. Cavalry and light cohorts (groups of soldiers) charged them, and nobody clearly won. Then the enemy backed off. For the next few days they picked fights in front of the gates, until constant losses forced them behind the walls. The Romans turned to assault. Wait for the enemy to starve? That didn't seem worthy of them. The soldiers pushed for the riskier course, some out of courage and many out of savagery and greed for rewards. Titus himself kept Rome in view, with its wealth and pleasures. Unless Jerusalem fell at once, they seemed stuck. Yet the city sat high, and huge works made it tougher still, tough enough to defend even level ground. Picture two enormous hills, closed in by walls. Clever design: slanted or curved inward, they left the attackers' sides open to blows. The rock's edge dropped sheer. Where the hill gave a boost, towers climbed sixty feet. Where the ground fell away, they climbed 120 feet. They were amazing to look at. From far off, they all seemed the same height. Inside, more walls ringed the palace. Above them stuck up the tower Antonia, with its conspicuous top. Herod named it for Marcus Antonius.

5:12 Picture a fortress inside a fortress. That was the temple, and it had its own walls, and builders had poured more labor into it than into the rest of the city. Its colonnades made a strong outer shield, and a spring ran there that never went dry. Hills had been hollowed out underground, and pools and cisterns caught the rain. Why all that? The founders saw wars coming, since their customs were so different. So everything was built to outlast a long siege. Then Pompey conquered the city, and fear and hard experience taught the people most of the rest. Next came the greedy Claudian years. The people bought the right to fortify, and in peacetime they raised walls as if for war. Meanwhile their numbers swelled. A big mixed mob poured in, and the disaster of the other cities sent more. The most stubborn rebels had all run there, and the rioting inside got worse. Three commanders. Three armies. Simon held the outermost walls, the widest ones. John, also called Bargioras, held the middle city. Eleazar had fortified the temple. John and Simon were strong in numbers and weapons, while Eleazar was strong in position. Still, they spent their strength on each other: battles, treachery and fires. A great store of grain burned. Soon John sent men to Eleazar's band, pretending they had come to offer a sacrifice. Their real job was to cut down Eleazar and his men. It worked, and John took the temple. The city split into two factions. It stayed split until the Romans drew near, and a foreign war finally brought harmony. Funny how that works.

5:13 Then the strange signs began. This people loved superstition but hated religious rites. So they refused to make up for the signs with sacrifices or vows. Armies seemed to clash across the sky, and their weapons glowed red. Fire from the clouds suddenly lit the temple. The shrine doors swung open all at once. A voice louder than any human voice said the gods were leaving. At the same moment came a huge stir, like beings walking out the door. A few people took it as a reason to be afraid. Most were sure of something else. Their priests' ancient writings said that now the East would grow strong, and men setting out from Judea would rule the world. Those riddles really did predict Vespasian and Titus. But the crowd did what human ambition always does. It claimed that great destiny for itself. Not even disasters could turn them toward the truth. We're told the besieged numbered six hundred thousand, men and women of every age. Everyone who could carry a weapon had one, and more of them dared to use it than their numbers would suggest. Men and women were equally stubborn, and if they were driven out, they feared living more than dying. That was the city and the nation Titus Caesar faced. Not an easy job. The terrain ruled out a rush or any sudden strike, so he decided to fight with earthworks and covered shelters for his men. The legions split up the jobs, and the fighting paused until the work was done. Every trick for taking a city, old or new, got built first.

5:14 Civilis had just lost badly among the Treveri. Did he quit? No. He rebuilt his army out of Germany and settled at the Old Camp. He felt safe there, and remembering past successes at that spot would lift the barbarians' spirits. Cerialis followed him to the same place, and his forces doubled when the 2nd, 13th and 14th legions arrived. Cohorts and cavalry squadrons he had summoned long before also hurried up after the victory. Neither commander liked to wait, but the wide plains, wet by nature, kept them apart. Civilis made that worse. He had added a dam slanting into the Rhine, so the obstacle threw the river back and flooded the land nearby. The ground was treacherous, with fords of uncertain depth, and it worked against us. Why? Because a Roman soldier is heavy with armor and afraid of swimming. The Germans grew up on rivers, and their light weapons and tall bodies hold them up. It was like playing on the other team's home field. Except the field was a swamp.

5:15 The Batavians started it by provoking us, and our boldest men jumped in first. Then came panic, because the very deep marshes swallowed up weapons and horses. The Germans knew the shallows. They leapt about, mostly ignoring our front and circling around to hit our flanks and rear. Picture it. This was not a close infantry fight. It was more like a sea battle, with men drifting among the waters. Anyone who hit something solid had to strain there with his whole body to stay put. Wounded and unhurt, swimmers and nonswimmers, all got tangled together in the same disaster. Ouch. The slaughter was smaller than the chaos suggested, since the Germans didn't dare leave the marsh and went back to camp. Still, that fight pushed both commanders toward the decisive test of the whole war. They hurried, with very different feelings. Civilis wanted to press his luck. Cerialis wanted to wipe out the disgrace. The Germans were fierce from success. The Romans were stung by shame. The barbarians spent the night singing and shouting. Our men spent theirs in anger and threats.

5:16 Morning came. Cerialis lined up cavalry and auxiliary cohorts in front, with the legions behind them as a second line. Picked men stayed at his own side, ready for anything unexpected. Civilis didn't stretch his army into a line. He formed wedges instead, with the Batavians and Gugerni on the right. The tribes from across the Rhine held the left, nearer the river. Neither general gave one big speech. Instead each rode up to a unit and talked to it in turn. Cerialis first reminded his men how old and glorious the Roman name was, with victories from long ago and from lately. What did the enemy need? Not a battle, he said, but punishment. Treacherous, cowardly, beaten: that was the enemy, and punishment would wipe them out for good. His men had recently fought with fewer against more, and still the Germans, the muscle of that army, had been routed. Some Germans were left, with running away on their minds and wounds on their backs. Then Cerialis gave each legion its own spur. He called the 14th the Conquerors of Britain. Of the 6th, he said that Galba had been made emperor by its authority. And in this battle, he said, the men of the 2nd would be the first to dedicate their new standards and their new eagle. Next he rode to the army of Germany. He held out his hands and begged them to win back their own riverbank and their own camp, paid for with the enemy's blood. The shout that came back was louder. Some men craved battle after a long peace. The war-weary loved the thought of peace. Everyone hoped for rewards and then rest.

5:17 Civilis kept his own army from going quiet too. He pointed at the battlefield and called it a witness to their courage. "Germans and Batavians, look where you stand," he said. "This ground marks your glory. Under your feet lie the ashes and bones of legions. A Roman looks around here and sees captivity, disaster, every dreadful thing. Don't let the mixed result among the Treveri frighten you. There, the Germans' own victory got in their way. They dropped their weapons, and loot tied up their hands. But since then, everything has gone well for us and against the enemy. I have taken every precaution that a general's cunning should. We know these flooded plains well. These marshes hurt the enemy. The Rhine and the gods of Germany are in sight, so fight with their power behind you. Think of your wives, your parents, your country. Either today will be the most glorious day in our history, or it will be a disgrace in the eyes of those who come after us." The troops approved in their own way. Weapons clashed, and war dances began. Then stones, sling bullets and other missiles started flying. Our soldiers wouldn't enter the marsh. The Germans kept goading them in.

5:18 The missiles ran out. The fight got hotter. The enemy charged harder than before. They were huge men with very long spears, and they jabbed at our soldiers from a distance. Our men were thrashing around and slipping. Picture it. Meanwhile, a wedge of Bructeri swam over from the dam we told you about. It was being built out into the Rhine. Chaos. Our auxiliary infantry lines were shoved backward. Then the legions jumped in. The enemy's fury was held down, and the fight leveled out. Right in the middle of it, a Batavian deserter came to Cerialis. He offered to hit the enemy from behind if Cerialis sent cavalry along the edge of the marsh. The ground there was solid. The Gugerni had been given that post to guard, but they weren't watching closely. Sloppy. So two cavalry squadrons rode off with the deserter and swept around an enemy who never saw them coming. The shout told us what was happening. The legions attacked from the front, and the beaten Germans bolted for the Rhine. The war would have ended that day if the Roman fleet had moved fast enough to follow. Even the cavalry stopped chasing. Rain poured down all at once, and night was closing in. So close.

5:19 The next day, the army shuffled its pieces. The 14th legion marched off to the Upper Province to join Annius Gallus. The 10th legion came in from Spain to fill out Cerialis's army. Auxiliaries from the Chauci came to help Civilis. Even so, he didn't have the nerve to defend the Batavians' town by force. He grabbed what he could carry and torched the rest. Then he retreated to the island. There were no boats to make a bridge, and he knew the Roman army had no other way across. But he went further. He tore down the dam Drusus Germanicus had built and scattered whatever held it back. The Rhine came gushing out, like water draining from a tub, racing down a steep channel toward Gaul. With the river pushed off like that, only a thin channel was left between the island and Germany. Dry land all the way across, or so it looked. Tutor and Classicus crossed the Rhine too. With them went 113 Treveran senators, and Alpinius Montanus was one of them. Remember him? Primus Antonius had sent him into the Gauls. His brother Decimus Alpinius went along. At the same time, the rest scrambled to recruit auxiliaries. They begged for pity and offered gifts. They went after tribes that loved danger. Of course they did.

5:20 Plenty of war was still left. Civilis proved it in a single day. He hit four places at once, going after the garrisons of cohorts, cavalry squadrons and legions. The 10th legion at Arenacum got hit, and so did the 2nd at Batavodurum. So did the camps of the cohorts and the cavalry at Grinnes and Vada. He cut his army into pieces. Four leaders each took a band: Civilis himself, Verax (his sister's son), Classicus and Tutor. None of them was sure they could pull off everything. But with so many attempts, luck would have to show up somewhere. They also figured Cerialis wasn't careful enough. He was racing from place to place as the reports poured in, like a man running between fires. He might get cut off along the way. The men who were given the 10th legion's camp to attack decided a legion would be a tough target. So they threw a party of soldiers into chaos instead, men who had gone out to chop wood. They killed the camp prefect, five of the senior centurions, and a few soldiers. The rest fought back from behind their walls. Meanwhile, bands of Germans tried to wreck the bridge begun at Batavodurum. Night ended the fight. Nobody had won.

5:21 Grinnes and Vada were in even more trouble. Civilis went after Vada, and Classicus went after Grinnes. Nobody could stop them, because our bravest men were dead. Briganticus was one of them, a cavalry squadron commander. Remember him? We told you he was loyal to Rome and hostile to his uncle Civilis. Then Cerialis showed up with a handpicked group of cavalry, and the luck flipped. The Germans were driven headlong into the river. Civilis tried to hold back the men running away. Someone recognized him and aimed missiles at him. So he abandoned his horse and swam for it. Verax got away the same way. Light boats, pulled up to the bank, picked up Tutor and Classicus. Even then, the Roman fleet wasn't at the battle, though it had been ordered to be there. Fear got in the way, and the rowers had been spread out across other military jobs. Cerialis, honestly, gave people too little time to carry out his orders. His plans came fast and surprised everyone, but he was famous for how they turned out. Luck stayed with him even where skill had failed him. So he and his army cared less about discipline. Hold that thought. A few days later, Cerialis slipped out of the danger of being captured. Disgrace was another matter.

5:22 Here's how the disgrace happened. Cerialis went to Novesium and Bonna to inspect the camps being built for the legions' winter quarters. On the way back he sailed by ship. His column was spread out, and the lookouts were careless. The Germans noticed and set a trap. They chose a black, cloudy night. Riding the current, with nobody stopping them, they got inside the rampart. The first killing got a boost from a trick. They cut the tent ropes, and the tents came down on the soldiers like nets. Then the Germans butchered them. Another group threw the fleet into chaos. They hurled grappling hooks and yanked the ships away by their sterns. At first they stayed quiet so nobody would notice. Once the killing started, they filled the air with yelling to make it scarier. The Romans, jolted awake by their wounds, grabbed for weapons and ran through the camp's streets. A few wore military gear. Most had a cloak twisted around their arms and their swords drawn. The general was half asleep and nearly naked, and the enemy's mistake saved him. They spotted the flagship, marked by its flag, and assumed the commander was aboard. They grabbed it and hauled it off. Cerialis had spent that night somewhere else. Most people believed it was an affair with an Ubian woman named Claudia Sacrata. The sentries tried to excuse their own shameful carelessness by pointing at the general's disgrace. They claimed they had been told to keep quiet so he could rest. So the password and the calls were dropped, and then they fell asleep too. A likely story. In broad daylight, the enemy sailed back with the captured ships. They dragged the flagship, a trireme, up the river Lupia. A present for Veleda.

5:23 Civilis wanted to put on a show. He gave every ship he had two rows of oars, and every boat a single row. He threw in a huge pile of small boats too. Each was said to hold thirty or forty men, all fitted out the way Liburnian galleys (fast, narrow warships) usually were. The captured boats had little cloaks in many colors hoisted as sails. That helped, and they looked decent. Honestly, not bad at all. For his stage, he picked a stretch of water that felt like open sea, where the mouth of the Mosa pours the Rhine into the Ocean. His people were naturally vain, sure. But the main reason was to scare off the supply convoys coming from Gaul and catch them. Cerialis was more amazed than afraid, and he steered his fleet out to meet them. He had fewer ships. But his rowers were better trained, his pilots were more skilled, and his ships were bigger. The Romans had the current behind them. The others had the wind. They sailed past each other, traded a short volley of light missiles, and broke off. Big show, small fight. Civilis didn't dare go any further, so he pulled back across the Rhine. Cerialis hit the Batavians' island as an enemy, raiding and wrecking it. But he left Civilis's fields and farmhouses alone, a trick commanders know well. Meanwhile, autumn turned, and the rains around the equinox came down hard. The river spilled over and flooded the low, marshy island until it looked like a lake. No fleet. No supplies. The Roman camp sat on flat ground, and the river was carrying it away.

5:24 Civilis said the legions could have been wiped out right then. The Germans wanted it, he claimed. But he turned them away with a trick. Sound far off? Not really, since a surrender came only a few days later. Meanwhile Cerialis was busy behind the scenes, sending secret messages to the other side. He offered the Batavians peace and Civilis a pardon. He urged Veleda and her relatives to turn the tide of the war. So many disasters had gone against them, and now was the time for a well-timed favor to the Roman people. Then he reminded them of the record. The Treveri had been cut down, the Ubians taken back in, and the Batavians stripped of their homeland. And what had Civilis's friendship gotten them? Wounds, running for their lives, and grief. A banished wanderer would only weigh down anyone who took him in. They had done plenty of wrong already by crossing the Rhine so many times. Keep going, and the injury and the guilt would be theirs. Revenge and the gods would be on Rome's side.

5:25 Threats and promises were handed out together, side by side. The loyalty of the tribes across the Rhine was wobbling, and soon the Batavians were talking too. "We can't put off our ruin forever," they said. "One nation can't hold back slavery for the whole world. Look at what killing legions and torching camps got us: more legions. Bigger ones. All sent after us. If we fight for Vespasian, Vespasian gets the power. But if we take on the Roman people themselves? How small a slice of humanity the Batavians are! Look at the Raetians and Noricans, and at the heavy load our other allies carry. We pay no tribute. We hand over courage and men. That's one step from freedom. And if we must have masters, we'd rather put up with Rome's emperors than with German women." That's what the common people said. The nobles? They hit harder, and they were blunter about the cause. "Civilis's rage shoved us into war," they said. "He traded the ruin of our whole nation for his own troubles at home. The gods turned on the Batavians when the legions were besieged and the legates were killed. One man needed that war, and it would kill us. We're at the very edge of the cliff. 5:26 Civilis saw the mood shifting, and he moved first. He was worn down by his troubles. He was also clinging to the hope of staying alive, the kind of hope that bends even great spirits. So he asked for a meeting. The bridge over the Nabalia River was cut, and the two commanders stepped out to its broken ends. Picture it. Civilis opened: "If I were on trial before a legate of Vitellius, I wouldn't get forgiveness for what I did. My words wouldn't be believed. Everything between us was hostile. Vitellius started it, and I made it worse. Enemies, basically. Vespasian is different. I've respected him for a long time, and when he was only a private citizen, we were called friends. Primus Antonius knows this. His letters pushed me into the war, so that the German legions and the young men of Gaul wouldn't cross the Alps. What Antonius wrote, Hordeonius Flaccus said to my face.
