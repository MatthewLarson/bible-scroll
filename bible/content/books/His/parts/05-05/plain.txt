## Titus Marches on Jerusalem
5:1 Early in that same year, Titus Caesar, chosen by his father to finish conquering Judea, was already a well-known soldier from the days when both of them were private citizens, and now he carried more power and a bigger reputation, as the provinces and the armies competed to show how much they were behind him. He himself, eager to be thought bigger than his position, kept showing himself dignified and ready for action. With his friendly manner and the way he talked to people, he got their best effort out of them. He often mixed in with the ordinary soldiers at work and on the march, without losing the respect due a general. In Judea three legions met him: the 5th, the 10th and the 15th, Vespasian's veteran troops. He added the 12th from Syria, plus men of the 22nd and 3rd, whom he'd brought from Alexandria. With him came twenty allied cohorts and eight cavalry squadrons, along with the two kings, Agrippa and Sohemus, the auxiliaries of King Antiochus, and a strong force of Arabs, who hated the Jews with the hatred neighbors usually have. Many others came too, people from Rome and from Italy, each drawn by his own hope of winning Titus's favor before anyone else had. With this army he crossed into enemy territory, keeping his column in good order, scouting every spot, and ready to fight at any moment. He made camp not far from Jerusalem.
## Where Did the Jews Come From? Five Stories
5:2 Since I'm about to tell the last day of a famous city, it makes sense to lay out its beginnings first. Some say the Jews were refugees from the island of Crete who settled in the farthest part of Libya, around the time Saturn was driven out of his kingdom by Jupiter's force. The proof is sought in the name. There's a famous mountain in Crete called Ida, and the neighbors there, the Idaei, supposedly got the longer, foreign-sounding name Judaei. Some say that in the reign of Isis, Egypt's overflowing crowds, led by Hierosolymus and Judas, were emptied out into the nearby lands. Many say they came from the Ethiopians, who were driven to move by fear and hatred of their neighbors in the time of King Cepheus. Others tell us they were Assyrian newcomers, a people short of land, who took over part of Egypt, and soon lived in cities of their own, in the Hebrew country and the lands closer to Syria. Others give the Jews a famous beginning: the Solymi, a nation celebrated in Homer's poems, who gave the city they founded the name Hierosolyma, taken from their own.
## Moyses and the Flight from Egypt
5:3 Most writers agree that a disease broke out across Egypt that horribly disfigured the body. King Bocchoris went to the oracle of Hammon looking for a cure, and was told to purify his kingdom and carry this kind of people, hated by the gods, off to other lands. So the crowd was hunted down and rounded up, and then left in empty country. While everyone else sat numb and in tears, one of the exiles, Moyses, warned them not to expect help from gods or from men, since both had abandoned them. They should trust themselves, he said, and take as their heaven-sent leader whoever first got them out of their present misery. They agreed, and knowing nothing about the way, they set out at random. But nothing wore them down like the lack of water, and they were lying all over the plains, close to death, when a herd of wild asses left their grazing and went up to a rock shaded by trees. Moyses followed them, guessed from the grassy ground, and opened up plenty of veins of water. That was relief. They went on a nonstop journey of six days, and on the seventh they drove out the people living there and took the land, where a city and a temple were dedicated.
## Customs and the Reasons Given for Them
5:4 Moyses, to make sure of the nation's loyalty in the future, gave them new rites, the opposite of what everyone else does. Everything that is sacred with us is unholy there, and what's forbidden to us is allowed to them. In their inner shrine they dedicated a statue of the animal that showed them the way and ended their wandering and thirst. They sacrifice a ram, as if to insult Hammon, and they sacrifice an ox too, since the Egyptians worship Apis. They avoid pork in memory of the disaster, because a skin disease once disfigured them, and pigs are prone to it. They admit their long-ago hunger by frequent fasts, and Jewish bread, made with no yeast, is kept as proof of the grain they grabbed in a hurry. They say they chose to rest on the seventh day because it ended their labors; then, charmed by idleness, they gave the seventh year to laziness too. Others say this honor is for Saturn, either because the Idaei, who we're told were driven out along with Saturn and became the founders of the nation, handed down the first elements of the religion, or because, of the seven stars that rule mortal lives, Saturn's star travels in the highest orbit and has the greatest power, and most of the heavenly bodies run their paths and cycles in multiples of seven.
## Beliefs, Burial and the Worship of One God
5:5 However these rites were introduced, they're defended by their age. Their other customs are twisted and foul, and they got their strength from that very wickedness. The worst people of other nations dropped their own religions and kept piling up tribute and gifts there, and that's how the Jews' wealth grew. It also grew because among themselves their loyalty is stubborn and their pity is quick, but toward everyone else they feel hostile hatred. They eat separately and sleep separately, and though they're a people extremely given to lust, they keep away from foreign women; among themselves nothing is forbidden. They set up circumcision so that they'd be recognized by the difference. Those who cross over to their ways take up the same practice, and the first thing they're taught is to despise the gods, give up their country, and treat parents, children and brothers as worthless. Still, they look after the growth of their numbers: it's a crime to kill any of their newborn children, and they think the souls of those killed in battle or by execution are eternal. From that come the love of having children and the contempt for dying. They bury their bodies rather than burning them, following the Egyptian custom, with the same care for the dead and the same belief about the underworld, but the opposite belief about heavenly things. The Egyptians worship many animals and images put together in odd shapes, while the Jews understand only with the mind, and one divine power. They call people profane who make images of God in human form out of perishable materials. They hold that the supreme and eternal being can't be copied and will never die. So they put up no statues in their cities, let alone in their temples. They don't pay this flattery to their kings, or this honor to the emperors. But because their priests sang to flutes and drums, were wreathed in ivy, and a golden vine was found in the temple, some have decided that they worship Father Liber, the conqueror of the East, though their practices don't fit that at all. Liber set up festive and joyful rites, while the Jewish way is absurd and sordid.
## The Land, the Jordan and the Bitter Lake
5:6 The country's eastern edge runs up against Arabia, Egypt lies to the south, Phoenicia and the sea are to the west, and to the north it looks out a long way over the side of Syria. The people are healthy and can take hard work. Rain is rare, and the soil is rich. The crops are plentiful, like ours, and besides them there's balsam and palms. The palm groves are tall and handsome. Balsam is a modest tree: each branch swells with sap, and if you use iron, the veins shrink back; they're opened with a chip of stone or a piece of pottery. The sap is used by doctors. The chief mountain is Libanus, which rises, strange to say, with shade and dependable snow in the middle of such heat. It also feeds the river Jordan and sends it on its way. The Jordan doesn't empty into the sea; it flows whole through one lake and another and is held in the third. That lake is huge, looks like a sea, and tastes fouler, and its heavy smell is deadly to the people living near it. Wind doesn't stir it, and it won't take fish or the water birds that are used to water. The sluggish water holds up whatever is thrown on it as if it were solid ground, and swimmers and non-swimmers are lifted up alike. At one season of the year it throws up bitumen, and experience, as with other skills, has taught people how to gather it. By its own nature it's a black liquid, and when vinegar is sprinkled on it, it thickens and floats. The people in charge of this take it by hand and pull it onto the top of the boat. Then, with nobody helping, it flows in and loads the boat until you cut it off. And you can't cut it with bronze or iron. It runs from blood, and from any cloth stained with the blood by which women are relieved each month. That's what the old authors say. But people who know the region report that the bitumen floats in heaving masses, is pushed and hauled by hand to the shore, and then, when it dries from the earth's heat and the sun's force, is split with axes and wedges like logs or stone.
## The Burned Plain and the Glass Sand
5:7 Not far from there are plains that they say were once rich and home to great cities, until lightning struck and burned them. They say traces of it remain, and that the ground itself, scorched in appearance, has lost its power to grow crops. Everything that comes up by itself or is planted by hand, whether it grows only to the grass or flower stage or reaches its usual form, turns black and empty and fades away like ash. For my part, I'd grant that famous cities may once have burned with fire from heaven, but I think the ground is poisoned by the lake's fumes, the air above it spoiled, and that this is why the crops and the autumn fruit rot, since soil and sky are equally heavy there. The river Belus also runs into the Jewish Sea. Around its mouth, sand is gathered and melted into glass with nitre mixed in. The shore is small, but it never runs out of sand for the people who carry it off.
## Jerusalem, Its Temple and Its Rulers
5:8 A large part of Judea is scattered in villages. They have towns too. Jerusalem is the nation's capital. There stood a temple of immense wealth. First came the city with its first defenses, then the royal palace, then the temple, shut away in the innermost part. Only a Jew could come up to its doors, and everyone except priests was kept back from the threshold. While the East belonged to the Assyrians, the Medes and the Persians, the Jews were the most despised of the subject peoples. After the Macedonians became dominant, King Antiochus tried to strip away their superstition and give them Greek ways, but the war with the Parthians kept him from changing this vilest of nations for the better, because Arsaces had revolted at that time. Then, with the Macedonians weak, the Parthians not yet grown up (and the Romans far away), the Jews set up kings for themselves. These kings were driven out by the fickle crowd, won back their rule by force of arms, and dared everything: exiling citizens, tearing down cities, killing brothers, wives and parents, and the other things kings usually do. They fostered the superstition, because they took the honor of the priesthood as the foundation of their power.
## Rome and Judea, from Pompey to Felix
5:9 Gnaeus Pompeius (Pompey) was the first Roman to conquer the Jews, and by right of victory he went into the temple. From that came the word that the place was an empty seat with no image of any god inside, and that its secret rooms held nothing. The walls of Jerusalem were torn down, but the sanctuary stood. Later, in our civil war, after these provinces came under Marcus Antonius, Pacorus, king of the Parthians, took Judea. He was killed by Publius Ventidius, and the Parthians were driven back across the Euphrates. Gaius Sosius conquered the Jews. The kingdom Antony had given Herod, Augustus enlarged after his victory. After Herod's death, a man named Simon, without waiting for the emperor, seized the royal title. Quintilius Varus, who held Syria at the time, punished him, and the nation, held in check, was ruled in three parts by Herod's children. Under Tiberius it was quiet. Then, when they were ordered by Caligula to put his statue in the temple, they took up arms rather than do it. Caligula's death broke up that unrest. Claudius, with the kings dead or cut down to little, handed the province of Judea to Roman Knights or freedmen (former slaves). One of them, Antonius Felix, using every kind of cruelty and lust, exercised a king's power with a slave's mind. He had married Drusilla, granddaughter of Cleopatra and Antony, so Felix was Antony's grandson-in-law, while Claudius was Antony's grandson.
## The War Comes Back to Jerusalem
5:10 Still, the Jews' patience lasted until Gessius Florus was procurator, and the war broke out under him. When Cestius Gallus, the legate of Syria, tried to put it down, he had several battles, mostly going badly. When he died, either from natural causes or from sheer disgust, Nero sent Vespasian. With his luck, his reputation and his excellent officers, Vespasian held, within two campaign seasons and with his victorious army, all the flat country and every city except Jerusalem. The next year was taken up with civil war, and as far as the Jews were concerned it passed quietly. When peace was won in Italy, foreign worries came back. It made us angrier that the Jews alone hadn't given in. At the same time, it seemed useful for Titus to stay with the armies, in case anything happened under the new regime.
## First Fights and the Walls of the City
5:11 So, with his camp set up in front of the walls of Jerusalem, as we said, Titus put his drawn-up legions on display. The Jews formed their line right under the walls, ready to venture farther if they were winning and with a refuge prepared if they were pushed back. Cavalry sent against them with light cohorts fought it out with no clear result. Then the enemy gave way, and on the following days they kept starting fights in front of the gates, until their steady losses drove them inside the walls. The Romans turned to assault. It didn't seem worthy of them to wait for the enemy to starve. The soldiers demanded the riskier course, some out of courage, many out of savagery and greed for rewards. Titus himself had Rome, with its wealth and pleasures, before his eyes, and unless Jerusalem fell at once, they seemed to be held back. But the city stood high, and huge works made it stronger still, strong enough to defend even level ground. Two hills of enormous height were closed in by walls that were cleverly slanted or curved inward, so that the sides of the attackers lay open to blows. The edge of the rock was a sheer drop, and the towers rose to sixty feet where the hill helped, and to 120 feet where the ground dropped away. They were amazing to look at, and to people looking from far off they all seemed the same height. Inside were other walls around the palace, and, with a conspicuous top, the tower Antonia, named by Herod in honor of Marcus Antonius.
## The Temple and the Three Warlords
5:12 The temple was like a citadel, with its own walls, built with more labor and skill than the rest. The very colonnades around the temple were an excellent outer defense. There was a spring that never ran dry, hills hollowed out underground, and pools and cisterns for saving rain. The founders had foreseen frequent wars from the difference in their customs, and so everything was built to hold out even through a long siege. After Pompey's conquest, fear and experience taught them most of the rest. Because of the greed of the Claudian years, they bought the right to fortify, and in peacetime they built walls as if for war. Their numbers were swelled by a big mixed mob and by the disaster of the other cities, since all the most stubborn rebels had run there, and that made the rioting inside worse. There were three commanders and three armies. Simon held the outermost and widest walls, John, whom they also called Bargioras, held the middle city, and Eleazar had fortified the temple. John and Simon were strong in numbers and weapons, Eleazar in position. But among them there were battles, treachery and fires, and a great store of grain was burned. Soon John sent men under cover of offering a sacrifice, to cut down Eleazar and his band, and took the temple. So the city split into two factions, until the Romans' approach brought harmony out of a foreign war.
## Signs from Heaven and the Start of the Siege
5:13 Omens had appeared, which this people, prone to superstition but hostile to religious rites, didn't think it right to make up for with sacrifices or vows. Armies were seen clashing across the sky, weapons glowing red, and the temple suddenly lit by fire from the clouds. The doors of the shrine swung open all at once, and a voice louder than any human voice was heard saying the gods were leaving, and at the same time there was a huge stir, as of beings going out. A few took these things as reason to be afraid. Most were convinced that the ancient writings of their priests said that at this very time the East would grow strong and men setting out from Judea would rule the world. Those riddles had really predicted Vespasian and Titus, but the crowd, in the usual way of human ambition, took such a great destiny for themselves, and not even disasters could change their minds to the truth. We've been told that the besieged, of every age, men and women, numbered six hundred thousand. Everyone who could carry weapons had them, and more dared to use them than their numbers would suggest. The stubbornness of men and women was equal, and if they were forced to move away, they feared life more than death. That was the city and the nation Titus Caesar faced. Since the terrain ruled out a rush or any sudden stroke of war, he decided to fight with earthworks and covered shelters for his men. Jobs were divided among the legions, and there was a pause in the fighting, until every device invented for taking cities, by people of old or by recent ingenuity, had been built.
## Civilis and Cerialis at the Old Camp
5:14 Meanwhile Civilis, after the bad fight among the Treveri, had rebuilt his army out of Germany and settled at the Old Camp. He was safe in that place, and the memory of past successes there would build up the barbarians' spirits. Cerialis followed to the same place, his forces doubled by the arrival of the 2nd, 13th and 14th legions. Cohorts and cavalry squadrons, summoned long before, had hurried to him after the victory. Neither commander was a staller, but the breadth of the plains, naturally wet, kept them apart. Civilis had also added a dam slanting into the Rhine, so that the river, thrown back by the obstacle, flooded the nearby land. The lay of the land was treacherous, with fords of uncertain depth, and it worked against us. The Roman soldier is heavy with armor and afraid of swimming, while the Germans, used to rivers, are held up by their light weapons and tall bodies.
## The Battle in the Marsh
5:15 So when the Batavians started provoking us, the fight began with the boldest of our men. Then panic rose when weapons and horses were swallowed in the very deep marshes. The Germans, who knew the shallows, leapt about, usually ignoring the front and circling around to our flanks and rear. It wasn't fought at close quarters like an infantry battle, but more like a naval battle, with men drifting among the waters or, if they hit anything solid, straining there with their whole bodies. The wounded and the unhurt, those who could swim and those who couldn't, were tangled together in common destruction. The slaughter was smaller than the chaos would suggest, because the Germans didn't dare leave the marsh and went back to camp. The outcome of that fight pushed both commanders, with different feelings, to hurry toward the decisive test of the whole war. Civilis wanted to press his luck, Cerialis to wipe out the disgrace. The Germans were fierce from success, and shame had stirred up the Romans. The night among the barbarians was spent singing and shouting; among our men, in anger and threats.
## The Second Day: Two Speeches
5:16 The next morning Cerialis filled his front line with cavalry and auxiliary cohorts, with the legions in the second line, and he kept picked men with himself for anything unexpected. Civilis didn't stretch his army out in a line; he stood in wedges, with the Batavians and Gugerni on the right, and the tribes from across the Rhine holding the left, closer to the river. The commanders' pep talks weren't a formal address to everyone, but were given to each unit as they rode up to it. Cerialis spoke of the old glory of the Roman name and its victories, old and recent. He said that to destroy forever this treacherous, cowardly, beaten enemy, what was needed was punishment more than battle. They had recently fought with fewer men against more, and still the Germans, the strength of that army, had been routed. Some were left who carried flight in their hearts and wounds on their backs. Then he gave each legion its own spur. He called the 14th the Conquerors of Britain, said Galba had been made emperor by the authority of the 6th legion, and said that in this battle the men of the 2nd would be the first to dedicate their new standards and their new eagle. Then he rode over to the army of Germany and held out his hands, begging them to win back their own bank of the river and their own camp with the enemy's blood. A louder shout came from everyone. Some had a craving for battle after a long peace, and those tired of war loved the thought of peace, and they all hoped for rewards and rest to come.
5:17 Civilis didn't line up his army in silence either. He called on the place of the fight to witness their courage. "You Germans and Batavians are standing on the marks of your own glory," he said, "trampling the ashes and bones of legions. Wherever a Roman turns his eyes, he sees captivity, disaster and every dreadful thing. Don't let the mixed outcome of the battle among the Treveri frighten you. There, their own victory got in the Germans' way, because they dropped their weapons and tied up their hands with loot. But everything since has gone well for us and against the enemy. I've provided every precaution that a general's cunning should provide: these flooded plains that we know so well, and these marshes that hurt the enemy. The Rhine and the gods of Germany are in sight. With their power behind you, take up the fight, remembering your wives, your parents and your country. Today will either be the most glorious day in our history or a disgrace in the eyes of those who come after us." The troops approved these words in their own way, with clashing weapons and war dances, and then the fight began with stones, sling bullets and other missiles. Our soldiers wouldn't go into the marsh, and the Germans were trying to provoke them into it.
## The Fight Turns
5:18 When the missiles were used up and the fight heated, the enemy rushed forward more fiercely. With their huge bodies and very long spears, they stabbed from a distance at our soldiers, who were floundering and slipping. At the same time, from the dam we described being built out into the Rhine, a wedge of Bructeri swam across. That threw things into confusion, and the line of auxiliary infantry was being pushed back, when the legions took up the fight. The enemy's ferocity was held down, and the battle evened out. In the middle of this, a Batavian deserter came to Cerialis, promising the enemy's rear if cavalry were sent along the edge of the marsh. The ground there was firm, and the Gugerni, who had been given that post to guard, weren't watching closely. Two cavalry squadrons sent with the deserter swept around the unsuspecting enemy. When we knew from the shout, the legions pressed in from the front, and the beaten Germans ran for the Rhine. The war would have been finished that day if the Roman fleet had hurried to follow. Even the cavalry didn't press on, since the rain suddenly poured down and night was near.
## Civilis Falls Back to the Island
5:19 The next day the 14th legion was sent to the Upper Province to join Annius Gallus, and the 10th legion, from Spain, filled out Cerialis's army. Auxiliaries from the Chauci came to Civilis. Even so, he didn't dare defend the Batavians' town with arms. He took what could be carried, set fire to the rest, and withdrew to the island, knowing there were no boats to make a bridge and that the Roman army couldn't cross any other way. He even tore down the dam Drusus Germanicus had built, and, scattering what held it back, let the Rhine pour out, rushing down a steep channel toward Gaul. With the river driven off like that, a thin channel was left between the island and Germany, which made it look like dry land all the way across. Tutor and Classicus crossed the Rhine too, with one hundred and thirteen Treveran senators, among them Alpinius Montanus, whom we mentioned earlier as sent into the Gauls by Primus Antonius. His brother Decimus Alpinius went with him. At the same time the rest tried to raise auxiliaries, appealing to their pity and offering gifts, among tribes that loved danger.
## Four Attacks in One Day
5:20 So much of the war was left that Civilis, in a single day, attacked in four places the garrisons of cohorts, cavalry squadrons and legions. He went for the 10th legion at Arenacum, the 2nd at Batavodurum, and the camps of the cohorts and cavalry at Grinnes and Vada. He split his forces so that he himself, Verax (his sister's son), Classicus and Tutor each led a band of his own. They weren't confident of getting everything done, but they figured that with so many attempts, luck would favor them somewhere. They also figured that Cerialis wasn't careful enough, and, racing from place to place as the reports poured in, might be cut off along the way. The men who drew the camp of the 10th thought attacking a legion would be hard, so they threw into confusion a party of soldiers who had gone out to cut timber. They killed the camp prefect, five of the senior centurions and a few soldiers, and the rest defended themselves behind their fortifications. In the meantime, bands of Germans tried to break up the bridge begun at Batavodurum. Night ended a fight that hadn't been decided.
5:21 There was more danger at Grinnes and Vada. Civilis attacked Vada and Classicus attacked Grinnes, and they couldn't be stopped, since all our bravest men had been killed, among them Briganticus, commander of a cavalry squadron, whom we said was loyal to the Romans and hostile to his uncle Civilis. But when Cerialis came to help with a picked band of cavalry, the luck turned. The Germans were driven headlong into the river. Civilis, while trying to hold back the fleeing men, was recognized and targeted by missiles. He left his horse and swam across. Verax escaped the same way. Light boats, brought up to the bank, carried off Tutor and Classicus. Not even then was the Roman fleet at the battle, though it had been ordered to be there. Fear stood in the way, and the rowers had been scattered among other military jobs. Cerialis in truth gave people too little time to carry out his orders. His plans were sudden, but he was famous for how they turned out. Luck was with him even where skill had failed, and for that reason both he and his army paid less attention to discipline. And a few days later, though he escaped the danger of being captured, he didn't escape disgrace.
## The Night Raid on Cerialis's Camp
5:22 He set out for Novesium and Bonna to look over the camps being built for the legions' winter quarters, and was heading back by ship with his column strung out and the watches careless. The Germans noticed this and laid a trap. They picked a night black with clouds, and, carried down the current with nobody to stop them, they entered the rampart. The first killing was helped along by a trick: they cut the ropes of the tents and slaughtered the soldiers, buried under their own tents. Another band threw the fleet into confusion, threw on grappling hooks, and dragged the ships away by their sterns. They had kept silent at first to avoid being noticed, but once the killing began, they filled the air with shouting to add to the terror. The Romans, woken by their wounds, looked for weapons and ran through the camp streets, a few in military gear, most with a cloak twisted around their arms and swords drawn. The general, half asleep and nearly naked, was saved by the enemy's mistake, because they took the flagship, marked by its flag, thinking the commander was there, and carried it off. Cerialis had spent the night somewhere else, in an affair, most people believed, with an Ubian woman, Claudia Sacrata. The sentries excused their own shameful neglect by pointing at the general's disgrace. They said they'd been ordered to keep quiet so as not to disturb his rest, so the watchword and the calls had been dropped, and they too had fallen asleep. In broad daylight the enemy sailed back with the captured ships, and towed the flagship, a trireme, up the river Lupia as a gift for Veleda.
## The Fleets and the Flooded Island
5:23 Civilis got an urge to put on a show of naval strength. He filled every ship he had with two banks of oars, and all the boats with a single row of oars, and added a huge number of small boats, said to hold thirty or forty men each, the usual crew of a Liburnian galley. The captured boats, with small cloaks of many colors for sails, were helped along, and it looked not bad at all. For his stage he picked a stretch like open sea, where the mouth of the river Mosa pours the Rhine into the Ocean. The reason for lining up the fleet, besides his people's natural vanity, was to scare off and intercept the supply convoys coming from Gaul. Cerialis, more amazed than afraid, steered his fleet out. He had fewer ships, but he had the better of it in the practice of his rowers, the skill of his pilots and the size of his ships. The Romans had the current behind them, and the others had the wind. They sailed past each other, tried a short exchange of light missiles, and broke off. Civilis dared nothing more and withdrew across the Rhine. Cerialis laid waste the Batavians' island as an enemy, but left Civilis's fields and farmhouses untouched, a trick well known among commanders. Meanwhile, as autumn turned and the rains around the equinox came heavy, the river overflowed and filled the marshy, low-lying island until it looked like a lake. There was neither fleet nor supplies, and the Roman camp, set on flat ground, was being carried off by the force of the river.
## Cerialis Offers Terms
5:24 Civilis claimed that the legions could have been crushed then and that the Germans wanted to do it, but that he had turned them aside by trickery. That doesn't sound far from the truth, since a surrender followed a few days later. For Cerialis, through secret messengers, held out peace to the Batavians and pardon to Civilis, and urged Veleda and her relatives to change the fortunes of war, which so many disasters had turned against them, by a well-timed service to the Roman people. He reminded them that the Treveri had been cut down, the Ubians taken back in, and the Batavians stripped of their homeland, and that Civilis's friendship had gotten them nothing but wounds, flight and grief. An exile and a wanderer would be a burden to those who took him in, and they had done enough wrong already by crossing the Rhine so many times. If they tried anything more, the injury and the guilt would be on their side, and on the other side would be vengeance and the gods.
## The Batavians Waver
5:25 The threats were mixed with promises. With the loyalty of the tribes across the Rhine shaken, talk started among the Batavians too. "We can't put off our ruin any longer," they said. "One nation can't push back the slavery of the whole world. What did we get from killing legions and burning camps, except that more and stronger ones were called up? If we went to war for Vespasian, then Vespasian is in power. But if we're calling out the Roman people to fight, what a tiny part of the human race the Batavians are! Look at the Raetians and Noricans, and at the burdens of our other allies. We aren't ordered to pay tribute, only to supply courage and men. That's the next thing to freedom. And if we have to choose masters, it's more honorable to put up with the emperors of the Romans than with the women of the Germans." That's what the common people said. The nobles spoke more harshly. "Civilis's rage drove us into arms," they said. "He set the destruction of our nation against his own troubles at home. The gods were angry at the Batavians back when the legions were besieged and the legates killed, when a war was started that one man needed and that was deadly to us. We've come to the last extremity unless we start to come to our senses and prove our repentance by punishing the guilty one."
## Civilis Asks to Talk
5:26 Civilis didn't miss this change of mood among the Batavians, and he decided to get ahead of it. He was worn out by his troubles, and also by the hope of staying alive, which so often bends great spirits. He asked for a talk. They cut the bridge over the river Nabalia, and the two commanders walked out to the broken ends. Civilis began: "If I were defending myself before a legate of Vitellius, my action would deserve no pardon and my words no belief. Everything between us was hostile. He started the enmity and I made it worse. Toward Vespasian I've had a long-standing respect, and when he was a private citizen we were called friends. Primus Antonius knew this, and his letters pushed me into the war, so that the legions in Germany and the young men of Gaul wouldn't cross the Alps. What Antonius urged in his letters, Hordeonius Flaccus urged to my face. I took up arms in Germany, just as Mucianus did in Syria, Aponius in Moesia and Flavianus in Pannonia
