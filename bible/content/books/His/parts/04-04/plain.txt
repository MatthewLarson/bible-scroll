## Rome after the victory: the soldiers hunt the defeated; Domitian and Lucius Vitellius
4:1 After Vitellius died, the war was over, but peace hadn't started yet. Sword in hand, the winners hunted down the losers all over the city with merciless hatred. The streets were choked with bodies, and the squares and temples reeked of blood, because men were cut down wherever chance put them in the way. Soon, as the soldiers grew bolder, they began to search out hidden enemies and drag them into the open. Whenever they saw a tall young man, they killed him, making no difference between soldiers and civilians. At first their fury could only be satisfied with blood, but it soon turned into greed for gain. They let nothing stay hidden and nothing stay shut, since Vitellianists might be hiding there, they claimed. That was how the home break-ins started, and if anyone resisted, they had their excuse to kill. The poorest of the people and the most worthless of the slaves came forward to betray their rich masters, and other men were turned in by their friends. Everywhere there was crying and wailing and all the misery of a captured city, until people looked back with regret even on the wild behavior of the Vitellianist and Othonianist soldiers, which had once seemed so hateful. The party leaders had worked hard to kindle the civil war, but they couldn't stop their troops from abusing the victory. Bad men are at their best stirring up trouble and violence, but peace and quiet can't be built without good character.
4:2 Domitian had taken the name of Caesar and moved into the Caesars' residence, but he hadn't turned to the work of ruling yet. He played the emperor's son with sexual misconduct and adultery instead. Arrius Varus held the command of the Praetorian Guard, but the real power was in the hands of Primus Antonius, who carried off money and slaves from the emperor's household as if they were the spoils of Cremona. The other generals, whose modesty or obscurity had kept them out of sight in the war, got none of the rewards either. The country was terrified and ready to accept servitude. It kept demanding that Lucius Vitellius and his cohorts be cut off as they came back from Tarracina, so the last sparks of the war could be stamped out. Cavalry was sent ahead to Aricia, and the main body of the legions halted this side of Bovillae. Vitellius didn't hesitate. He handed himself and his cohorts over to the mercy of the winner, and the soldiers threw down their unlucky weapons, as angry as they were afraid. A long line of prisoners, hemmed in by armed guards, marched through the city. Not one of them looked like a man begging for mercy. They were grim and fierce, and they stood unmoved by the cheers and the jeering of the insulting crowd. A few who dared to break away were pushed back by the men around them, and the rest were locked up. Not one of them said anything unworthy, and even in defeat their reputation for courage stayed whole. Then Lucius Vitellius was executed. He was as rotten as his brother, though he'd been sharper while his brother ruled. You could say he didn't so much share his brother's rise as get dragged down by his fall.
## The Senate honors Vespasian
4:3 About the same time Lucilius Bassus was sent with some light cavalry to bring order to Campania, where the towns were still unsettled because of rivalries among themselves, not because of defiance of the new emperor. The sight of soldiers brought quiet, and the smaller colonies went unpunished. At Capua the 3rd legion was stationed for the winter, and the noble families there suffered badly, while Tarracina got no help at all. It's much easier to repay an injury than a kindness, because gratitude feels like a burden and revenge looks like a profit. The people of Tarracina found some comfort in seeing the slave of Vergilius Capito, the man I've already named as the one who betrayed Tarracina, hung on a gallows still wearing the Roman Knight's rings Vitellius had given him. At Rome the Senate, delighted and full of confident hope, gave Vespasian all the honors usually given to emperors. The civil war had begun in Gaul and Spain, then drawn in Germany and then Illyricum, and had swept through Egypt, Judea, Syria and every province and army. Now, with the whole world purged of guilt, it seemed to have reached its end. The Senate's eagerness grew when a letter from Vespasian arrived, written as if the war were still going on. At first glance it looked like that kind of letter. But he spoke like an emperor: modest about himself, and excellent about the state. The Senate didn't fall short in respect. Vespasian was given the consulship along with his son Titus, and Domitian was given the office of praetor with a consul's authority.
4:4 Mucianus had also sent some letters to the Senate, and they gave people something to talk about. They said: "If he's a private citizen, why does he talk like a public official? In a few days he could say all this from his own seat in the Senate. His attack on Vitellius comes too late, and it's ungenerous. And it's arrogant toward the state, and an insult to the emperor, to boast that he held the imperial power in his hands and made a present of it to Vespasian." They hid their dislike of Mucianus, but their flattery was out in the open. In glowing language they voted him a triumph, a triumph for a civil war, though the excuse was an expedition against the Sarmatae. Primus Antonius received the insignia of a consul, and Arrius Varus and Cornelius Fuscus the honors of praetors. Then they remembered the gods and decided that the Capitol should be rebuilt. Valerius Asiaticus, the consul-elect, proposed all of this. Most of the senators showed their agreement by their looks or by raising a hand. A few, men of high rank or with a practiced talent for flattery, put it into prepared speeches. When it was the turn of Helvidius Priscus, the praetor-elect, he gave an opinion that was full of respect for a worthy emperor and yet free of falsehood, and the Senate's sympathy was strongly with him. For Priscus, this day was the beginning of a great quarrel and of great fame.
## Helvidius Priscus and the quarrel over the envoys
4:5 Since I've mentioned again a man I'll have to speak of often, it seems right to give a short account of his life, his pursuits and his fortunes. Helvidius Priscus came from the town of Cluviae, in the Carecina region of Italy. His father had been a top-ranking centurion. From his early youth he turned his great talents to the highest pursuits. He didn't do it like the many who hide a lazy life behind an impressive name, but so he could enter public life with a mind ready for whatever fortune brought. He followed those teachers of philosophy who hold that nothing is good but what is honorable and nothing is bad but what is shameful, and who wouldn't call power, rank or anything outside the mind good or bad. While he was still a quaestor, Paetus Thrasea picked him to be his son-in-law, and from his father-in-law's example he took up the love of liberty with special eagerness. As a citizen and a senator, as a husband, a son-in-law and a friend, and in every part of life, he was always the same. He looked down on wealth, held firmly to what was right, and wasn't shaken by danger.
4:6 Some people thought he was too eager for fame, and the desire for glory is in fact the last thing even wise people shake off. After his father-in-law's downfall, he was driven into exile. When Galba became emperor he came back and moved to prosecute Marcellus Eprius, who had been the informer against Thrasea. It's hard to say whether this payback was bigger or more just. It split the Senate into factions, because if Marcellus fell, a whole army of guilty men would go down with him. At first there was a threatening struggle, as the fine speeches of both men show. Later, since Galba's feelings were uncertain and many senators pleaded with him, Priscus dropped the case. Opinions varied, as people's temperaments do. Some praised his moderation, and others missed his firmness. On the day the Senate was voting on Vespasian's imperial powers, it had been decided to send envoys to the new emperor. That started a sharp quarrel between Helvidius and Eprius. Priscus wanted the magistrates to choose the envoys by name, under oath. Marcellus demanded the urn (drawing lots), which was also what the consul-elect had proposed.
4:7 Marcellus pushed hard because he was ashamed at the thought of looking snubbed if other men were picked. Little by little the argument grew into long, hostile speeches. Helvidius asked: "Why is Marcellus so afraid of the magistrates' judgment? He has money and a gift for speaking, enough to put him ahead of most men, if the memory of his crimes weren't weighing on him. Drawing lots tells you nothing about character. Votes and the Senate's opinion were invented to reach into each man's life and reputation. It serves the public good, and it honors Vespasian, that he be met by the men the Senate considers most blameless, who can fill the emperor's ears with honest advice. Vespasian was a friend of Thrasea, Soranus and Sentius, and even if it isn't the right time to punish their accusers, they shouldn't be paraded in front of him. By this choice the Senate would, so to speak, warn the emperor whom to approve and whom to fear. There's no better tool of good government than good friends. Let Marcellus be satisfied with having driven Nero to destroy so many innocent men. Let him enjoy his rewards and his impunity, but leave Vespasian to better advisers."
4:8 Marcellus said: "It's not my opinion that's under attack. The consul-elect made a motion in line with old precedent, which called for lots to choose envoys, so there'd be no room for scheming or personal grudges. Nothing has happened that should make us drop long-standing customs, or turn the honor due the emperor into an insult to anyone. Every senator is fit to pay his respects. What we should avoid is irritating, by some people's stubbornness, a mind unsettled by the newness of power, one that watches everyone's looks and words closely. I haven't forgotten the times I was born in, or the form of government our fathers and grandfathers set up. I may admire an earlier age, but I follow the present one, and while I pray for good emperors, I can put up with whatever kind we get. It wasn't my speech, any more than the Senate's judgment, that brought Thrasea down. Nero's savage temper amused itself through such forms. And friendship with a ruler like that was as nerve-racking for me as exile was for others. Fine, let Helvidius match the Catos and Brutuses of old for toughness and courage. I'm just one man from a Senate that bowed together. I'd also advise Priscus not to try to climb above the emperor, or to lecture Vespasian, an old man who has had a triumph and has grown sons. The worst emperors want power without limit, and even the best like some limit on freedom." These speeches were thrown back and forth with great heat on both sides, and people took them in with mixed feelings. The side that preferred choosing the envoys by lot won. Even the neutral senators worked to keep the old custom, and the most distinguished men leaned the same way, afraid of envy if they themselves were chosen.
## Money, the Capitol and the first trial of an informer
4:9 Another fight followed. The praetors of the Treasury (the Treasury was run by praetors at that time) complained about the state's poverty and demanded a limit on spending. The consul-elect, because the problem was so big and the cure so hard, wanted to leave it for the emperor. Helvidius held that the Senate should decide it. When the consuls were asking for votes, Vulcatius Tertullinus, a tribune of the people, blocked it, saying nothing should be decided on so important a matter while the emperor was away. Helvidius had also moved that the Capitol be rebuilt with public money and that Vespasian help. The more moderate senators let that go by in silence and later forgot it, but some did remember.
4:10 Then Musonius Rufus attacked Publius Celer fiercely, charging that he had trapped Barea Soranus with false testimony. This trial seemed to bring back all the hatreds of the days of the informers. But the accused was so worthless and so obviously guilty that no one could protect him. Soranus's memory was held sacred, and Celer, who claimed to be a teacher of philosophy, had testified against Barea and so betrayed and corrupted the very friendship he said he taught. The next day was set for the trial. But people were watching less for Musonius or Publius than for Priscus and Marcellus and the rest, now that their anger for revenge had been stirred.
## Mucianus takes control of Rome
4:11 With things in that state, with division in the Senate, anger among the defeated, no real authority among the winners, and neither laws nor an emperor in the state, Mucianus entered the city and drew everything to himself at once. The power of Primus Antonius and Arrius Varus was broken. Mucianus's anger at them was badly hidden, even though he kept it off his face, and the public, quick to sniff out a grudge, switched sides and moved its loyalty. He alone was courted and flattered, and he played his part. Surrounded by armed men, trading one house and garden for another, he took hold of an emperor's power with his show, his bearing and his guards, while letting the title go. The killing of Calpurnius Galerianus caused the most terror. He was a son of Gaius Piso. He'd done nothing wrong, but his famous name and his good looks as a young man were on everyone's lips. In a state that was still unsettled and delighted with new talk, there were people who linked him with empty rumors of imperial rule. On Mucianus's order he was put under military guard. So that his death wouldn't be too conspicuous in the city itself, he was taken to the fortieth milestone from Rome on the Appian Road and killed there by opening his veins. Julius Priscus, who had been prefect of the Praetorian cohorts under Vitellius, killed himself, more out of shame than because he had to. Alfenus Varus outlived the shame of his cowardice. Asiaticus, who was only a freed slave, paid for his abuse of power with a slave's death.
## The Batavians and the revolt of Civilis
4:12 At this time the city was hearing, with anything but sorrow, rumors of disaster in Germany that grew stronger every day. People talked of armies slaughtered, legion winter camps captured, the Gauls in revolt, as if these weren't evils. I'll go back further to explain what caused this war and how big a stir it caused among foreign and allied peoples. The Batavians used to live across the Rhine, as part of the Chatti. Driven out by a civil quarrel, they settled the farthest edge of the Gallic coast, empty of farmers, and also the island next to it, which the Ocean washes in front and the river Rhine around the back and sides. Their resources were never drained (which is rare for the partner of a stronger power), and they gave the empire only men and weapons. They had long been trained in the German wars, and then added to their fame in Britain, where their cohorts had been sent, led, by old custom, by the noblest men of their people. At home they also had a select cavalry with a special passion for swimming, who could cross the Rhine with their weapons and horses while keeping their squadrons whole.
4:13 Julius Paullus and Julius Civilis came from the royal family and ranked far above everyone else in their nation. Fonteius Capito had Paullus killed on a false charge of rebellion. Civilis was put in chains and sent to Nero. Galba acquitted him, but under Vitellius he was in danger again when the army demanded his execution. These were the causes of his anger, and his hopes grew out of Rome's misfortunes. Civilis was cleverer than barbarians usually are, and he liked to compare himself to Sertorius or Hannibal, because his face was disfigured in a similar way. To avoid being met as an enemy if he openly broke with the Roman people, he put on a show of friendship with Vespasian and zeal for his side. A letter had indeed been sent to him by Primus Antonius, ordering him to turn back the auxiliary troops Vitellius had called up and to hold the legions in place by the appearance of a German uprising. Hordeonius Flaccus, who was there in person, had advised the same. He leaned toward Vespasian, and he worried for the empire, which faced ruin if the war broke out again and so many thousands of armed men poured into Italy.
4:14 So Civilis, certain that he would rebel, hid his deeper plan for the moment, meant to judge the rest as events went, and began the uprising this way. On Vitellius's orders the young Batavian men were being called up for the draft. It was a heavy burden by its nature, and the officials made it worse with greed and wild living. They hunted down old or weak men so they could let them go for a price. And they dragged off boys who weren't grown yet but were good-looking (most Batavians are tall even as boys) for shameful purposes. That stirred up resentment, and the organizers of the planned rebellion talked the people into refusing the draft. Civilis invited the chiefs of the nation and the boldest of the common people to a sacred grove, supposedly for a feast. When he saw them warmed by the night and the good cheer, he began with the renown and glory of their people, then went through the wrongs, the plundering and all the other evils of slavery. He said: "We're not treated as allies, as we once were, but like slaves. When does even a legate come to us? And then it's with a heavy, arrogant escort. We're handed over to prefects and centurions, and when they've stuffed themselves with our spoils and our blood, they're replaced, and new hiding places for plunder and new names for robbery are sought out. Now the draft is on us, and it will tear children from their parents and brothers from brothers, as if for the last time. Rome's power has never been in worse shape. In the winter camps there's nothing but loot and old men. Just lift your eyes, and stop trembling at the empty names of legions. We have a strong force of infantry and cavalry, we have our blood relatives the Germans, and we have the Gauls, who want the same thing. Even the Roman people won't mind this war. If it fails, we'll count it as a service to Vespasian, and if it succeeds, no one has to account for it."
## Brinno and the Canninefates strike; the Roman fleet is lost
4:15 Civilis was heard with great approval. He bound them all by their tribe's rites and the curses they swear in their oaths. Envoys were sent to the Canninefates to join in the plan. This tribe lives on part of the island and matches the Batavians in origin, language and courage, but has fewer people. Next he used secret messengers to win over the British auxiliaries and the Batavian cohorts that, as I told earlier, had been sent into Germany and were then stationed at Mogontiacum. Among the Canninefates was Brinno, a man of stubborn boldness and famous birth. His father had dared many hostile acts, and had scorned, and got away with scorning, Caligula's ridiculous expedition. The mere name of a family of rebels made Brinno popular. He was placed on a shield in the tribe's custom and swung up on the shoulders of the bearers, and so he was chosen leader. At once he called in the Frisii, a tribe from across the Rhine, and broke into the winter camp of two cohorts, at the point nearest the sea. The soldiers hadn't foreseen the enemy's attack, and if they had, they didn't have the strength to hold it off. So the camp was captured and looted. Then the attackers fell on the camp followers and Roman traders, who were wandering around freely as in peacetime. At the same time they were about to wipe out the forts, but the cohort prefects burned them, since they couldn't defend them. The standards, flags and what soldiers there were gathered in the upper part of the island under Aquilius, a top-ranking centurion: an army in name more than in strength. Vitellius had pulled the real troops out of the cohorts and loaded a crowd of loafers from the nearby Nervian and German villages with weapons.
4:16 Civilis thought he had to work by trickery. He even blamed the prefects for abandoning the forts, saying he would put down the Canninefate uprising himself with the cohort he commanded, and that they should each go back to their own winter camps. It was plain that treachery lay under this advice: the cohorts were being split up so they'd be easier to crush, and the man leading this war was not Brinno but Civilis. Signs of the truth were slowly leaking out, and the Germans, a people who enjoy war, couldn't keep them hidden for long. When the trick failed, he turned to force, setting up the Canninefates, Frisii and Batavians each in its own column. A line was drawn up opposite them, not far from the river Rhine, with the ships that the Romans had run ashore there after burning the forts turned to face the enemy. Before the fighting had gone on long, a cohort of Tungri carried its standards over to Civilis. The rest of the soldiers, thrown off by the sudden betrayal, were cut down by friend and foe alike. The fleet had the same treachery. Some of the rowers were Batavians, and they got in the way of the sailors and fighting men, as if from lack of skill. Then they rowed the opposite way and ran the sterns onto the enemy bank. At last they killed the helmsmen and centurions who wouldn't join them, until the whole fleet of twenty-four ships either went over or was captured.
## Civilis tries to win over Gaul; Lupercus is beaten in the field
4:17 For the moment this was a brilliant victory, and it would be useful later. The rebels got the weapons and ships they needed, and across Germany and Gaul they were celebrated as the champions of freedom. The German tribes at once sent envoys offering troops. Civilis tried to win over the Gauls with clever gifts. He sent the captured cohort prefects back to their own states, and gave the cohorts the choice to leave or stay. Those who stayed were offered honored service, and those who left were offered spoils taken from the Romans. At the same time, in private talks, he reminded them of the wrongs they'd put up with for so many years while they wrongly called their miserable slavery peace. He said: "The Batavians, though they're exempt from tribute, have taken up arms against our common masters. In the first battle the Roman army was routed and beaten. What if Gaul throws off the yoke? How much is left in Italy? Provinces are conquered with provincials' blood. Don't think about Vindex's army. It was Batavian cavalry that trampled the Aedui and the Arverni, and there were Belgians among Verginius's auxiliaries. Anyone who thinks it over honestly will see that Gaul fell by its own strength. Now everyone is on the same side, and we have whatever military discipline was left in the Roman camps. With us are the veteran cohorts that Otho's legions recently fell to. Let Syria, Asia and the East, used to being ruled by kings, bow to slavery. Many people are still alive in Gaul who were born before tribute. Just lately Quintilius Varus was killed and slavery was driven out of Germany. And the man that war challenged wasn't a Vitellius but a Caesar Augustus. Freedom is a gift of nature even to animals that can't speak. Courage is humanity's own good, and the gods stand with the braver. So let's strike, free and fresh as we are, at a distracted and worn-out enemy. While some back Vespasian and others Vitellius, the field is open against both."
4:18 So Civilis, with his eyes on Gaul and Germany, was reaching, if his plans succeeded, for rule over the most powerful and richest nations. Hordeonius Flaccus had fed his first moves by pretending not to know. But when frightened messengers brought news that a camp had been stormed, cohorts wiped out, and the Roman name driven from the island of the Batavians, he ordered the legate Munius Lupercus, who commanded the winter camp of two legions, to go out against the enemy. Lupercus rushed across the Rhine the legionaries who were on hand, some Ubians from nearby, and Treveran cavalry stationed not far away, along with a wing of Batavian cavalry. They had been bought off long before and were faking loyalty, so that by betraying the Romans in the battle itself they could desert for a higher price. Civilis surrounded himself with the standards of the captured cohorts, so his own men would have their recent glory before their eyes and the enemy would be terrified by the memory of defeat. He ordered his mother and sisters, and the wives and little children of all his men, to take a position behind the line, to spur them on to victory or shame them if they were beaten. The line rang with the men's singing and the women's shrieks, and the cheer that came back from the legions and cohorts was nowhere near as strong. The Batavian wing deserted and left the left flank bare, and turned on the Romans at once. Still the legionaries, though in a frightening spot, held onto their weapons and ranks. The Ubian and Treveran auxiliaries ran off in a shameful rout and scattered across the fields. The Germans threw themselves on that side, and meanwhile the legions got a chance to escape into the camp called the Old Camp. Claudius Labeo, prefect of the Batavian cavalry, was a rival of Civilis in a quarrel between their towns. He was shipped off to the Frisii, because killing him might cause resentment among his countrymen, and keeping him around might sow the seeds of discord.
## Hordeonius Flaccus and the Batavian cohorts
4:19 About the same time, the messenger Civilis had sent caught up with the cohorts of Batavians and Canninefates, who were on their way to Rome on Vitellius's orders. They immediately swelled up with pride and arrogance and demanded a bonus for their march, double pay and more cavalry. Vitellius had indeed promised these, but they asked now not to get them but to have an excuse for mutiny. By giving in so often, Flaccus had only made them demand more fiercely what they knew he'd refuse. They treated him with contempt and headed for Lower Germany to join Civilis. Hordeonius called together the tribunes and centurions and asked whether he should use force on the cohorts that refused to obey. Before long he gave in to his natural timidity and to the fears of his officers, who were worried by the shaky loyalty of the auxiliaries and by the legions having been filled out by a rushed draft, and he decided to keep the troops inside the camp. Then he regretted that. The very officers who had urged him to do it now disapproved. So he acted as if he meant to chase the Batavians, and wrote to Herennius Gallus, legate of the 1st legion, who held Bonna, to stop them from crossing, saying he would follow close behind them with his army. They could have been crushed if Hordeonius from one side and Gallus from the other had moved their forces and trapped them in between. But Flaccus dropped the plan, and in another letter told Gallus not to threaten them as they left. That gave rise to the suspicion that the war was being kindled with the legates' consent, and that everything that had happened or was feared came not from the soldiers' sluggishness or the enemy's strength, but from the generals' treachery.
4:20 When the Batavians drew near the camp at Bonna, they sent ahead a messenger to give Herennius Gallus the cohorts' message: "We have no quarrel with the Romans, for whom we've fought so often. We're worn out by a long, pointless service, and we want to go home and rest. If no one blocks us, our march will do no harm. But if an armed force meets us, we'll cut our way through with the sword." The soldiers had pushed the hesitating legate into trying his luck in battle. Three thousand legionaries, some hastily raised Belgian cohorts, and a mob of peasants and camp followers, cowardly but loud before the danger, burst out of all the gates to surround the Batavians, who were outnumbered. But the Batavians were veterans. They formed wedges, solid on every side, with front, flanks and rear protected, and so they broke the thin Roman line. The Belgians gave way, the legion was pushed back, and in a panic they headed for the rampart and the gates. That's where most of the slaughter happened. The ditches were piled with bodies. Many died not only from killing blows and wounds but also crushed in the rush and by their own weapons. The winners avoided Colonia Agrippinensis and tried no other hostile act on the rest of the march. They excused the battle at Bonna by saying they'd asked for peace and, when it was refused, had only looked to their own safety.
## Civilis besieges the Old Camp
4:21 Civilis now had a complete army, since the veteran cohorts had arrived, but he was still undecided about his plans and kept thinking about Rome's power. He made everyone with him swear allegiance to Vespasian, and sent envoys to the two legions, which after their defeat in the earlier battle had retreated into the Old Camp, telling them to take the same oath. They answered: "We don't take advice from a traitor or an enemy. Vitellius is our emperor. We'll keep faith with him and use our swords for him to our last breath. So a Batavian deserter shouldn't pretend to settle Rome's fate. Let him wait for the punishment he deserves for his crime." When this answer was brought to Civilis, he blazed with anger and pulled the whole Batavian nation into open war. The Bructeri and Tencteri joined him, and messengers called all Germany to share in the loot and glory.
4:22 To meet the threats of the coming war, the legates of the legions, Munius Lupercus and Numisius Rufus, strengthened the entrenchments and walls. The buildings that had grown up like a town near the camp during a long peace were torn down so they wouldn't help the enemy. But too little was done to bring supplies into the camp. They allowed them to be plundered, so in a few days, through carelessness, what could have lasted a long time against need was used up. Civilis held the middle of the line with the best of the Batavians. To look more terrifying, he filled both banks of the Rhine with bands of Germans, while his cavalry rode wildly across the plains. At the same time the ships were rowed upstream. On one side were the standards of the veteran cohorts, on the other the images of wild animals, brought out of the woods and sacred groves, as each tribe is used to going into battle. The mixed look of civil war and foreign war stunned the besieged. And the size of the rampart raised the attackers' hopes: it was built for two legions, and barely five thousand Roman soldiers defended it. But a great mass of camp followers, gathered there when peace was disturbed, was on hand to help in the war.
4:23 Part of the camp rose up a gentle hill, and part could be approached on level ground. Augustus had believed these winter camps would hold Germany in a siege and keep it under pressure. He never imagined such disaster, that the Germans would come to attack the legions on their own. So no work was put into the site or the defenses: strength and weapons seemed enough. The Batavians and the tribes from across the Rhine took their places, each tribe by itself, so that each one's courage would be seen more clearly. At first they harassed the camp from a distance. Then they saw that most of their missiles stuck harmlessly in the towers and battlements of the walls while they themselves were hurt by stones thrown from above, so with a shout and a rush they stormed the rampart. Most set up ladders, and others climbed up on a shield-roof their friends held over their heads. Some were already climbing over when they were hurled down by swords and the jab of weapons and buried under stakes and javelins. They were overbold at the start and too proud when things went well, but now, hungry for plunder, they put up with setbacks too. They even dared to use siege engines, something new to them. They had no skill of their own, but deserters and prisoners taught them to pile up timber in the shape of a bridge and then put wheels under it and push it along, with some men fighting from the top as if from a rampart and others hidden inside to dig under the walls. But rocks shot from the catapults smashed the shapeless contraption. When they began preparing wicker screens and covered sheds, burning spears were fired into them from the machines, and the attackers themselves were hit with fire. Finally, giving up on force, they changed their plan to waiting. They knew there was food for only a few days in the camp and a large crowd of non-fighters. At the same time they hoped for treachery born of hunger, for the slaves' shaky loyalty and for the chances of war.
## The army turns on Hordeonius; Vocula takes command
4:24 Meanwhile Flaccus learned of the siege of the camp. He sent people through Gaul to gather auxiliaries, and he handed picked men from the legions to Dillius Vocula, legate of the 22nd legion, to hurry along the riverbank by the longest daily marches he could. Flaccus himself went by ship, weak in body and hated by the soldiers. They grumbled openly about it. They said: "The Batavian cohorts were let out of Mogontiacum, Civilis's moves were ignored, and the German tribes are being invited into an alliance. Vespasian has grown no stronger from the help of Primus Antonius or Mucianus than from this. Open hatred and open weapons can be driven back, but fraud and deceit work in the dark and can't be avoided. Civilis stands against us and draws up his battle line, while Hordeonius gives orders from his bedroom and his cot, whatever helps the enemy. All these armed bands of the bravest men are ruled by one old man's ill health. Better to kill the traitor and free our luck and our courage from this bad omen." Stirred up by such talk among themselves, they were set on fire even more by letters that came from Vespasian. Flaccus read them out before the assembled troops, since they couldn't be hidden, and sent the men who had brought them to Vitellius in chains.
4:25 With tempers calmed this way, they came to Bonna, the winter camp of the 1st legion. The soldiers there were angrier and put the blame for the defeat on Hordeonius. They said it was on his orders that the battle line had been drawn up against the Batavians, as if the legions from Mogontiacum were following, and that it was through his betrayal that they'd been cut down, with no help arriving. The other armies knew nothing of this, they said, and the emperor wasn't being told, though the sudden treason could have been put down by the quick help of so many provinces. Hordeonius read out to the army copies of all the letters in which he'd begged for help through Gaul, Britain and Spain. He also set up a very bad precedent: letters were to be handed to the legions' eagle-bearers, who read them to the soldiers before the generals did. Then he had one of the mutineers put in chains, more to claim his authority than because one man was at fault. The army moved from Bonna to Colonia Agrippinensis. Gallic auxiliaries kept flowing in, because at first the Gauls helped Rome's cause with energy. But soon, as the Germans grew stronger, most of the Gallic states took up arms against Rome, hoping to be free and, once the yoke was off, even to rule over others. The legions' anger kept growing, and the chains on one soldier didn't scare them. He actually turned around and accused the general of being in on it. He said he'd been a messenger between Civilis and Flaccus, and was being crushed on a false charge as a witness to the truth. Vocula climbed the platform with remarkable steadiness and ordered the soldier, who had been seized and was shouting, to be led away to execution. While the bad men were scared, all the best men obeyed the order. Then, as they all agreed in demanding Vocula as their general, Flaccus handed the supreme command over to him.
## Hunger, drought and mutiny at Gelduba
4:26 But many things stirred up the already divided soldiers. Pay and grain ran short, and Gaul was refusing the draft and the taxes. The Rhine, in a drought unheard of in that climate, could hardly carry ships, so supplies were tight. Guard posts had to be set along the whole bank to keep the Germans from crossing at the fords, so for the same reason there was less grain and more mouths to eat it. Among the uneducated, the very lack of water counted as an omen, as if even the rivers, the empire's old defenses, were deserting Rome. What in peacetime would have been called chance or nature was now called fate and a god's anger. When they entered Novesium, the 16th legion joined them. Herennius Gallus the legate was added to Vocula to share the duties. They didn't dare march on the enemy, so they made a camp at a place called Gelduba. There they steadied the soldiers by forming battle lines, building fortifications, digging and walling, and the other drills of war. To fire the men's courage with the lure of loot, Vocula led the army against the nearest villages of the Gugerni, who had accepted Civilis's alliance. Part of the army stayed with Herennius Gallus.
4:27 By chance a ship loaded with grain, stuck in the shallows not far from the camp, was being dragged by the Germans to their own bank. Gallus couldn't stand it and sent a cohort to help. The number of Germans grew too, and as more and more help joined in, it turned into a regular battle. The Germans killed many Romans and carried off the ship. The beaten soldiers, as had now become their habit, blamed not their own cowardice but the treachery of their legate. They dragged him out of his tent, tore his clothes, beat him, and ordered him to say what price he'd been paid and who his accomplices were when he betrayed the army. Their hatred of Hordeonius came back. They called him the author of the crime and Gallus his tool, until Gallus, terrified by threats of death, himself accused Hordeonius of treason. He was then put in chains, and released only when Vocula finally arrived. The next day Vocula had the ringleaders of the mutiny put to death. That army had that wide a gap between its wildness and its obedience. The common soldiers were certainly loyal to Vitellius, but all the most distinguished men leaned toward Vespasian. So there were outbreaks and executions in turn, and a strange mix of obedience and frenzy, so that men who could be punished could not be held back.
## The Ubii attacked; the siege tightens
4:28 Meanwhile all Germany lifted Civilis up with huge reinforcements, and the alliance was sealed with hostages from the noblest families. He ordered the lands of the Ubii and the Treveri to be ravaged by the nearest tribes, and another force to cross the river Mosa and shake the Menapii and the Morini and the farthest parts of Gaul. Plunder was taken in both places, with more hostility toward the Ubii, because they were a people of German origin who had given up their homeland and been called by a Roman name, the Agrippinenses. Their cohorts were cut down in the village of Marcodurum, where they were careless because they were far from the riverbank. The Ubii didn't stay quiet but went raiding in Germany, at first with impunity, and then they were surrounded. Through that whole war they proved more loyal than lucky. With the Ubii crushed, Civilis became more dangerous and bolder with success, and he pressed the siege of the legions. He set close guards so that no secret messenger of coming help could get in. He gave the machines and the great siege works to the Batavians. When the tribes from across the Rhine demanded battle, he ordered them to go and tear down the rampart, and if driven back, to renew the fight, since they had more than enough men and a loss wouldn't be felt.
4:29 Even night didn't end the struggle. They piled wood around the walls and lit it, and sat feasting, and as each man heated up with wine, he rushed into the fight with pointless daring. Their own missiles flew uselessly in the dark, while the Romans could see the barbarian line clearly and picked out for a shot anyone who stood out by his boldness or his glittering decorations. Civilis noticed this and had the fires put out, so that everything was thrown into confusion by darkness and weapons. Then it was all noise and clashing and chance outcomes, and no one could see how to strike or dodge. Wherever a shout had come from, men swung around and strained their limbs that way. Courage was no help, chance threw everything into confusion, and the bravest often fell to a coward's missile. The Germans fought with reckless fury. The Roman soldiers knew the dangers, and threw iron-tipped stakes and heavy stones with purpose, not by chance. Where the noise of men at work or a ladder set against the wall put the enemy within reach, they shoved them back with their shields and followed up with javelins. Many who had gotten up onto the walls they stabbed with daggers. After a night spent this way, day brought a new kind of attack.
4:30 The Batavians had built a two-story tower and pushed it up to the Praetorian gate, where the ground was most level. But the Romans pushed out strong poles against it and rammed it with beams and broke it down, killing many of the men standing on top. They attacked the dazed enemy in a sudden and successful sortie. Meanwhile the legionaries, who were better in experience and skill, built many more engines. One machine terrified the enemy especially. It hung in the air and swayed, and then was suddenly lowered, and as it went past the faces of their friends it caught one or more of the enemy and lifted them high, and then, by shifting the counterweight, tossed them inside the camp. Civilis gave up hope of taking the camp by storm. He sat down again to wait it out, and he wore down the legions' loyalty with messages and promises.
## News of Cremona reaches the Rhine; Civilis answers Montanus
4:31 All this happened in Germany before the battle of Cremona. The result was announced in a dispatch from Primus Antonius, along with Caecina's proclamation. Alpinius Montanus, a cohort prefect from the beaten side, was there in person and admitted what had happened to his party. People reacted in different ways. The Gallic auxiliaries, who felt neither love nor hatred for either side and served without feeling, turned away from Vitellius at once at their prefects' urging. The veteran soldiers hesitated. But with Hordeonius Flaccus pressing and the tribunes insisting, they took the oath, though neither their faces nor their hearts backed it up. They spoke the other words of the oath, but when they came to Vespasian's name, they hesitated, passing over it with a faint murmur, and usually with silence.
4:32 Next, letters from Antonius to Civilis were read to the assembled soldiers, and they raised the soldiers' suspicions, as though they were written to a partner in the cause and spoke of the German army as an enemy. Soon the news reached the camp at Gelduba, and the same words and actions were repeated there. Montanus was sent to Civilis with instructions to stop the war and not to cloak a foreign enemy's plans under false weapons: if he'd set out to help Vespasian, he had already done enough. Civilis answered cleverly at first. But then he saw that Montanus was extremely high-spirited and ready for revolution. He began with his complaints and the dangers he had gone through over twenty-five years in Roman camps, and said: "A fine reward I've received for my labors: my brother's murder, my own chains, and the savage shouts of this army. A mob demanded my execution, and by the law of nations I demand payback for that. And you Treveri, and all the rest of you who are only the living souls of slaves, what reward do you expect for the blood you've spilled so many times? A thankless military service, endless taxes, rods, axes and the tempers of masters. Look at me. I'm the prefect of one cohort, and with the Canninefates and Batavians, a tiny part of Gaul, I've destroyed those huge, useless camps, or we have them penned in by sword and famine. In short, for those who dare, either freedom will follow, or if we lose, we'll be what we were before." That is how Civilis fired up Montanus. He sent him back, telling him to carry a milder answer. Montanus returned as if his mission had failed, hiding the rest, which soon came out.
## Battle outside Gelduba
4:33 Civilis kept back part of his forces and sent the veteran cohorts and the readiest part of the Germans against Vocula and his army, under Julius Maximus and Claudius Victor, his sister's son. On the way they plundered the winter camp of a body of horse at Ascibergium, and they fell on Vocula's camp so unexpectedly that he couldn't address his men or even form a line. In the confusion he could only order the legionaries under the standards to strengthen the center. The auxiliaries were scattered all around. The cavalry charged out, but met the enemy's ordered ranks and turned their backs on their own men. What followed was a slaughter, not a battle. The Nervian cohorts, out of panic or treachery, left the Roman flanks open. So the attack reached the legions, who had lost their standards and were being cut down inside the rampart, when suddenly new help changed the fortune of the battle. Vascon cohorts that Galba had raised, and that had been sent for by now, heard the noise of the fighting as they neared the camp, and attacked the enemy from behind while they were busy at the front. They spread more panic than their numbers deserved, since some believed that the entire forces from Novesium had arrived, and others that those from Mogontiacum had. This mistake gave the Romans new courage, and by trusting in others' strength they got their own back. All the bravest of the Batavians, as many as were infantry, were routed. The cavalry got away with the standards and with the prisoners they had grabbed in the first clash. More Romans were killed that day, but they were the weaker fighters. The Germans lost the very best of their force.
4:34 Both generals were equally to blame. They deserved their defeats and didn't make the most of their wins. If Civilis had drawn up a bigger force, so few cohorts couldn't have outflanked him, and he could have broken into the camp and destroyed it. Vocula hadn't scouted the enemy's approach, so he marched out and was beaten at once. Then, not trusting his victory, he wasted several days for nothing before moving camp against the enemy. If he had pushed them back right away and followed up his success, he could have lifted the siege of the legions in the same stroke. Meanwhile Civilis had tried to shake the besieged by claiming that Rome's cause was lost and his own side had won. Standards and flags were carried around, and even captives were shown off. One of them, with remarkable daring, told the real story in a loud voice, and when the Germans cut him down on the spot, his report was believed all the more. At the same time the devastation of the countryside and the burning farmhouses showed that the victorious army was approaching. Vocula ordered the standards planted in sight of the camp, and a ditch and rampart built around them, so the men could drop their baggage and packs and fight unburdened. At that, the general was met with shouts demanding battle. They had gotten used to threatening. Without even taking time to form a line, disordered and tired, they started the fight. Civilis was there, relying as much on his enemy's faults as on his own men's courage. Among the Romans fortunes varied, and the loudest mutineers turned out to be cowards. Some, remembering their recent victory, held their ground and struck the enemy, encouraging themselves and their neighbors. When their line was re-formed, they reached their hands out toward the besieged, urging them not to miss their chance. The besieged, seeing everything from the walls, burst out of every gate. By chance Civilis was thrown to the ground when his horse fell. A report that he had been wounded or killed was believed through both armies, and it filled his own men with enormous terror and gave their enemies great energy. But Vocula didn't chase the backs of the fleeing enemy. Instead he built up the rampart and towers of the camp, as if another siege were coming. He had spoiled victory so often that he was rightly suspected of preferring the war.
## Supply trouble, mutiny and the murder of Hordeonius
4:35 Nothing wore down the Roman armies like the shortage of supplies. The legions' baggage was sent to Novesium with a crowd of non-fighters to bring back grain overland from there, since the enemy held the river. The first column traveled safely, because Civilis wasn't strong enough yet. But when he heard that grain-gatherers had been sent to Novesium again, with cohorts assigned as an escort walking along as if in deep peace, few soldiers around the standards, weapons packed in the wagons, everyone wandering wherever he pleased, he attacked in formation, after sending men ahead to hold the bridges and narrow places in the road. The fight stretched along a long column with an uncertain outcome until night broke it up. The cohorts went on to Gelduba, and the camp there stayed as it was, held by the garrison of soldiers left behind. There was no doubt what danger the grain-carriers would face on the way back, heavily loaded and rattled. Vocula added to his army a thousand picked men from the 5th and 15th legions that were besieged at the Old Camp, an unruly lot who resented their officers. More left than were ordered, and on the march they openly grumbled that they would no longer put up with hunger and the legates' treachery. But those who stayed behind complained that they had been abandoned when part of the legions was taken away. So there was mutiny from two directions: some called on Vocula to come back, and the others refused to return to the camp.
4:36 Meanwhile Civilis laid siege to the Old Camp. Vocula withdrew to Gelduba and from there to Novesium. Civilis took Gelduba, and soon after won a cavalry fight not far from Novesium. But the soldiers, in victory and defeat alike, were being stirred up to ruin their commanders. The legions, swelled by the arrival of the men of the 5th and 15th, demanded a bonus, having found out that Vitellius had sent money. After a short delay Hordeonius paid it out in Vespasian's name, and that was the main fuel for the mutiny. They threw themselves into excess, feasting and nighttime gatherings, and revived their old anger at Hordeonius. No legate or tribune dared stand in their way, since the darkness seems to have taken away all their shame, and they dragged him from his bed and killed him. The same was planned for Vocula, but he put on a slave's clothes and slipped away in the dark, not recognized.
4:37 When their rage settled and fear returned, they sent centurions with letters to the states of Gaul to ask for troops and pay. The soldiers themselves, like any mob without a leader, headstrong, fearful and lazy, snatched up weapons recklessly when Civilis approached, dropped them right away, and ran. The setback bred disunion, as the men from the Upper army separated their cause from the others. Even so, the images of Vitellius were put back up in the camp and in the nearby Belgian cities, though Vitellius was already dead. Then the soldiers of the 1st, 4th and 22nd legions changed their minds and regretted it, and followed Vocula. They took the oath to Vespasian again before him and were led to lift the siege of Mogontiacum. The besiegers, a mixed force of Chatti, Usipii and Mattiaci, had left, full of loot and not without bloodshed. The Roman soldiers had attacked them on the road, scattered and unaware. The Treveri also built a breastwork and rampart across their land, and they and the Germans kept fighting, with heavy losses on both sides, until the Treveri, by rebelling, spoiled their fine services to the Roman people.
## Rome: a scare about Africa; honors and maneuvers
4:38 Meanwhile Vespasian, for the second time, and Titus took up the consulship while absent from Rome. The city was gloomy and gripped by many fears, since besides the real dangers it had taken up groundless panics: that Africa had revolted, with Lucius Piso working for revolution. He was proconsul of the province and was far from a restless character. But the grain ships were being held back by the severe winter weather, and the common people, who were used to buying food day by day and whose one concern in public affairs was the grain supply, believed in their fear that the coast was closed and shipments were being held back. The Vitellianists, who hadn't yet given up their party spirit, helped the rumor grow, and it wasn't unwelcome even to the winners, whose greed not even foreign wars could fill, and no civil war victory ever satisfied.
## The Senate meets on the first of January; Mucianus and Antonius Primus
4:39 On the first of January, at a Senate meeting called by Julius Frontinus, the city praetor, thanks and praise were voted to the legates, the armies and the allied kings. The praetorship was taken from Tettius Julianus, on the grounds that he had deserted his legion when it went over to Vespasian's side, so that it could be transferred to Plotius Griphus. Hormus was given the rank of Roman Knight. Then, when Frontinus resigned, Domitian, the Caesar, took the praetorship. His name was put at the head of letters and edicts, but the power was in Mucianus's hands, except that Domitian ventured many acts of his own at his friends' urging or on his own whim. Mucianus's main fear was Primus Antonius and Arrius Varus, who were fresh and famous for their achievements, and who had the soldiers' devotion and even the people's favor, since they had not been cruel to anyone beyond the battlefield. There was a report that Antonius had urged Scribonianus Crassus, who stood out because of his distinguished ancestors and his brother's honors, to take over the government. He wouldn't have lacked a band of supporters if Scribonianus hadn't refused. He was a man not easily tempted even by a sure thing, and very careful about risk. So Mucianus, since Antonius couldn't be crushed openly, piled praises on him in the Senate and loaded him with secret promises, dangling Nearer Spain, which was empty after Cluvius Rufus left. At the same time he showered Antonius's friends with posts as tribunes and prefects. Then, once he had filled the man's empty head with hope and ambition, he destroyed his strength by sending the 7th legion, which loved Antonius most passionately, to winter quarters. And the 3rd legion, whose soldiers were close to Arrius Varus, was sent back to Syria. Part of the army was being led into Germany. With everything troublesome removed, the city got back its usual shape, with the laws and the magistrates' duties.
## Domitian in the Senate; Publius Celer is convicted; the oath
4:40 On the day Domitian took his seat in the Senate, he gave a short, modest speech about his father and brother being away and about his own youth. He carried himself well, and since his character wasn't known yet, his frequent blushing was taken for modesty. When Domitian proposed restoring Galba's honors, Curtius Montanus moved that Piso's memory be honored too. The senators ordered both, but nothing came of the one about Piso. Then commissioners were chosen by lot to see that property seized in the war was returned, to examine the bronze law tablets that had fallen down from age and put them back in place, to clear the calendar of the flattery that had defaced it in recent times, and to set a limit on public spending. Tettius Julianus got his praetorship back once it was learned that he had fled to Vespasian. Griphus kept his honor. Then it was decided that the case between Musonius Rufus and Publius Celer should be heard again. Publius was convicted, and amends were made to the spirit of Soranus. A day marked by public justice wasn't without personal praise as well. Musonius seemed to have carried out a fair prosecution. People spoke differently about Demetrius, who professed the Cynic philosophy, because he had defended an obviously guilty man more to win favor than with honor. Publius himself had neither courage in his danger nor words to speak. With the signal for revenge on the informers given, Junius Mauricus asked Domitian to let the Senate see the imperial records, so they could learn who had asked for which prosecutions. Domitian answered that on such a matter the emperor must be consulted.
4:41 The Senate, with its leading members starting it, drew up an oath, which all the magistrates, and the other senators as they were called on to vote, eagerly swore, calling the gods as witnesses that nothing had been done by their help to harm anyone's safety, and that they had taken no reward or honor from citizens' disasters. Those who were conscious of guilt were nervous and changed the words of the oath in various ways. The senators approved the scruple and condemned the perjury. This was a sort of public censure, and it fell hardest on Sariolenus Vocula, Nonius Attianus and Cestius Severus, all notorious for frequent denunciations under Nero. Sariolenus also faced a recent charge, that he had tried the same thing under Vitellius. The Senate kept making threatening gestures at Sariolenus until he left the chamber. Turning to Paccius Africanus, they hounded him too, for having pointed out to Nero the Scribonius brothers, famous for their mutual affection and their wealth, for destruction. Africanus didn't dare confess and couldn't deny it. He turned on Vibius Crispus, who was wearing him down with questions, and by mixing in things he couldn't defend, he turned aside the hatred by sharing the guilt.
## Regulus on trial: Messalla's plea and Montanus's attack
4:42 Vipstanus Messalla earned great fame that day for family loyalty and for speaking, though he was too young to be a senator, by pleading for his brother Aquilius Regulus. The fall of the Crassus and Orfitus families had made Regulus hated more than anyone. He seemed to have taken on the prosecution of his own free will, while still quite young, not to ward off danger but in hope of power. And Sulpicia Praetextata, Crassus's wife, and her four children were present, ready to take revenge if the Senate heard the case. So Messalla did not defend the case or the accused, but put himself in the way of his brother's dangers, and so moved some senators. Curtius Montanus answered him with a savage speech. He went so far as to say that after Galba's death Regulus had given money to the man who killed Piso, and had even bitten the dead man's head. Montanus said: "Nero didn't force you to do that, Regulus, and that savagery bought you neither rank nor safety. We can put up with the excuses of men who preferred to ruin others rather than take a risk themselves. But you were left safe by your father's exile and the division of his property among his creditors. You were too young for office, and there was nothing in you that Nero could want or fear. It was lust for blood and greed for rewards that made you, a talent still unknown and untried in any defense, soak yourself in noble blood. You had grabbed a former consul's property from the Republic's grave, grown fat on seven million sesterces, and gleamed with priestly honors, and you crushed innocent boys, famous old men and noblewomen in one ruin. You blamed Nero for his slowness in wearing himself and his informers out house by house, when the whole Senate could be destroyed with a single word. Keep him, Senators! Protect a man with such clever advice, so every age has its teacher and our young men can copy Regulus the way our old ones copy Marcellus and Crispus. Even failed wickedness finds imitators. What happens if it flourishes and thrives? And the man we don't dare offend now, when he's only of quaestor's rank, will we dare to offend as a praetor and a consul? Do you think Nero was the last of the tyrants? Those who outlived Tiberius and Caligula thought the same, and meanwhile a worse and crueler one appeared. We aren't afraid of Vespasian. His age and moderation reassure us. But examples last longer than characters. We've gone limp, Senators. We're not the Senate that demanded, when Nero was dead, that the informers and the tyrant's agents be punished by ancient custom. The day right after a wicked emperor falls is the best chance there is."
4:43 Montanus was heard with such approval from the Senate that Helvidius hoped Marcellus could be brought down too. So he began with praise for Cluvius Rufus, who was just as rich and famous for speaking but had never endangered anyone under Nero. By this comparison and by direct accusation he pressed Eprius hard, with the senators' tempers blazing. When Marcellus noticed, he made as if to leave the chamber and said: "We're leaving, Priscus, and we leave you your Senate. Play the king, with Domitian himself here." Vibius Crispus followed him. Both were furious, but their faces were different. Marcellus had threatening eyes, and Crispus was smiling. Friends ran up and pulled them back. As the fight grew hotter, with many good men on one side and a few powerful men on the other pushing stubborn hatreds, the day was used up in quarreling.
## Mucianus protects the informers
4:44 At the next Senate meeting, Domitian began by urging that grief, anger and the harsh necessities of earlier times be put aside. Mucianus spoke at length in favor of the informers. He also warned in gentle, pleading tones those who were reviving cases they had started and dropped. The senators, once opposed, gave up the freedom they had begun to use. So that it wouldn't look like the Senate's judgment was being ignored, or that everything done under Nero went unpunished, Mucianus sent back to the same islands two men of senatorial rank, Octavius Sagitta and Antistius Sosianus, who had left their places of exile. Octavius, after an illicit affair with Pontia Postumia, who refused to marry him, had killed her in an uncontrollable fit of passion. Sosianus's depravity had been fatal to many. Both had been condemned and banished by a severe decree of the Senate, and although others were allowed to return, they were kept under the same penalty. But this didn't soften the hatred toward Mucianus. Sosianus and Sagitta were worthless even if they returned. What people feared was the informers' cleverness, their wealth and a power practiced in evil ways.
4:45 A hearing held in the Senate by the old rules brought the senators' feelings together for a while. Manlius Patruitus, a senator, complained that he had been beaten in the colony of Sena, by a gathering of the crowd and on the magistrates' orders. The wrong hadn't stopped there: weeping and wailing, a mock funeral, had been staged around him as he stood there, with insults and abuse thrown at the whole Senate. Those accused were summoned, and after the case was heard, the guilty were punished. A decree of the Senate was also added, warning the common people of Sena to behave. In the same days Antonius Flamma was convicted under the law on extortion, on the complaint of the people of Cyrene, and was exiled for cruelty.
## The Praetorian Guard and the Vitellianist soldiers
4:46 In the middle of this, a mutiny in the army nearly broke out. Men dismissed by Vitellius who had rallied for Vespasian wanted the Praetorian service back, and soldiers picked from the legions with the same hope demanded the pay they had been promised. The Vitellianists couldn't be got rid of without a great deal of bloodshed either. But keeping so many men would cost an enormous sum. Mucianus went into the camp to look more closely at each man's service. He drew up the victorious army with their proper decorations and weapons, spaced a little apart. Then the Vitellianists, whose surrender at Bovillae I've already described, and others collected in the city and its surroundings, were brought out almost naked. Mucianus ordered them to be separated, and the German and British soldiers, and any from other armies, to stand apart by themselves. The first sight stunned them. Opposite them was what looked like a battle line bristling with spears and weapons, and they saw themselves shut in, unarmed and filthy. When they began to be pulled this way and that, fear went through them all, and the German soldiers especially were terrified, as if this separation marked them for slaughter. They grabbed their comrades by the chest, hung on their necks, begged for last kisses, and asked not to be abandoned alone or made to suffer a different fate for the same cause. They called on Mucianus, then on the absent emperor, and finally on heaven and the gods, until Mucianus came forward, called them all soldiers bound by the same oath and serving the same emperor, and met their false fear. The victorious army even backed the tears of the beaten men with shouts. That ended the day. A few days later, when Domitian spoke to them, they received him with new confidence. They scorned the land offered to them and begged for service and pay. These were prayers, but ones that couldn't be refused. So they were taken into the Praetorian camp. Then those of the right age or with enough years served were discharged with honor, and others were dismissed for misconduct, but one at a time and in small batches, the safest remedy for weakening the agreement of a crowd.
4:47 It's a fact that, whether out of real poverty or to look poor, the Senate voted to accept a loan of sixty million sesterces from private persons, and Pompeius Silvanus was put in charge of it. Not long after, the need went away, or the pretense was dropped. Next, on Domitian's motion, the consulships Vitellius had given out were canceled, and a censor's funeral was held for Flavius Sabinus. Both were great lessons in how unstable fortune is, always mixing the highest honors with the lowest disgrace.
## Lucius Piso in Africa
4:48 About this time the proconsul Lucius Piso was killed. I'll tell this killing as accurately as I can, by going back a little to the beginning to cover a few earlier points that bear on the causes of such deeds. Under the divine Augustus and Tiberius, the legion and the auxiliaries in Africa, guarding the empire's borders, were under the proconsul. Later Caligula, restless by nature and afraid of Marcus Silanus, who then governed Africa, took the legion away from the proconsul and gave it to a legate he sent for that purpose. The two shared patronage equally, and with their orders mixed together, rivalry was sought out and made worse by an ugly contest. The legates' standing grew with the length of their terms, or maybe because the lesser man cares more about such a contest. The best of the proconsuls looked to their safety more than to power.
4:49 At that time Valerius Festus commanded the legion in Africa, a young man of extravagant habits and wild ambition, who was also nervous because of his family tie to Vitellius. Whether in their frequent talks he tempted Piso to revolt, or Piso turned down his offers, is unknown, since no one was present at their private meetings, and after Piso was killed, most people leaned toward flattering the killer. There's no doubt that the province and the soldiers were hostile to Vespasian. Some Vitellianists who had fled the city kept telling Piso that the Gauls were wavering and Germany was ready, that he was in danger himself, and that for a man suspected even in peacetime, war was the safer course. Meanwhile Claudius Sagitta, prefect of the Petrian cavalry wing, got there first on a quick voyage, ahead of the centurion Papirius, whom Mucianus had sent. Sagitta told Piso that an order to kill him had been given to the centurion, that his cousin and son-in-law Galerianus was dead, and that his only hope of safety was bold action. There were two ways to dare it. He could take up arms at once, or he could sail to Gaul and offer himself as leader to the Vitellianist armies. Piso wasn't moved by any of this. The centurion sent by Mucianus, when he reached the port of Carthage, shouted joyful greetings to Piso in a loud voice, as if to an emperor, and urged those who met him, stunned by the strangeness of it, to shout the same. The gullible crowd rushed to the forum and demanded to see Piso. They threw everything into confusion with joy and shouting, careless about the truth and eager to flatter. Piso, either on Sagitta's warning or from natural modesty, did not go out in public and did not trust himself to the crowd's enthusiasm. He questioned the centurion, found that he had been looking for a charge against him and a way to kill him, and had him executed, less out of hope of saving his life than out of anger at the killer. The man was one of the murderers of Clodius Macer, and had come back to kill the proconsul with hands still bloody from the legate's blood. Then, in an anxious edict, he scolded the people of Carthage, and he stopped even his usual duties, shutting himself up in his house in case some cause of new disturbance arose by chance.
4:50 But when Festus learned of the crowd's uproar, the centurion's execution, and the reports true and false, exaggerated as usual by rumor, he sent cavalry to kill Piso. They rode hard and broke into the proconsul's house in the half-light of early dawn with swords drawn. Most of them didn't know Piso by sight, since Festus had picked Carthaginian and Moorish auxiliaries for the killing. Not far from the bedroom they met a slave by chance and asked who he was and where Piso was. The slave gave a noble lie, saying he was Piso, and was cut down at once. Soon after, Piso himself was killed, since there was someone there who recognized him, Baebius Massa, one of the procurators of Africa. His was a name already deadly to good men even then, and one that would often come back among the causes of the troubles we soon suffered. Festus had waited at Adrumetum to see how it turned out. Then he hurried to the legion and ordered Cetronius Pisanus, the camp prefect, to be put in chains. It was because of private quarrels, but he called the man Piso's henchman. He punished some soldiers and centurions and rewarded others, neither on merit, but so that people would believe he had crushed a war. Then he settled the quarrels between the people of Oeea and the Leptitani. These had begun small, with raids on grain and cattle between country people, but by now they were being fought with armies and battle lines. The people of Oeea, outnumbered, had called in the Garamantes, an untamed tribe that was rich in robbery among its neighbors. That put the Leptitani in a tight spot. Their fields were ravaged far and wide, and they were trembling inside their walls, until cohorts and cavalry wings arrived and scattered the Garamantes and recovered all the loot, except what the raiders, roaming through unreachable hamlets, had sold to tribes further away.
## Vespasian, Titus and Domitian
4:51 After the battle of Cremona and good news from every side, many people of every rank reached Vespasian with news that Vitellius had fallen, having braved the winter sea with equal daring and luck. Envoys of King Vologesus were there, offering forty thousand Parthian horsemen. It made him proud and glad to be courted with such help from allies and not need it. Vologesus was thanked and told to send envoys to the Senate and to know that peace had come. Vespasian, with his attention on Italy and the city, heard an unfavorable report of Domitian, that he was overstepping the limits of his age and what a son was allowed. So he handed the strongest part of the army to Titus to finish what was left of the Jewish war.
4:52 They say Titus, before leaving, talked at length with his father, begging him not to be easily set off by the reports of accusers, and to stay open-minded and forgiving toward his son. Legions and fleets, he said, are not as sure a defense for imperial power as a large family. Friends are worn down, shifted and lost by time, by changes in fortune, and sometimes by their own desires or mistakes. But a man's own blood can't be separated from him, least of all a ruler's, whose successes others share in, while his troubles touch only those closest to him. Even brothers won't keep up harmony unless the parent sets the example. Vespasian was delighted by Titus's brotherly feeling, though he was not reconciled to Domitian. He told his son to be of good cheer and to build the state up through war and arms, while he himself would see to peace and the family. Then he loaded the fastest ships with grain and sent them out on the still rough sea. In fact the city was in such a dangerous state that it had no more than ten days' grain in the storehouses when Vespasian's supplies came to the rescue.
## The Capitol is rebuilt
4:53 He gave the job of rebuilding the Capitol to Lucius Vestinius, a Roman Knight but of such standing and reputation that he ranked among the leading men. The soothsayers he called together advised that the remains of the old shrine be carried off to the marshes and the temple be set up on the same footprint. The gods did not want the old form changed. On the 21st of June, in clear weather, the whole space set aside for the temple was wrapped in ribbons and garlands. Soldiers with lucky names went in carrying branches of good omen. Then the Vestal Virgins, with boys and girls whose fathers and mothers were both living, washed the place with water drawn from springs and rivers. Then Helvidius Priscus the praetor, with Plautius Aelianus the pontiff reciting the words for him, purified the area with a sacrifice of a pig, a sheep and a bull, laid the entrails on a turf altar, and prayed to Jupiter, Juno, Minerva and the gods who guard the empire to prosper the work and lift up, with divine help, the homes that human devotion had begun for them. He touched the ribbons that bound the stone and the ropes tied to it. At the same moment the other magistrates, the priests, the Senate, the Knights and a large part of the people, pulling together with eagerness and joy, dragged the huge stone. Offerings of silver and gold and the first fruits of metals, never melted in a furnace but just as they come from the earth, were thrown into the foundations everywhere. The soothsayers had warned that the work must not be defiled by stone or gold meant for another use. They made the building taller. That was the only change religion allowed, and it was thought to be what the old temple's splendor had lacked.
## Gaul thinks Rome is finished; the plot of Classicus, Tutor and Sabinus
4:54 Meanwhile the news of Vitellius's death, spreading through Gaul and Germany, had doubled the war. Civilis dropped the disguise and rushed against the Roman people, and the Vitellianist legions preferred even foreign slavery to Vespasian as emperor. The Gauls had taken heart, thinking the Roman armies were doing badly everywhere. A rumor spread that the winter camps in Moesia and Pannonia were besieged by the Sarmatae and Dacians, and equally false stories were invented about Britain. But nothing had pushed them so much as the burning of the Capitol, to believe that the end of the empire was at hand. The city had once been captured by the Gauls, but because Jupiter's seat was untouched, the empire had survived. Now, said the Druids with the empty superstition of prophets, the deadly fire was a sign of heaven's anger and foretold that mastery of human affairs would pass to the peoples beyond the Alps. Another rumor had gone around that the leading men of Gaul, sent by Otho against Vitellius, had agreed before they left not to fail the cause of freedom, if the Roman people were broken by an unbroken string of civil wars and troubles at home.
4:55 Before the murder of Hordeonius Flaccus, nothing had come out that would show a conspiracy. After he was killed, messengers went back and forth between Civilis and Classicus, the commander of the Treveri cavalry wing. Classicus stood above all others in rank and wealth. He was of a royal line, with an origin famous in peace and war, and he boasted that his ancestors had been enemies of the Roman people rather than allies. Julius Tutor and Julius Sabinus joined him, one a Treveran, the other a Lingonian. Tutor had been put in charge of the Rhine bank by Vitellius. Sabinus was vain by nature and swelled up over an imagined family tie. He claimed his great-grandmother had pleased the divine Julius, then campaigning in Gaul, in an adulterous affair. Tutor and Sabinus held secret meetings to sound out the rest of their countrymen. Once they had picked up accomplices they thought suitable and bound them with their knowledge of the plot, they met in a private house in Colonia Agrippinensis, since the city's official policy was against any such plot. Still, some of the Ubii and Tungri took part. But most of the weight lay with the Treveri and Lingones, and they couldn't stand the delay of discussion. They competed in shouting that the Roman people were raging with quarrels, that the legions had been cut down, that Italy was devastated, that Rome itself was being captured at that very moment, and that every army was tied up in its own wars. If the Alps were secured with garrisons, they said, Gaul, with her freedom firmly established, could then decide how far she wanted her power to reach.
4:56 These views were stated and approved together. About the survivors of the Vitellianist army, they were unsure. Most thought they should be killed: turbulent, disloyal men with the blood of their generals on their hands. But the argument for sparing them won, so that taking away all hope of pardon wouldn't stir up stubborn resistance. It would be better to draw them into the alliance. If only the legates of the legions were killed, the rest of the crowd, aware of their guilt and hoping to go unpunished, would easily join. That was the outline of their first plan. Agents were sent all over Gaul to stir up war, while they themselves pretended to obey so they could crush Vocula when he wasn't expecting it. Some people did warn Vocula, but he lacked the strength to put the movement down, with the legions under strength and disloyal. Among doubtful soldiers and hidden enemies, he thought the best choice available was to answer pretense with pretense and to use the same tricks that were being used against him, and he went down to Colonia Agrippinensis. There Claudius Labeo, who, as I said, had been captured and sent out of the way to the Frisii, escaped by bribing his guards. He promised that if he were given a force, he would go into Batavian territory and win back the more influential part of that nation to the Roman alliance. He got a small force of infantry and cavalry, but he didn't dare try anything against the Batavians. He drew some of the Nervii and Betasii into arms and raided the Canninefates and Marsaci, more like a thief than a soldier.
## Vocula's last speech and death
4:57 Vocula was lured on by the Gauls' trickery and marched against the enemy. He was not far from the Old Camp when Classicus and Tutor, who had gone ahead on the pretext of scouting, made agreements with the German leaders. Then for the first time they split off from the legions and fenced their own camp with its own rampart. Vocula pleaded that Rome had not been so shaken by civil wars that even the Treveri and Lingones could look down on it. There were still loyal provinces, victorious armies, the empire's good fortune and avenging gods. That, he said, was how Sacrovir and the Aedui in the old days, and Vindex and the Gauls more recently, were crushed in single battles. The same powers and the same fate awaited those who broke treaties. Julius and Augustus understood these people far better, he said. It was Galba, and the lowering of their tribute, that put hostility into them. Now they were enemies because slavery was easy on them. When they had been plundered and stripped, they would be friends. After this defiant speech, seeing that Classicus and Tutor kept at their treachery, he changed course and withdrew to Novesium. The Gauls camped in the fields two miles away. Centurions and soldiers who went back and forth there were bought off with bribes, to make a Roman army commit the unheard-of disgrace of swearing loyalty to foreigners, and to give as a pledge of so great a crime the death or chains of its legates. Though most people advised Vocula to run away, he thought he should dare to stay, and he called an assembly and spoke to this effect.
4:58 "I've never spoken to you more worried for you or less worried for myself. I'm glad to hear that destruction is being prepared for me, and amid so many evils I look to death at the enemy's hands as the end of my miseries. For you, I feel shame and pity. No enemy battle line is being drawn up against you. That would at least be lawful war, an enemy's right. Instead Classicus hopes to wage his war against the Roman people with your hands, and he shows off to you an empire and an oath of the Gauls. If our luck and courage have failed us for the moment, have the old examples failed us too, all the times Roman legions chose to die rather than be driven from their post? Our allies have often endured having their cities destroyed and being burned with their wives and children, with nothing to show for their end but loyalty and reputation. Right now the legions at the Old Camp are enduring hunger and siege, and they can't be shaken by threats or promises. We have, besides weapons and men and the excellent defenses of the camp, grain and supplies enough for a war however long. Lately we had money enough even for a bonus, and whether you prefer to say it came from Vespasian or Vitellius, you at least received it from a Roman emperor. You won so many wars and routed the enemy so many times at Gelduba and at the Old Camp. If you fear a battle line, that is unworthy of you, but there is the rampart and the walls and ways to drag things out until help and armies come running from the nearby provinces. Say I displease you. There are other legates, tribunes, even a centurion or a common soldier. Don't let this monstrous news spread through the whole world, that Civilis and Classicus will invade Italy with you as their escort. If Germans and Gauls lead you to the walls of the city, will you take up arms against your country? My soul shudders at the picture of such a crime. Will guard duty be stood for Tutor the Treveran? Will a Batavian give the signal for battle? Will you fill out the ranks of the German bands? And what will be the end of your crime when Roman legions are lined up against you? Will you wander as deserters from deserters and traitors from traitors, hated by the gods, between a new oath and an old? You, Jupiter, best and greatest, to whom we have paid the honor of so many triumphs for eight hundred and twenty years, and you, Quirinus, father of the city of Rome, I pray and beg: if it wasn't your will for this camp to be kept clean and untouched under my command, at least do not let it be defiled and made foul by Tutor and Classicus. Give the Roman soldiers either innocence, or a quick repentance that does no harm."
4:59 His speech was received in different ways, between hope, fear and shame. Vocula withdrew and was thinking about his last act, when his freedman and his slave stopped him from getting ahead of a most shameful death by his own hand. Classicus sent Aemilius Longinus, a deserter from the 1st legion, and hurried on his murder. For the legates Herennius and Numisius, chains were thought enough. Then Classicus put on the insignia of Roman imperial power and entered the camp. Though hardened to every crime, he could find no more words than it took to recite the oath. Those present swore loyalty to the empire of the Gauls. He promoted Vocula's killer to high rank, and rewarded the others according to how each had served the crime. Then the management of the work was divided between Tutor and Classicus. Tutor surrounded the Agrippinenses with a strong force and made them, and all the troops along the upper bank of the Rhine, take the same oath, after killing the tribunes at Mogontiacum and driving away the camp prefect, who had refused. Classicus told the most corrupt of the surrendered men to go to the besieged, offering pardon if they went along with the present situation. Otherwise, there was no hope, and they would suffer hunger, the sword and the worst. The men he sent added their own example.
## The Old Camp surrenders and is attacked
4:60 Loyalty on one side and hunger on the other pulled the besieged between honor and disgrace. While they hesitated, ordinary and unusual food ran out. They had used up their pack animals, horses and the other animals that are unclean and foul, which necessity turned to use. Finally they pulled up shrubs, roots and the grass growing between stones, and gave an example of misery and endurance, until they stained their splendid fame with a shameful ending: they sent envoys to Civilis begging for their lives. Their pleas weren't heard until they swore allegiance to the Gauls. Then Civilis made a deal for the loot of the camp and gave them guards to hold back the money, the camp servants and the baggage, and to escort the men themselves as they left with nothing to carry. About five miles out, the Germans rose up and attacked the column, which had no idea. The most warlike were killed where they stood, and many died scattered in flight. The rest ran back into the camp. Civilis complained, and scolded the Germans for breaking their word by this crime. Whether this was pretense, or he couldn't hold back their rage, isn't certain. They looted the camp and threw torches into it, and the fire swallowed everyone who had survived the battle.
4:61 Civilis fulfilled a vow of the kind barbarians make: the hair he had let grow long and had dyed red from the day he took up arms against the Romans, he cut off now that the slaughter of the legions was finished. It was also reported that he handed over some of the prisoners to his small son to shoot with a child's arrows and javelins. He neither swore the oath of the Gauls himself nor made any Batavian swear it, relying on the strength of Germany and, if he had to fight the Gauls over who would have mastery, thinking he'd come out ahead through his famous name and greater power. Munius Lupercus, legate of a legion, was sent to Veleda among the gifts. She was a maiden of the Bructeri tribe who ruled a wide territory. The Germans have an old custom of treating many women as prophetesses and, as the superstition grows, as goddesses. At that time Veleda's authority was at its height, since she had predicted the Germans' success and the destruction of the legions. But Lupercus was killed on the road. A few centurions and tribunes born in Gaul were kept as a pledge of the alliance. The winter camps of the cohorts, cavalry wings and legions were torn down and burned, except those at Mogontiacum and Vindonissa.
## The legions march out in shame
4:62 The 16th legion, with the auxiliaries who had surrendered at the same time, was ordered to move from Novesium to the colony of the Treveri, with a set day by which they had to leave the camp. They spent the whole time in between in anxious thoughts. The most cowardly trembled, remembering the men cut down at the Old Camp. The better ones blushed with shame and disgrace. They said: "What a march this will be! Who will lead us on the road? Everything is in the power of those we've made masters of our life and death." Others, with no concern for their disgrace, tied money and their dearest things to their bodies, and some got their weapons ready and strapped on their gear as if for battle. While they were thinking this over, the hour to leave came, gloomier than they had expected. Inside the rampart their ugliness hadn't been so noticeable. The open plain and daylight exposed their shame. The images of the emperors had been torn down, the standards were carried without their usual honors, and the Gauls' flags glittered on every side. The column moved on in silence like a long funeral procession. Their leader was Claudius Sanctus, who had lost an eye, was hideous in face, and was even weaker in mind. Their disgrace seemed doubled when the other legion, leaving the camp at Bonna, joined them. When the news of the captured legions spread, everyone who a short time before had trembled at the name of Rome came running out of fields and houses and spilled out everywhere, enjoying the unusual sight far too much. The Picentine cavalry wing couldn't stand the gloating of the jeering crowd, and, ignoring Sanctus's promises and threats, rode off to Mogontiacum. By chance they met Longinus, the killer of Vocula, and hit him with a shower of javelins, making a start at paying off their guilt for the future. The legions didn't change their route and camped before the walls of the Treveri.
## Colonia Agrippinensis: spared or destroyed
4:63 Elated by their success, Civilis and Classicus wondered whether to let their armies plunder Colonia Agrippinensis. Their savage temper and greed for loot pulled them toward destroying the city. Against that stood the reasoning of war and the usefulness of a reputation for mercy for men starting a new empire. Civilis was also swayed by the memory of a kindness: his son, arrested in Colonia Agrippinensis at the first movement of the revolt, had been held in honorable custody. But the tribes from across the Rhine hated the city for its wealth and growth. They thought the only end to the war would be to make that place open to all Germans, or to tear it down and scatter the Ubii as well.
4:64 Next the Tencteri, a tribe separated from the colony by the Rhine, sent envoys with orders to deliver their message to the council of the Agrippinenses. The boldest of the envoys spoke it this way: "We thank our shared gods, and Mars above all the gods, that you've come back into the body and name of Germany, and we congratulate you that at last you'll be free among the free. Up to today the Romans have shut off rivers and land and, in a way, the very sky, to keep us from talking and meeting. Or, even more insulting to men born for arms, we had to meet unarmed and nearly naked, under a guard and for a fee. But so that our friendship and alliance will last forever, we demand that you pull down the walls of your colony, which are the fortresses of slavery. (Even wild animals forget their courage if you keep them penned up.) We demand that you slaughter all Romans in your territory. (Freedom and masters don't mix easily.) Let the property of the dead go into a common pile, so no one can hide anything or separate his own cause from ours. Let us and you live on both banks, as our ancestors once did. As nature has opened light and day to all men, so she has opened every land to brave men. Take up again the customs and way of life of your fathers, and break off the pleasures that give the Romans more power over their subjects than weapons do. As a pure, unmixed people who have forgotten slavery, you will either live as equals or rule over others."
4:65 The Agrippinenses took time to think it over. Fear of the future wouldn't let them accept the terms, and their present condition wouldn't let them openly refuse, so they answered like this: "We grabbed the first chance at freedom more eagerly than carefully, so that we could join you and the other Germans, our blood relatives. As for the walls of our city, with Roman armies gathering right now, it's safer for us to strengthen them than to tear them down. Any foreigners from Italy or the provinces who were in our territory were wiped out by the war, or ran back each to his own home. As for those settled here long ago and joined with us by marriage, and those born since, this is their homeland. We don't think you so unfair as to want us to kill our parents, brothers and children. We cancel the taxes and burdens on trade. Let crossings be unguarded, but by day and unarmed, until these new and recent rights turn into custom with age. We will take Civilis and Veleda as judges between us, and with their approval the treaty will be made." The Tencteri were calmed by this. Envoys carrying gifts were sent to Civilis and Veleda, and they settled everything the way the Agrippinenses wanted. But the envoys weren't allowed to see Veleda or speak to her. They were kept from seeing her so that there would be more reverence. She lived in a tall tower, and one of her relatives, chosen for the job, carried the questions and answers back and forth like a messenger of a god.
4:66 Strengthened by the alliance of the Agrippinenses, Civilis decided to win over the neighboring states or make war on those that resisted. He took the Sunuci and organized their young men into cohorts. To stop him from going further, Claudius Labeo stood against him with a hastily raised force of Betasii, Tungri and Nervii, relying on his position, since he had seized the bridge over the river Mosa ahead of time. The fighting in the narrow pass was undecided until Germans swam across and attacked Labeo from behind. At the same moment Civilis, whether on a sudden bold impulse or by plan, threw himself into the column of the Tungri and shouted: "We didn't take up arms so that the Batavians and Treveri could rule over nations. Such arrogance is far from us. Accept our alliance. I'm crossing over to you, as your general or as one of your soldiers, whichever you prefer." The crowd was moved and began sheathing their swords, when Campanus and Juvenalis, two leading Tungri, handed the whole tribe over to him. Labeo fled before he could be surrounded. Civilis also took the Betasii and Nervii under his protection and added them to his forces. He now commanded huge resources, since the states were either shaken or leaning his way on their own.
## Sabinus fails; Gaul debates among the Remi
4:67 Meanwhile Julius Sabinus threw away the monuments of the treaty with Rome, had himself hailed as Caesar, and drove a huge, disorganized crowd of his countrymen against the Sequani, a neighboring state loyal to Rome. The Sequani didn't shrink from the fight. Fortune sided with the better cause, and the Lingones were beaten. Sabinus ran from the battle he had rushed into so recklessly, with equal fear. To spread the word that he was dead, he set fire to the country house he'd taken refuge in. People believed he died there by his own hand. How he hid and stayed alive for nine years, his friends' steady loyalty, and the fine example of his wife Epponina, I'll tell in the right place. With the Sequani's success in battle, the push of the war stopped. The Gallic states gradually came back to their senses and began to look to what is right and to treaties. The Remi took the lead, and announced across Gaul that delegates should be sent to a common assembly to decide whether freedom or peace was what they wanted.
4:68 At Rome, everything was heard in a worse light, and it troubled Mucianus that the generals, though excellent men (he had by now chosen Annius Gallus and Petilius Cerialis), would not be strong enough to carry the weight of the whole war. And the city could not be left without a ruler. Domitian's unbridled desires were feared, and Primus Antonius and Arrius Varus were under suspicion, as I said. Varus, who commanded the Praetorian Guard, still kept power and weapons. Mucianus removed him from his post and, so that he wouldn't be left without consolation, made him head of the grain supply. To soften Domitian's mind, which was not unfriendly to Varus, he put Arrecinus Clemens in charge of the Praetorian Guard. Clemens was tied to Vespasian's house by marriage and was very dear to Domitian. Mucianus kept saying that Clemens's father had filled that post with distinction under Caligula, that the same name would please the soldiers, and that Clemens himself, though of senatorial rank, was up to both jobs. The most distinguished men of the city were chosen to go along, and others through influence. At the same time Domitian and Mucianus were getting ready, in different spirits. Domitian was in a hurry, full of hope and youth, and Mucianus kept making delays to hold back his fiery companion, afraid that if he got to the army, the wildness of youth and bad advisers might wreck the chances for both war and peace. The victorious 8th, 11th and 13th legions, the 21st from the Vitellianist army, and the 2nd, made of new recruits, were marched into Gaul, some over the Pennine and Cottian Alps and some over the Graian. The 14th legion was called from Britain, and the 6th and 1st from Spain. At the news of the approaching army, and from their own leaning toward gentler counsels, the Gallic states met in the territory of the Remi. The envoys of the Treveri were waiting for them there, led by Julius Valentinus, the fiercest promoter of the war. In a prepared speech he poured out everything usually said against great empires, with many insults and hostile remarks against the Roman people. He was a troublemaker, good at stirring up revolts, and with his raving eloquence he pleased many.
4:69 On the other side, Julius Auspex, one of the leading men of the Remi, spoke about the power of Rome and the blessings of peace. He pointed out that cowards may start a war, but the bravest men have to risk their lives fighting it, and that the Roman legions were already almost overhead. He held back the most sensible by appealing to respect and loyalty, and the younger men with warnings of danger and fear. So they praised Valentinus's spirit but followed Auspex's advice. It is agreed that the Treveri and Lingones were hurt in the eyes of the Gauls by having sided with Verginius when Vindex rose up. Many were put off by the rivalry between the provinces. They asked where the head of the war would be, where they would get the right and the auspices, and which seat of power they would choose if everything went well. There was no victory yet, but already there was discord. Some boasted in quarrels about their treaties, some about their wealth and strength, others about the age of their origins. Sick of the future, they settled for the present. Letters were written to the Treveri in the name of the states of Gaul, telling them to lay down their arms. Pardon could be had, they said, and intercessors were ready if they repented. Valentinus stood in the way again and blocked his state's ears, though he was not as busy getting ready for war as he was making speeches.
## Tutor is beaten; Cerialis arrives
4:70 So neither the Treveri nor the Lingones nor the other rebel states acted in proportion to the danger they had taken on. Even their leaders didn't plan together. Civilis was going around the trackless wilds of the Belgae, trying to capture Claudius Labeo or drive him out. Classicus spent most of his time in lazy leisure, enjoying an empire as though it were already won. Even Tutor was slow to close the upper bank of the Rhine and the steep passes of the Alps with garrisons. Meanwhile the 21st legion, by way of Vindonissa, and Sextilius Felix with auxiliary cohorts, by way of Raetia, broke into the province. They were joined by the Singularian cavalry wing, raised earlier by Vitellius and then gone over to Vespasian's side. Its commander was Julius Briganticus, the son of Civilis's sister, hated by his uncle and hating him back, with the bitterness of a family feud that is usual among close relatives. Tutor strengthened the Treveri's forces, swelled by a fresh draft of Vangiones, Caeracates and Triboci, with veteran infantry and cavalry, legionaries he had corrupted with hope or beaten down with fear. At first they killed a cohort that Sextilius Felix had sent ahead. Soon afterward, when the Roman generals and army drew near, they returned to their duty by an honorable desertion, and the Triboci, Vangiones and Caeracates followed them. Tutor, with the Treveri following, avoided Mogontiacum and withdrew to Bingium, trusting in the position, since he had broken down the bridge over the river Nava. But the cohorts Sextilius led attacked, a ford was found, and Tutor was betrayed and beaten. The Treveri were stunned by this blow, and the common people threw down their weapons and scattered through the fields. Some of the leading men, to look like the first to lay down the war, fled to the states that hadn't given up the Roman alliance. The legions that had been moved from Novesium and Bonna into the territory of the Treveri, as I said earlier, swore allegiance to Vespasian on their own. This happened while Valentinus was away. When he came back in a rage, set on throwing everything into chaos and ruin again, the legions withdrew to the Mediomatrici, a friendly state. Valentinus and Tutor dragged the Treveri back into war, and killed the legates Herennius and Numisius, so that with less hope of pardon, the bond of crime would grow stronger.
4:71 That was the state of the war when Petilius Cerialis reached Mogontiacum. His arrival raised hopes. He was eager for battle and better at despising enemies than guarding against them. He set the soldiers on fire with his fierce words, promising that as soon as it was possible to meet the enemy, he would make no delay for battle. He sent the Gallic recruits back to their states with orders to announce that the legions were enough to defend the empire, and that the allies could go back to the duties of peace, secure as if the war were over now that Roman hands had taken it on. This increased the Gauls' obedience. With their young men given back, they bore the tribute more easily, and since they were looked down on, they were readier to do their duty. But when Civilis and Classicus heard that Tutor had been beaten, the Treveri cut down and everything going the enemy's way, they were alarmed and hurried to gather their scattered forces, and meanwhile warned Valentinus again and again by messengers not to risk the whole outcome on a battle. That made Cerialis move faster. He sent men to the Mediomatrici to direct the legions there against the enemy by a shorter route. He gathered the soldiers at Mogontiacum and those he had brought with him, and in three days' march came to Rigodulum. Valentinus had occupied the place with a large force of Treveri. It was protected by mountains and the river Mosella, and he had added ditches and barricades of stones. These defenses didn't deter the Roman commander from ordering his infantry to break in and sending his cavalry up the hill. He despised an enemy that had been gathered in a hurry and, he judged, was helped by the position less than his own men's courage would outweigh. There was a short delay on the climb while the enemy's missiles flew over. When it came to hand-to-hand fighting, the enemy was knocked loose and thrown headlong like a collapsing building. A part of the cavalry rode around by easier ridges and captured the most noble of the Belgae, among them the leader, Valentinus.
## Cerialis in the colony of the Treveri
4:72 The next day Cerialis entered the colony of the Treveri, with his soldiers eager to wipe the city out. They said: "This is the homeland of Classicus and of Tutor. It was through the crime of these men that the legions were trapped and cut down. What had Cremona done to deserve such a fate? Cremona was torn out of the heart of Italy just because it delayed the winners one night. Here, on the border of Germany, a city stands unharmed, celebrating over the spoils of armies and the murder of generals. Let the loot go into the treasury. We're satisfied with the fire and the ruins of a rebel colony, to pay for the destruction of so many camps." Cerialis, afraid of the shame of being thought to have taught his soldiers license and cruelty, held back their anger. They obeyed: with civil war over, they were more restrained toward foreigners. Their thoughts were then turned by the pitiful sight of the legions summoned from the Mediomatrici. They stood sad with the knowledge of their disgrace, eyes fixed on the ground. When the armies met there were no friendly greetings. The legions gave no answer to those who tried to comfort or encourage them, hid in their tents, and avoided the very daylight. It wasn't so much danger or fear that had stunned them as shame and disgrace. Even the victorious soldiers were stunned. They didn't dare speak or plead aloud, but asked for pardon with tears and silence, until Cerialis soothed their minds. He kept saying that what had happened was driven by fate, through the quarrels of soldiers and generals or the enemy's trickery. They should count that day as the first of their service and their oath. He said that neither the emperor nor he would remember their earlier crimes. Then they were taken into the same camp, and an order went out to every company that no soldier was to throw mutiny or defeat in a comrade's face during an argument or quarrel.
## Cerialis speaks to the Treveri and Lingones
4:73 Next he called the Treveri and Lingones to an assembly and spoke to them like this: "I've never practiced eloquence. I've proved the courage of the Roman people with weapons. But since words count for the most with you, and you judge good and bad not by their own nature but by the words of troublemakers, I've decided to say a few things that, now that the war is beaten, it will do you more good to have heard than it does me to have said. Roman generals and emperors came into your land and the rest of Gaul with no greed. They came because your ancestors called on them, worn down by quarrels that went all the way to ruin, and because the Germans, brought in to help, had put slavery on friend and enemy alike. How many battles we fought against the Cimbri and Teutones, how hard our armies worked, and with what outcome we've handled the German wars is well enough known. We didn't settle on the Rhine to protect Italy, but so that no second Ariovistus would seize the kingdom of Gaul. Do you think you are dearer to Civilis and the Batavians and the tribes from across the Rhine than your fathers and grandfathers were to their ancestors? The Germans have always had the same reason to cross into Gaul: lust, greed, and a love of changing homes, so that they leave their marshes and wastelands and take possession of this rich soil and of you. For the rest, freedom and fine-sounding names are used as pretexts. No one has ever wanted someone else's slavery and rule for himself without using those very words."
4:74 "Gaul always had kings and wars until you came under our law. Though we've been provoked so often, we used the right of victory to add to your burden only what it takes to protect the peace. For there can be no peace among nations without armies, no armies without pay, and no pay without taxes. Everything else we share equally. You often command our legions yourselves. You rule these provinces and others. Nothing is kept separate or shut off from you. The benefits of praised emperors reach everyone alike, even those far away, while cruel ones fall on those nearby. Put up with the extravagance or greed of your rulers the way you put up with barren seasons, too much rain and other natural evils. There will be vices as long as there are men. But they don't last forever, and better times make up for them. Unless, maybe, you hope for a milder rule under Tutor and Classicus, or think armies to keep out the Germans and Britons will be raised with lower taxes than now. If the Romans were driven out (the gods forbid), what would follow except wars among all the nations? This structure grew together over eight hundred years of good fortune and discipline, and it can't be torn down without destroying those who tear it down. But the greatest danger is yours, since you have gold and wealth, the main causes of wars. So love and honor peace, and the city that we, winners and losers, hold by the same right. Let the lessons of fortune in both forms teach you not to choose stubbornness with ruin over obedience with safety." With words like these he calmed and encouraged them, since they had feared harsher treatment.
## The rebels' war council and the battle at the Treveri colony
4:75 The Treveri were held by the victorious army when Civilis and Classicus sent letters to Cerialis, to this effect: Vespasian, although they were hiding the news, had died. Rome and Italy had been used up by civil war. Mucianus and Domitian were empty names with no strength. If Cerialis wanted the empire of Gaul, they would be content with the borders of their own states. If he preferred battle, they didn't refuse that either. Cerialis gave Civilis and Classicus no answer. He sent the man who brought the letters, and the letters themselves, to Domitian. The enemy came up from all sides with their forces divided. Many blamed Cerialis for letting them join up when he could have cut them off while they were apart. The Roman army surrounded its camp with a ditch and rampart. Before, it had camped there recklessly, undefended.
4:76 Among the Germans there was a fight over plans. Civilis wanted to wait for the tribes from across the Rhine, whose terrifying name would wear down the broken strength of Rome. As for the Gauls, what were they but loot for the winners? And still, the real strength, the Belgae, stood with them, openly or in wish. Tutor insisted that delay only strengthened Rome as armies came together from every side. A legion had crossed over from Britain, others had been summoned from Spain, others were coming from Italy. And these weren't sudden recruits but old soldiers experienced in war. As for the Germans they were hoping for, they couldn't be commanded or ruled, but did everything on a whim. Besides, the money and gifts that were the only way to win them over were more plentiful on the Roman side, and nobody was so set on fighting that he wouldn't take rest over danger when the pay was the same. But if they met the enemy at once, Cerialis had no legions except those from the remnants of the German army, bound by treaties to the Gauls. And the very fact that these troops, against their own expectation, had just routed Valentinus's disorderly band would feed their recklessness and their general's. They would dare again, and they would come into the hands not of an inexperienced youngster who practiced speeches and assemblies more than steel and arms, but of Civilis and Classicus. When they saw them, fear would come back into their minds, the fear of flight and famine and the begging for life that they had done as prisoners so many times. The Treveri and Lingones weren't held in line by goodwill, and they would take up arms again once their fear passed. Classicus settled the difference of opinion by approving Tutor's view, and they carried it out at once.
4:77 The center was given to the Ubii and Lingones, the right wing to the Batavian cohorts, the left to the Bructeri and Tencteri. One part came over the mountains, and the others along the road between the hills and the river Mosella, and they attacked so suddenly that Cerialis, who hadn't spent the night in the camp, was still in his bedroom, in bed, when he heard that the fighting had started and that his men were being beaten. He scolded the messengers for panicking, until the whole disaster was in front of his eyes: the legions' camp broken into, the cavalry routed, and the middle of the bridge over the Mosella, which joins the colony to the land beyond, held by the enemy. Cerialis was calm in the confusion. He held back the fleeing with his own hand, and, throwing himself forward without armor among the missiles, with lucky recklessness and the rush of the bravest men to him, he won back the bridge and held it with a chosen force. Then he went back to the camp and saw the scattered companies of the legions captured at Novesium and Bonna, with few soldiers around the standards and the eagles almost surrounded. Burning with anger, he shouted: "It isn't Flaccus or Vocula you're deserting. There's no treachery here. I've nothing to excuse but that I rashly believed you had forgotten your treaty with the Gauls and remembered your oath to Rome. I'll be counted with Numisius and Herennius, so that all your commanders will have been brought down by their own soldiers or by the enemy. Go, tell Vespasian, or, since they're nearer, Civilis and Classicus, that you left your commander on the battlefield. Legions will come that won't leave me unavenged or you unpunished."
4:78 It was all true, and the tribunes and prefects piled the same reproaches on their men. The troops formed up by cohorts and companies, since the line couldn't open out with the enemy spread everywhere and the fighting going on inside the rampart, with tents and baggage in the way. Tutor, Classicus and Civilis, each in his own place, drove the fight, urging the Gauls on for freedom, the Batavians for glory, the Germans for loot. Everything went the enemy's way until the 21st legion, which had more room than the others, bunched into a tight body, held off the charging enemy, and soon pushed them back. It wasn't without divine help that the winners suddenly changed their minds and turned their backs. They themselves said they had been scared by the sight of cohorts which, scattered at the first charge, were gathering again on the hilltops and looked like new reinforcements. But what stopped the winners was an ugly scramble among themselves for plunder, after they forgot the enemy. Cerialis, who had nearly ruined everything through carelessness, repaired it with firmness. Following his luck, he captured and destroyed the enemy's camp the same day.
## The Agrippinenses strike; Civilis loses ground
4:79 The soldiers weren't given a long rest. The Agrippinenses were begging for help and offering Civilis's wife and sister and Classicus's daughter, left with them as pledges of the alliance. In the meantime they had slaughtered the Germans scattered in their houses. That brought fear and justified pleas for help, before the enemy could rebuild their strength and gather themselves for hope or revenge. For Civilis was also heading that way, not weak at all, with the most ardent of his cohorts still intact, made up of Chauci and Frisii, stationed at Tolbiacum on the Agrippinenses' border. But sad news turned him aside: that cohort had been wiped out by a trick of the Agrippinenses, who put the Germans to sleep with a lavish feast and wine, locked the doors, set the houses on fire, and burned them. At the same time Cerialis came to the rescue by a quick march. Another fear hemmed Civilis in, that the 14th legion, joined by the fleet from Britain, would strike the Batavians where the Ocean surrounds them. But the legion was marched overland by the legate Fabius Priscus into the territory of the Nervii and Tungri, and these states were taken in surrender. The Canninefates attacked the fleet on their own initiative, and the greater part of the ships were sunk or captured. The same Canninefates also routed a crowd of Nervii who had risen on their own to fight for the Romans. Classicus also won a battle against cavalry that Cerialis had sent ahead to Novesium. These losses, small but frequent, tore at the reputation of the recent victory.
## Mucianus and Antonius Primus
4:80 In the same days Mucianus ordered Vitellius's son to be killed, claiming that discord would last unless he put out the seeds of war. And he didn't allow Primus Antonius to be taken among Domitian's companions, uneasy about his popularity with the soldiers and the man's pride, which couldn't stand equals, let alone superiors. Antonius set out to Vespasian and was received not as he had hoped, but without a hostile spirit. The emperor was pulled two ways: on one side the services of Antonius, under whose leadership the war had without doubt been finished, on the other the letters of Mucianus. At the same time the others went after him as hostile and puffed up, adding charges from his earlier life. And he himself didn't fail to stir up offense with his arrogance, going on too much about what he had earned. He called other men cowards, and called Caecina a prisoner and a man who had surrendered. So bit by bit he came to be thought lighter and cheaper, though the friendship remained in appearance.
## Vespasian at Alexandria: the healings
4:81 In those months while Vespasian waited at Alexandria for the regular days of the summer winds and a dependable sea, many wonders happened, showing heaven's favor and a certain leaning of the gods toward Vespasian. A man of the common people of Alexandria, well known for the disease of his eyes, threw himself at the emperor's knees and begged with groans for a cure for his blindness. He did it on the advice of the god Serapis, whom this people, devoted to many superstitions, worships above other gods. He begged the emperor to deign to wet his cheeks and the rims of his eyes with spit from his mouth. Another man, sick in the hand, on the authority of the same god, asked that it be stepped on by the emperor's foot. At first Vespasian laughed at them and brushed them off. They kept at it, and he feared now the reputation of a fool's errand, and now was led to hope by their pleading and the words of the flatterers. In the end he ordered the doctors to judge whether such blindness and weakness could be overcome by human help. The doctors answered in different ways. In the one man, the power of sight hadn't been eaten away and would return if the obstacles were removed. In the other, the joints, which had slipped into a bent position, could be made whole if a healing force were applied. Maybe that was the gods' wish, and the emperor had been chosen as the divine servant. In any case, the glory of a cure would belong to the emperor, and the ridicule of failure to the poor sufferers. So Vespasian, thinking that everything lay open to his fortune and that nothing was beyond belief now, did what he was told, with a cheerful face, while the crowd standing around was on tiptoe. At once the hand was turned back to use, and daylight shone again for the blind man. Those who were present still tell of both, now that there is nothing to gain from lying.
4:82 From this Vespasian conceived a stronger desire to visit the holy seat of the god and ask about the affairs of the empire. He ordered everyone kept out of the temple. He went in and was concentrating on the god when he looked back and saw behind him one of the leading Egyptians, named Basilides, whom he knew very well to be held back by illness, many days' journey from Alexandria. He asked the priests if Basilides had come into the temple that day. He asked people he met if he'd been seen in the city. Finally, he sent horsemen and found out that at that very moment the man had been eighty miles away. Then he took it as a divine appearance, and read the force of the response from the name Basilides.
## How the god Serapis came to Alexandria
4:83 The origin of the god hasn't yet been made well known by Roman writers. The Egyptian priests tell it this way. King Ptolemy, the first of the Macedonians to make Egypt's power secure, was adding walls, temples and rites to the newly founded Alexandria. In a dream a youth appeared to him, of extraordinary beauty and larger than human size. He told him to send his most trusted friends to Pontus and fetch his statue from there. This, he said, would bring the kingdom good fortune, and the city that received it would be great and famous. At that moment Ptolemy saw the same youth lifted into the sky in a blaze of fire. Roused by the sign and the wonder, Ptolemy told the night visions to the Egyptian priests, whose custom is to understand such things. They knew little of Pontus or foreign lands, so he asked Timotheus, an Athenian of the Eumolpid family, whom he had called from Eleusis to be in charge of the sacred rites, what this religion was and which deity it was. Timotheus questioned people who had traveled to Pontus and learned that there was a city there called Sinope, and not far from it a temple long known among the local people as the temple of Jupiter of the underworld. A woman's statue stood beside it, which most people called Proserpina. But Ptolemy, as kings go, was prone to fear, and when his sense of safety came back, he cared more about pleasures than religion. He slowly neglected the matter and turned his mind to other concerns, until the same vision came again, more terrifying and more demanding, and announced ruin for him and his kingdom if his orders were not carried out. Then he ordered envoys and gifts prepared for King Scydrothemis, who was then ruling the people of Sinope, and told them, as they were about to sail, to visit the Pythian Apollo. The sea was kind to them, and Apollo's answer was clear: they should go and bring back the statue of his father, and leave his sister's behind.
4:84 When they reached Sinope, they presented the gifts, requests and instructions of their king to Scydrothemis. He was torn: now dreading the god's power, now frightened by the threats of the opposing people. He was often swayed by the envoys' gifts and promises. Meanwhile, three years passed, and Ptolemy did not stop his eagerness or his pleas. He raised the rank of the envoys, the number of ships and the weight of gold. Then a threatening figure appeared to Scydrothemis, telling him not to delay the god's plans any longer. When he hesitated, all kinds of disasters and sicknesses, and the clear anger of the heavens, getting heavier by the day, wore him down. He called an assembly and laid out the god's orders, his own and Ptolemy's visions, and the troubles closing in. The crowd turned against the king, envied Egypt, feared for themselves, and surrounded the temple. The more widely told story says that the god himself boarded the ships that had been brought up to the shore, of his own will. Strange to say, they crossed so great a stretch of sea and landed at Alexandria on the third day. A temple fit for the size of the city was built at a place called Rhacotis, where a small shrine to Serapis and Isis had stood since long before. This is the most popular account of the god's origin and arrival. I know that some say he was brought from the city of Seleucia in Syria under the Ptolemy of the third generation. Others name the same Ptolemy, but give Memphis as the place he came from, a city once famous and the pillar of ancient Egypt. Many identify the god himself with Aesculapius, because he cures sick bodies, some with Osiris, the oldest god of those peoples, quite a few with Jupiter, as ruler of all things, and most of all with Pluto, going by the emblems visible on his statue, or by guesses.
## Domitian is held back at Lugdunum
4:85 Before Domitian and Mucianus got near the Alps, they received good news of events among the Treveri. The best proof of the victory was the enemy leader Valentinus, who, with his spirit far from broken, showed in his face the pride he had acted with. He was heard only so that his character could be seen, and he was condemned. At his execution, when someone taunted him that his country was captured, he answered that he took it as a comfort in dying. Then Mucianus brought out, as if it were a new idea, a plan he had kept hidden for a long time. Since the enemy's strength had been broken by the kindness of the gods, he said, it would hardly be fitting for Domitian, with the war nearly finished, to step in on someone else's glory. If the stability of the empire or the safety of Gaul were at stake, the Caesar ought to be on the battle line, but the Canninefates and Batavians should be left to lesser commanders. Domitian himself could stay near Lugdunum and show the strength and prestige of imperial power from close by. He shouldn't mix in small risks, and he wouldn't be missing when something bigger came.
4:86 People saw through these tricks, but showing respect meant not catching him at it. So they came to Lugdunum. From there Domitian is believed to have sent secret messages to Cerialis to test his loyalty, asking whether, if Domitian showed up in person, Cerialis would hand over the army and the command. Whether Domitian was thinking of war against his father by this plan, or of gathering money and men against his brother, was uncertain. For Cerialis sidestepped it with careful tact, as the empty, childish wish of a boy. Domitian saw that the older officers looked down on his youth, and dropped even the modest duties of government he had used to carry out. Under a show of simplicity and modesty, he buried himself in secrecy. He pretended to love literature and poetry, to veil his real mind and stay out of his brother's jealous sight. He misread his brother's different and milder nature, taking it the opposite way.
