4:1 Vitellius was dead and the war was over. Peace? Not yet. The winners prowled the city with swords out, hunting the losers with a hatred that would not quit. Bodies choked the streets, and blood soaked the squares and temples, because men were cut down wherever chance put them in the way. Then the soldiers got bolder. They dug out hidden enemies and dragged them into the open. A tall young man? Dead on the spot. Soldier or civilian made no difference. At first, only blood could cool their rage. Soon it turned into greed. Nothing stayed hidden and nothing stayed locked, since Vitellianists might be hiding inside, the soldiers claimed. That is how the break-ins began, and anyone who fought back handed them an excuse to kill. The poorest people and the most worthless slaves came forward to betray their rich masters, and friends turned in friends. Crying and wailing filled every corner, the misery of a captured city. People even began to miss the wild behavior of the Vitellianist and Othonianist soldiers. Once, they had hated it. The party leaders had worked hard to light the fire of civil war, but they could not stop their own troops from wrecking the victory. Bad men are at their best when there is trouble to stir up. Peace and quiet? You can't build those without good character.

4:2 Domitian had the name of Caesar now, and a place in the Caesars' home. Ruling? He wasn't doing it. He played the emperor's son in the worst ways, with sexual misconduct and adultery. Arrius Varus commanded the Praetorian Guard, the emperor's bodyguard troops in Rome. But the real power belonged to Primus Antonius. He was carrying off money and slaves from the emperor's household, like loot from Cremona. Some looting spree. The other generals had been too modest or too unknown to stand out in the war, so they got no rewards either. The country was scared stiff and ready to accept slavery. Meanwhile it kept demanding that Lucius Vitellius and his cohorts (army units) be cut off on their way back from Tarracina. Then the last sparks of the war would die. Cavalry was sent ahead to Aricia. The main body of the legions halted this side of Bovillae. Lucius Vitellius did not wait around. He surrendered himself and his cohorts to the winner's mercy. The soldiers threw down their unlucky weapons, angry and afraid at once. Then came the prisoners, a long line of them, boxed in by armed guards, marching through the city. Not one looked like a man begging for mercy. They were grim and fierce. The crowd cheered and jeered and insulted them, and they didn't flinch. A few tried to break away. The men around them shoved them back, and the rest were locked up. Not one said a single unworthy word. Beaten, yet their name for courage stayed whole. Lucius Vitellius was executed. He was as rotten as his brother, only sharper while his brother ruled. You could say he didn't share his brother's rise so much as get dragged down by his fall.

4:3 Next, Campania. Lucilius Bassus was sent there with some light cavalry to restore order. The towns were restless, but they were quarreling with each other, not defying the new emperor. Soldiers showed up and everything went quiet, and the smaller colonies were let off. At Capua the 3rd legion camped for the winter, and the noble families there took a beating. Tarracina got no help at all. Here's the ugly truth: paying back an injury is far easier than paying back a kindness. Gratitude weighs you down, while revenge pays off. The people of Tarracina found a little comfort in one sight. The slave of Vergilius Capito, the man I've already said betrayed Tarracina, was hanging on a gallows. He still wore the rings of a Roman Knight (the wealthy class just below the senators) that Vitellius had given him. Grim comfort. In Rome the Senate was thrilled, sure that good times were coming. It handed Vespasian every honor emperors usually got. The civil war had started in Gaul and Spain. Then it spread to Germany and Illyricum, and from there it ripped through Egypt, Judea, Syria, and every province and army. Now the whole world seemed cleansed of guilt, and the war looked finished. The Senate got even more excited when a letter from Vespasian arrived. It was written as if the war were still going on. At first glance, it read like that kind of letter. But he sounded like a real emperor: humble about himself, excellent about the state. The senators were not stingy with respect. Vespasian got the consulship, and his son Titus got it with him. Domitian? The praetorship, plus a consul's power. Quite a day for the family.

4:4 Mucianus had sent letters to the Senate too, and people could not stop talking about them. Here is what they said: "If he's a private citizen, why does he talk like a public official? He could have given this same speech a few days later from his own seat in the Senate. His attack on Vitellius came too late, and it's ungenerous. It's also arrogant toward the state, and an insult to the emperor. Mucianus boasts that the imperial power was in his hands and that he handed it over to Vespasian." The senators hid their dislike of Mucianus. Their flattery, though, was wide open. They praised him in glowing words and voted him a triumph. A triumph. For a civil war. The stated excuse was a campaign against the Sarmatae. Primus Antonius got a consul's badges of office, without the job. Arrius Varus and Cornelius Fuscus got praetorian honors. Then the senators remembered the gods and decided the Capitol needed rebuilding. Valerius Asiaticus, the man already chosen to be consul, put all of it forward. Most senators signaled agreement with a look or a raised hand. A few, men of high rank or with a practiced talent for flattery, backed it in polished speeches. Then came Helvidius Priscus, the praetor-elect (picked to be a praetor, not yet in office). He gave an opinion that respected a worthy emperor and had no lies in it. The senators' sympathy swung strongly his way. For Priscus, that day began a big feud. It also began his fame.

4:5 I keep mentioning this man, and I'll keep doing it. So here's a quick look at his life, his interests and his luck. Helvidius Priscus came from the town of Cluviae, in the Carecina region of Italy, and his father had been a top-ranking centurion. While he was still very young, Helvidius aimed his big brain at serious study. Plenty of people hide a lazy life behind a fancy name. We all know the type. Not him. He studied so he'd be ready for public life, and for whatever fortune threw at him. His teachers were philosophers, and their rule was simple: only what is honorable is good, and only what is shameful is bad. Power? Rank? Anything outside the mind? Neither good nor bad, they taught. While he was still a quaestor, Paetus Thrasea picked him as a son-in-law. From his father-in-law he soaked up a love of liberty, more than anything else. Citizen, senator, husband, son-in-law, friend: in every part of life, he was the same man. He looked down on money and stuck to what was right. And danger never shook him.

4:6 Some people said he hungered too much for fame. Honestly, even wise people let go of the desire for glory last. His father-in-law fell, and Helvidius was driven into exile. Then Galba became emperor, and Helvidius came home and moved to prosecute Marcellus Eprius, the man who had informed on Thrasea. Was that payback bigger, or more just? Hard to say. It split the Senate into camps, because if Marcellus went down, a whole crowd of guilty men would fall with him. At first the fight was dangerous, and both men gave brilliant speeches. Then Galba's feelings turned uncertain, and many senators begged him to drop it. So Helvidius did. People reacted the way people always do. Some praised his self-control, and others missed his toughness. Then came the day the Senate voted on Vespasian's imperial powers. It had already been agreed to send envoys to the new emperor, and that set off a sharp fight between Helvidius and Eprius. Helvidius wanted the magistrates to name the envoys one by one, under oath. Marcellus wanted the urn, meaning a drawing of lots, which the consul-elect had proposed too. Here we go again.

4:7 Marcellus pushed hard. Being passed over while other men were picked would look bad, and he couldn't stand it. Bit by bit, the argument got uglier, with long, hostile speeches on both sides. Then Helvidius asked: "Why does Marcellus fear a vote by the magistrates? Money? He has it. A gift for speaking? That too. He'd be ahead of most men, if his crimes weren't weighing him down. A drawing of lots can't show what a man is like. Votes and the Senate's opinion were invented to reach into each man's life and reputation. It serves the country. It honors Vespasian. Let him be met by the men the Senate counts most blameless, men who can fill the emperor's ears with honest advice. Vespasian was a friend of Thrasea, Soranus and Sentius. Their accusers may not be punished yet, but they shouldn't be paraded in front of him. This vote would warn the emperor, in a way, whom to approve and whom to fear. Good friends are the best tool of good government. Marcellus should be satisfied with having driven Nero to destroy so many innocent people. Let him enjoy his rewards and his getting away with it. Vespasian deserves better advisers."

4:8 Marcellus said: "It isn't even my idea you're attacking. The consul-elect made the motion, and it followed old precedent: lots to pick envoys, so nobody could scheme and nobody could settle a grudge. Nothing has happened to make us drop old customs, and the honor due the emperor shouldn't become an insult to anybody. Every senator is fit to pay his respects. What we should avoid is letting some people's stubbornness stir up the emperor's mind, still unsettled by new power. It watches every face and every word. I remember the times I was born into, and the government our fathers and grandfathers set up. I may admire the old days, but I follow the present. I pray for good emperors. Whatever kind we get, I can put up with. My speech didn't bring Thrasea down any more than the Senate's judgment did. Nero's savage temper amused itself through such shows. Friendship with a ruler like that made me as nervous as exile made others. Fine. Let Helvidius match the old Catos and Brutuses for toughness and courage. I'm just one man from a Senate that bowed down together. And here's my advice to Priscus: don't climb above the emperor. Don't lecture Vespasian, either, an old man who has had a triumph and has grown children. The worst emperors want power with no limit, and even the best like some limit on freedom." The speeches flew back and forth, hot on both sides. People took them in with mixed feelings. The side that wanted lots won. Neutral senators worked to keep the old custom. The most respected men leaned that way too, afraid of envy if they were picked themselves. Nobody wanted that spotlight.

4:9 Another fight followed. The Treasury was run by praetors at that time, and they complained that the state was broke. Not good. They asked for a limit on spending. The consul-elect wanted to leave the problem for the emperor, since it was huge and the cure was hard. Helvidius disagreed: the Senate should decide it. The consuls were asking for votes when Vulcatius Tertullinus, a tribune of the people, blocked it. Nothing should be decided on something this big, he said, while the emperor was away. Helvidius had also moved that the Capitol be rebuilt with public money, with Vespasian's help. The more moderate senators let that motion slide past in silence, and later they forgot it. Some didn't.

4:10 Then came a trial. Musonius Rufus went hard at Publius Celer, charging that he had trapped Barea Soranus with false testimony. The case seemed to drag back all the hatred of the days of the informers. But the accused was worthless and obviously guilty, so nobody could protect him. Everybody treated Soranus's memory as sacred. And Celer? He had claimed to be a teacher of philosophy, then testified against Barea. Some teacher. He betrayed the very friendship he claimed to teach, and he corrupted it too. The trial was set for the next day. But people weren't watching Musonius or Publius so much as Helvidius, Marcellus and the others. Their appetite for revenge was awake again.

4:11 Look at the state of things. The Senate was split, the defeated were angry, and the winners had no real authority. The city had no laws and no emperor. Into this mess walked Mucianus. He entered Rome and pulled everything toward himself at once. The power of Primus Antonius and Arrius Varus was broken. Mucianus was furious at them. He kept it off his face, but he hid it badly. The public sniffs out a grudge fast. So it switched sides and moved its loyalty to Mucianus. Only he was courted and flattered, and he played along. Armed men surrounded him. He kept trading one house and garden for another. With his show, his bearing and his guards, he took hold of an emperor's power. He just let the title go. The killing of Calpurnius Galerianus caused the most terror. He was a son of Gaius Piso. He had tried nothing. But his famous name and his good looks as a young man were on everyone's lips. The city was still shaky and hungry for new gossip, and some people linked him with empty rumors of imperial rule. On Mucianus's order, he was put under military guard. His death couldn't be too public in the city itself. So he was taken to the fortieth milestone from Rome on the Appian Road. There he was killed, his veins opened. Julius Priscus had been prefect of the Praetorian cohorts under Vitellius. He killed himself, more out of shame than need. Alfenus Varus outlived the shame of his cowardice. Asiaticus was only a freed slave, and he abused his power. He died a slave's death.

4:12 Rumors out of Germany kept reaching the city, and each day they got worse, though nobody in Rome grieved. Armies slaughtered. Winter camps of the legions captured. The Gauls in revolt. People talked about all of it as if none of it were a disaster. Sure it wasn't. Before I go on, I need to back up. What caused this war? And how big a stir did it make among foreign peoples and Rome's allies? Start with the Batavians. They once lived across the Rhine, as part of the Chatti. A quarrel at home drove them out. They settled the far edge of the Gallic coast, where no farmers lived, and the island right next to it. The Ocean washes the front of that island, and the Rhine wraps around the back and sides. Their resources were never drained, which is rare for the partner of a stronger power. They gave the empire men and weapons, nothing else. They had trained in the German wars for a long time. Then they added to their fame in Britain, where their cohorts (army units) had been sent. By old custom, the noblest men of the tribe led them. At home they also kept a picked cavalry unit with a passion for swimming. These riders could cross the Rhine with weapons and horses and keep their squadrons whole. Not a bad trick.

4:13 Julius Paullus and Julius Civilis came from the royal family. Nobody else in the nation came close to them. Then Fonteius Capito accused Paullus of rebellion, a false charge, and had him killed. Civilis got chains and a trip to Nero. Galba let him go free. Vitellius was another story: the army wanted Civilis executed, and he was in danger all over again. So his anger had plenty of causes, and his hopes came from Rome's misfortunes. He was sharper than most barbarians. He liked to compare himself to Sertorius or Hannibal, because his face was disfigured in a similar way. (Yes, really.) He did not want to be met as an enemy if he broke openly with the Roman people. So he played the friend to Vespasian and acted all zeal for his side. Just an act? Not quite. Primus Antonius had really sent him a letter. Its orders: send back the auxiliary troops Vitellius had called up, and pin the legions down by making a German uprising look likely. Hordeonius Flaccus, who was there in person, had given the same advice. Flaccus leaned toward Vespasian, and he also worried for the empire. Another war, with thousands of armed men pouring into Italy? That meant ruin. Plain ruin.

4:14 So Civilis meant to rebel. For now he buried his bigger plan and would judge the rest as events went. This is how he got started. Vitellius ordered the young Batavian men drafted. The draft was a heavy load already, and the officials made it worse with greed and wild living. Old or weak men? They hunted them down and let them go for a price. Boys who weren't grown yet but were good-looking? They dragged those off for shameful purposes. (Most Batavians are tall even as boys.) Anger boiled, and the organizers of the planned rebellion talked the people into refusing the draft. Civilis invited the chiefs and the boldest commoners to a sacred grove, supposedly for a feast. When night and good cheer had them warm, he began with the glory and renown of their people. Then he listed the wrongs, the plundering and every other evil of slavery. He said: "Allies? That's what we used to be. Now we're treated like slaves. When does a legate (a general who commands a legion) ever visit us? And when one does, he drags along a heavy, arrogant escort. Next we're handed to prefects, who are commanders, and to centurions, who are officers. They stuff themselves on our spoils and our blood. Then they're swapped out, and the search begins for new hiding places for plunder and new names for robbery. Now the draft is on us. It will rip children from their parents and brothers from brothers, as if for the last time. Rome's power has never been weaker. Its winter camps hold nothing but loot and old men. Look up! Quit shaking at the empty names of legions. We have strong infantry and cavalry, we have the Germans, our blood relatives, and we have the Gauls, who want the same thing. Even the Roman people won't mind this war. Fail, and we call it a favor to Vespasian. Win, and nobody has to answer for it."

4:15 Civilis got a roar of approval. He made them all swear by their tribe's rites and the curses used in their oaths. Envoys carried the plan to the Canninefates, who live on part of the island. Their origin, language and courage match the Batavians', but they are fewer. Secret messengers went next to the British auxiliaries and the Batavian cohorts, to win them over. Remember those cohorts? As I told earlier, they had been sent into Germany, and now they were stationed at Mogontiacum. Then there was Brinno, a Canninefate of famous birth and stubborn boldness. His father had dared many hostile acts and had even scorned Caligula's ridiculous expedition, and he got away with it. A family of rebels: that name alone made Brinno popular. The tribe set him on a shield, as their custom was, and swung him up on the bearers' shoulders. That made him leader. Right away he called in the Frisii, a tribe from across the Rhine. Together they broke into the winter camp of two cohorts, the one nearest the sea. The soldiers hadn't seen the attack coming, and even if they had, they couldn't have held it off. The camp was captured and looted. Next the raiders pounced on camp followers and Roman traders, who were wandering around freely, as in peacetime. The forts were next on the list. But the cohort prefects set fire to them, since they couldn't defend them. The standards, the flags and the few soldiers left gathered in the upper part of the island. Aquilius, a top-ranking centurion, commanded them. In name an army; in strength, hardly. Vitellius had pulled the real troops out of the cohorts. In their place he had armed a crowd of loafers from the nearby Nervian and German villages. Loafers with weapons.

4:16 Next, Civilis tried trickery. He blamed the prefects for abandoning the forts and said he would crush the Canninefate uprising himself, using the cohort he commanded. Meanwhile each prefect should go back to his own winter camp. Sounds helpful, right? Look closer. Treachery lay under that advice. Split the cohorts up, and they would be easier to crush. And the real leader of this war was not Brinno. It was Civilis. Signs of the truth were leaking out. The Germans, who love war, couldn't hide them for long. When the trick fell flat, Civilis went for force. He drew up the Canninefates, Frisii and Batavians, each in its own column. Across from them, not far from the Rhine, stood a Roman line. The Romans had run their ships ashore there after burning the forts, and now the ships faced the enemy. The fighting hadn't gone on long when a cohort of Tungri marched its standards over to Civilis. The sudden betrayal threw the rest of the soldiers off balance. Friend and foe both cut them down. The fleet had the same treachery, since some of the rowers were Batavians. They tangled up the sailors and fighting men, as if they simply lacked skill. Then they rowed the other way and drove the sterns onto the enemy bank. Last, they killed the helmsmen and centurions who refused to join them. The whole fleet of twenty-four ships went over or was captured. Every last one.

4:17 What a win. For the moment it was dazzling, and it would pay off later. The rebels now had the weapons and ships they had been missing. All over Germany and Gaul, people hailed them as freedom fighters. German tribes rushed to send envoys with offers of troops. Civilis tried a smarter trick on the Gauls: gifts. He sent the captured cohort prefects (the officers in charge of the cohorts) home to their own states. Then he let the cohorts pick between leaving and staying. Stay, and you'd get honored service. Leave, and you'd get loot taken from the Romans. Then, in private talks, he reminded them how much they had put up with over the years. They had called it "peace." Some peace. It was miserable slavery. He said: "We Batavians don't even pay tribute (the tax paid to Rome), and we still took up arms against the masters we all share. In the first battle, we smashed the Roman army. So what happens if Gaul shakes off the yoke? How much is even left in Italy? Provinces are won with provincial blood. Forget Vindex's army. Batavian cavalry ran over the Aedui and the Arverni, and Belgians were serving in Verginius's auxiliaries too. Think it through honestly, and you'll see that Gaul fell by its own strength. Now everyone is on one side. We even have whatever discipline survived in the Roman camps, plus the veteran cohorts that beat Otho's legions not long ago. Let Syria, Asia and the East, used to kings, bow to slavery if they like. Plenty of people in Gaul are alive who were born before tribute. Just lately, Quintilius Varus was killed and slavery was driven out of Germany. And the man that war challenged wasn't some Vitellius. It was Caesar Augustus. Freedom is nature's gift, even to animals that can't talk. Courage is what makes us human, and the gods stand with the braver. So strike now, while we are free and fresh and the enemy is distracted and worn out. Some of them back Vespasian, others back Vitellius. That leaves the field open against both."

4:18 Civilis was aiming high. He had Gaul and Germany in his sights, and if his plans worked, he would rule the richest and most powerful nations around. Hordeonius Flaccus had fed his first moves by pretending not to know. Then frightened messengers arrived with the news. A camp had been stormed. Cohorts were wiped out. Rome had been driven off the island of the Batavians. That got Flaccus moving. He ordered the legate (a senior commander) Munius Lupercus, who commanded the winter camp of two legions, to march against the enemy. Lupercus rushed across the Rhine with the legionaries he had on hand, some Ubians from nearby, and Treveran cavalry stationed close by. He also took a wing of Batavian cavalry. Here was the catch. Those Batavians had been bought off long before, and they were faking loyalty. Their plan was to betray the Romans in the middle of the battle, then desert for a bigger price. Civilis stood among the standards (the army's flags) of the cohorts he had captured. His men would see their recent glory in front of them. The enemy would shake at the memory of their defeat. Behind the line he placed his mother and sisters, along with the wives and little children of all his men. Their job was to spur the fighters toward victory, or shame them if they lost. Picture it. Men sang and women shrieked until the whole line rang. The cheer that came back from the legions and cohorts was nowhere near as strong. Then the Batavian wing deserted, leaving the left flank bare, and turned on the Romans at once. Still, the legionaries held their weapons and their ranks, even in a frightening spot. The Ubian and Treveran auxiliaries did not. They ran away in shame and scattered across the fields. The Germans threw themselves on that side. Meanwhile, the legions saw a chance and escaped into the camp called the Old Camp. One more man needed handling: Claudius Labeo, prefect of the Batavian cavalry. He and Civilis were rivals in a quarrel between their towns, and he was shipped off to the Frisii. Killing him might stir up his countrymen. Keeping him around might plant the seeds of trouble. Either way, trouble.

4:19 Meanwhile, the Batavian and Canninefate cohorts were marching to Rome on Vitellius's orders. Civilis's messenger caught up with them. Their heads swelled at once. They demanded a bonus for the march, double pay and more cavalry. Vitellius had in fact promised all of it. But they did not want it. They wanted an excuse for mutiny. Flaccus had given in so many times that they only pushed harder for what they knew he'd refuse. They sneered at him and marched off to Lower Germany to join Civilis. Hordeonius summoned the tribunes and centurions (senior officers and company captains). Should he use force on the cohorts that refused to obey? Before long, his natural timidity won out, and so did his officers' fears. They worried about the shaky loyalty of the auxiliaries. They worried about legions padded out by a rushed draft. So Hordeonius decided to keep the troops inside the camp. Then he regretted it. The very officers who had urged him on now disapproved. Of course they did. So he acted as if he meant to chase the Batavians. Herennius Gallus, legate of the 1st legion, held Bonna. Hordeonius wrote to him: stop them from crossing. He himself would follow close behind with his army. The Batavians could have been crushed. One army would come from each side, and the Batavians would be caught in between. But Flaccus dropped the plan. Then a second letter told Gallus not to threaten them as they left. That bred a suspicion: the war was being kindled with the legates' consent. People began to think that none of it, past or feared, came from lazy soldiers or a strong enemy. It came from the generals' treachery.

4:20 Bonna came into view. The Batavians sent a messenger ahead with the cohorts' message for Herennius Gallus: "We have no quarrel with the Romans. We've fought for them so often! A long, pointless service has worn us out, and all we want is to go home and rest. Don't block us, and our march will hurt no one. But if an armed force meets us, we'll cut our way through with the sword." Gallus hesitated. His soldiers shoved him into gambling on a battle. Through every gate poured three thousand legionaries, some hastily raised Belgian cohorts, and a mob of peasants and camp followers. The mob was cowardly, but loud and bold before the danger. Their aim was to surround the Batavians, who had fewer men. Bad bet. But the Batavians were veterans. They closed into tight wedges, guarded at front, flanks and rear. The thin Roman line snapped. The Belgians gave way. The legion was shoved back. In a panic, the Romans ran for the rampart and the gates. Most of the slaughter happened right there. The ditches filled with bodies. Some fell to blows and wounds. Others were trampled in the rush, or killed by their own weapons. The winners steered clear of Colonia Agrippinensis and tried no other hostile act for the rest of the march. As for Bonna, they had their excuse ready. They had asked for peace. It was refused. After that, they had only looked out for themselves. That was their story, anyway.

4:21 Civilis finally had a full army. The veteran cohorts (experienced army units) had shown up, but he still hadn't made up his mind, and Rome's power kept nagging at him. So he made everyone with him swear loyalty to Vespasian. Then he sent messengers to the two legions, who had lost the earlier battle and fallen back into the Old Camp. His message: take the same oath. Their answer was not polite. "We don't take advice from a traitor or an enemy. Vitellius is our emperor. We'll stay loyal to him and fight for him with our swords until our last breath. A Batavian deserter has no business deciding Rome's fate. Let him wait for the punishment his crime deserves." Did that calm Civilis down? Hardly. He blew up with rage and dragged the whole Batavian nation into open war. The Bructeri and Tencteri joined him, and he called on all of Germany to come share the loot and the glory. Come and get it.

4:22 The war was coming. The legates (the emperor's officers in charge of the legions) got ready. Munius Lupercus and Numisius Rufus beefed up the ditches, banks and walls. Buildings had sprung up like a town near the camp during the long peace, and down they came so the enemy couldn't use them. But supplies? Hardly a thought. The legates let them be looted. In just a few days, through carelessness, food that could have lasted a long time was gone. A huge mistake. Civilis took the center of the line himself, with the best of the Batavians. He wanted to look terrifying, so he packed both banks of the Rhine with bands of Germans. His cavalry galloped wildly across the plains while ships rowed upstream. Picture it. On one side, the banners of the veteran cohorts. On the other, images of wild animals, carried out of the woods and sacred groves, the way each tribe always went into battle. Civil war and foreign war, mixed together in one view. The Romans inside were stunned. The size of the wall gave the attackers hope, too. It was built for two legions. Barely five thousand Roman soldiers defended it, but a huge crowd of camp followers (people who came along but did not fight) had gathered there when the peace broke. Now they were there to help in the war. Every last one.

4:23 Picture the camp. Part of it sat on a gentle hill, and the rest lay on flat ground. Augustus had figured these winter camps would keep Germany stuck in a siege, under pressure. He never pictured this: the Germans coming to attack the legions on their own. So nobody bothered with the site or the defenses. Strength and weapons seemed like enough. They weren't. The Batavians and the tribes from across the Rhine took their places. Each tribe stood alone, so each one's courage would show. At first they harassed the camp from far off. Then they noticed something: their missiles mostly stuck in the towers and battlements, while stones dropped from above were hurting them. So with a shout and a rush, they stormed the wall. Most set up ladders, and others climbed up under a roof of shields their friends held over their heads. Some were already climbing over when swords and stabbing weapons hurled them back down, and others were buried under stakes and javelins. They had been way too bold at the start. They had been way too proud when things went well. Now, hungry for loot, they took their losses too. Then they tried siege engines (machines for attacking walls). These were new to them. Skill? They had none. Deserters and prisoners taught them to pile up timber in the shape of a bridge, put wheels under it, and push it forward. Some men would fight from the top, as if from a rampart. Others hid inside and dug under the walls. Rocks from the catapults smashed the shapeless contraption. Next came wicker screens and covered sheds. Burning spears flew out of the machines, and the attackers got hit with fire themselves. Ouch. In the end, they gave up on force and switched to waiting. The camp had food for only a few days, plus a huge crowd of non-fighters. So they bet on hunger breeding treachery. Slaves with shaky loyalty. The luck of war.

4:24 Meanwhile, word of the siege reached Flaccus. He sent people through Gaul to round up reinforcements. He also gave picked men from the legions to Dillius Vocula, the legate of the 22nd legion. Vocula's orders: rush along the riverbank, covering as much ground each day as he could. Flaccus took a ship. He was sick and weak, and the soldiers hated him. They didn't whisper about it. They grumbled out loud: "The Batavian cohorts were let out of Mogontiacum. Nobody did anything about Civilis's moves. Germans are being brought into the alliance. Vespasian has gotten no more strength from Primus Antonius or Mucianus than he gets from this. You can push back open hatred and open weapons. But trickery works in the dark, and there's no dodging it. Civilis is against us, lining up his battle line. Meanwhile, Hordeonius gives orders from his bedroom and his cot, whatever helps the enemy. All these armed bands of the bravest men are run by one old man's sickness. Better to kill the traitor and free our luck and our courage from this bad sign." Talk like that riled them up. Then letters from Vespasian set them on fire. Flaccus couldn't keep the letters secret. So he read them out to the assembled troops. And the men who had brought them? He sent them to Vitellius in chains.

4:25 After that, tempers cooled. Then the army reached Bonna, the winter camp of the 1st legion. The soldiers there were angrier still. They blamed Hordeonius for the defeat. On his orders, they said, the battle line had been drawn up against the Batavians, as if the legions from Mogontiacum were coming up behind. Through his betrayal, they claimed, the men had been cut down, and no help came. No other army knew, they said, and the emperor was kept in the dark. Yet the quick help of so many provinces could have crushed a sudden betrayal. Hordeonius read the army copies of every letter he had sent. He had begged for help from Gaul, Britain and Spain. He also set up a very bad precedent. Letters would now go to the eagle-bearers (the soldiers who carried each legion's eagle standard). They would read them to the troops before the generals did. Then he had one rebel put in chains. That was to show his authority, more than because one man was at fault. The army marched from Bonna to Colonia Agrippinensis. Gallic reinforcements kept pouring in, since at first the Gauls helped Rome with real energy. But the Germans grew stronger, and soon most of the Gallic states took up arms against Rome. What did they want? To be free. And once they had shaken off slavery, to rule others too. Meanwhile the legions' anger kept growing. The chains on one soldier scared nobody, and he turned around and accused Flaccus of working with Civilis. He claimed he had been a messenger between Civilis and Flaccus. A false charge was crushing him, he said, for being a witness to the truth. Vocula climbed the platform with amazing calm. He ordered the shouting soldier, who had been grabbed, led away to be executed. The bad men were scared. The best men all obeyed. Then the whole army demanded Vocula as their general. Flaccus handed him the top command.

4:26 Trouble piled up for soldiers who were already split. Pay and grain were running short, and Gaul refused to send recruits or pay its taxes. Then the Rhine dropped. A drought like nobody there had ever seen left it barely deep enough to float a ship, and supplies got tight. Guard posts had to line the whole bank to keep the Germans from wading across at the shallow spots. Less grain, more mouths to feed. Among the uneducated, the lack of water counted as a bad sign, as if even the rivers, the empire's old defenses, were abandoning Rome. In peacetime, people would have blamed bad luck or plain nature. Now they said fate, and a god's anger. Sure they did. The army entered Novesium, and the 16th legion joined up with it. Herennius Gallus was added to Vocula to share the work. He was a legate (an officer the emperor put in charge of a legion or province). They didn't dare march on the enemy. So they set up camp at a place called Gelduba. There they steadied the soldiers with drills: battle lines, fortifications, digging, walling, and the other routines of war. Vocula wanted to fire the men up with the hope of loot. So he marched the army against the nearest villages of the Gugerni, who had joined Civilis. Part of the army stayed behind with Herennius Gallus. Remember that.

4:27 Then a grain ship got stuck in the shallows, not far from the camp. The Germans started dragging it toward their own bank. Gallus couldn't stand it, so he sent a cohort to help. More Germans came, more Romans joined in, and soon it was a real battle. The Germans killed many Romans and hauled the ship away. The beaten soldiers fell back on their favorite habit. Whose fault was it? Anyone's but their own. They blamed the treachery of their legate, not their own cowardice. They dragged Gallus out of his tent, ripped his clothes, and beat him. Then they ordered him to say what price he had been paid, and who had helped him betray the army. Their hatred of Hordeonius came roaring back. They called Hordeonius the mastermind of the crime and Gallus his tool. Gallus, terrified by threats of death, finally accused Hordeonius of treason himself. Then he was thrown in chains, and he stayed there until Vocula finally arrived and freed him. The next day Vocula had the ringleaders of the mutiny executed. This army swung between wild and obedient. The common soldiers were loyal to Vitellius, no doubt, but all the most distinguished men leaned toward Vespasian. So outbreaks and executions took turns, a strange mix of obedience and rage. Punishing these men was easy. Holding them back was not.

4:28 Meanwhile, Civilis grew stronger. All Germany lifted him up with huge reinforcements. Hostages from the noblest families sealed the alliance. He ordered the nearest tribes to ravage the lands of the Ubii and the Treveri. Another force crossed the river Mosa to shake up the Menapii, the Morini, and the farthest parts of Gaul. Both raids brought back loot, but the Ubii got it worse. They were a people of German origin who had given up their homeland and taken a Roman name, the Agrippinenses. Their cohorts were cut down in the village of Marcodurum. They had gotten careless there, far from the riverbank. Still, the Ubii didn't sit quietly. They went raiding in Germany, and at first they got away with it. Then they were surrounded. Through that whole war, they proved more loyal than lucky. With the Ubii crushed, Civilis got more dangerous. Winning made him bolder, and he pressed the siege of the legions. Close guards went up, so no secret messenger could slip in with news of help on the way. He handed the siege engines (big war machines for attacking walls) and the great siege works over to the Batavians. The tribes from across the Rhine kept demanding a battle. So he sent them to tear down the rampart. Driven back? Go right back in. He had more than enough men. A loss wouldn't even be felt.

4:29 Even night didn't end the fight. The Germans piled wood around the walls and lit it. Then they sat down to feast, and as each man warmed up with wine, he charged into the fight with pointless daring. Wine and spears are a bad mix. Their missiles flew uselessly in the dark. The Romans, meanwhile, could see the enemy line clearly. They picked out anyone who stood out for boldness or shiny decorations, and took the shot. Civilis noticed and had the fires put out. Now darkness and weapons threw everything into chaos. It was like a brawl in a pitch-black room. Noise. Clashing. Chance decided everything, and nobody could see how to strike or dodge. Whenever a shout rang out, men spun that way and strained every limb. Courage didn't help, and the bravest often fell to a coward's missile. The Germans fought with reckless fury, but the Romans knew the dangers. They threw iron-tipped stakes and heavy stones on purpose, not by chance. When the noise of men at work, or a ladder against the wall, told them where the enemy was, they shoved back with their shields and followed up with javelins. Many who had climbed the walls were stabbed with daggers. That was the night. Then day brought a new kind of attack.

4:30 The Batavians had built a two-story tower. They pushed it up to the Praetorian gate, where the ground was flattest. It didn't last. The Romans shoved strong poles out against it and rammed it with beams until it broke apart. Many of the men standing on top died in the crash. Then the Romans burst out in a sudden strike and hit the dazed enemy hard. It worked. Meanwhile, the legionaries had more experience and skill, so they built many more engines. One machine scared the enemy most. It hung in the air and swayed. Then it dropped all at once. As it swung past their friends' faces, it snatched up one enemy, or several, and lifted them high. Then the Romans shifted the heavy counterweight, like kids on a seesaw, and flung them inside the camp. Civilis gave up on storming the camp and sat back down to wait it out. He tried to wear down the legions' loyalty with messages and promises instead.

4:31 All this happened in Germany before the battle of Cremona. The result came in a letter from Primus Antonius, along with Caecina's proclamation. Alpinius Montanus, a cohort prefect from the losing side, was there in person. He admitted his side had lost. People reacted in all kinds of ways. The Gallic auxiliaries had no love or hate for either side, and they just served, feeling nothing much. Their prefects urged them on, and they turned away from Vitellius at once. The veteran soldiers hesitated until Hordeonius Flaccus pushed and the tribunes insisted, so they took the oath. Their faces and hearts weren't in it, though. They spoke the rest of the words, but at Vespasian's name? They stalled. Some oath. A faint murmur got them past it. Most of the time, silence.

4:32 Next, Antonius's letters to Civilis were read aloud to the gathered soldiers. The soldiers got suspicious. The letters sounded as if they were written to a partner in the cause, and they called the German army an enemy. Soon the news reached the camp at Gelduba, and the same words and actions were repeated there. Montanus was sent to Civilis with orders to end the war. The Romans didn't want a foreign enemy hiding its plans behind fake weapons. If Civilis had set out to help Vespasian, he had already done enough. At first, Civilis answered slyly. But then he saw that Montanus was extremely hot-headed and ready for a revolution. So Civilis opened with his complaints, and with the dangers he had lived through over twenty-five years in Roman camps. Then he said: "Some reward for all my hard work, right? My brother was murdered. I was put in chains. And this army's savage yells were calling for my execution. A mob demanded my death, and by the law of nations (the rights every people is supposed to share), I'm owed payback. And you Treveri, and all the rest of you? Nothing but breathing slaves. What do you expect for all the blood you've spilled, time after time? Lousy military service. Endless taxes. Rods, axes, and the moods of masters. Look at me. I'm the prefect of one cohort, and with the Canninefates and Batavians, a tiny corner of Gaul, we've wrecked those huge, useless camps. Or we have them penned in with sword and famine. So here's the bottom line: if you dare, freedom comes next. If we lose, we'll be what we were before." That's how Civilis riled up Montanus. Then he sent him back with a milder answer to carry. Montanus went back as if his mission had failed. He hid the rest. It soon came out.

4:33 Civilis held part of his troops back. He sent the veteran cohorts (army units) and the toughest Germans after Vocula and his army instead. Julius Maximus and Claudius Victor led them. Victor was Civilis's sister's son. On the way, they plundered the winter quarters of a cavalry unit at Ascibergium. Then they hit Vocula's camp so fast that he couldn't speak to his men or even form a line. Chaos. All he could do was order the legionaries by the standards (the legions' flags) to hold the middle. The auxiliaries (support units of non-citizen soldiers) were scattered everywhere. The horsemen charged out, ran into the enemy's neat, tight ranks, and fled from their own side. That was no battle. It was a slaughter. Then the Nervian cohorts left the sides of the Roman line wide open, out of fear or betrayal. Not ideal. The enemy poured in on the legions. Those men had lost their standards, and they were being cut down inside the rampart. So who came to the rescue? Vascon cohorts that Galba had raised, and had been sent for by now. They heard the roar of the fight as they neared the camp, and they hit the enemy from behind while the enemy was busy at the front. They looked like far more men than they were. Some thought the whole army from Novesium had arrived. Others thought it was the army from Mogontiacum. A mistake, but a lucky one. It gave the Romans fresh courage, and by trusting in other people's strength, they found their own again. The bravest of the Batavians, all the ones on foot, broke and ran. The cavalry got away with the standards and the prisoners from the first clash. More Romans died that day, but they were the weaker fighters. The Germans lost their best.

4:34 Both generals share the blame. Each had earned his defeats, and neither made the most of his wins. Take Civilis first. Had he lined up a bigger force, so few cohorts could never have surrounded him, and he could have broken into the camp and wiped it out. Now Vocula. He never scouted the enemy's approach. So he marched out and got beaten right away. Then he stopped trusting his win. He wasted several days for nothing before moving his camp toward the enemy. Push them back at once and follow up, and he could have lifted the siege of the legions in one stroke. Meanwhile Civilis tried to rattle the men under siege. His message: Rome had lost, and his side had won. Standards and flags were paraded around. Even captives were put on show. One of them showed remarkable nerve and shouted the real story at the top of his voice. The Germans cut him down on the spot. That only made people believe him more. Burned farmhouses and a wrecked countryside showed the winning army was on its way. Vocula ordered the standards planted in sight of the camp, with a ditch and rampart dug around them. That way the men could drop their baggage and packs and fight without the load. Shouts came back, demanding a fight. These men had gotten used to making threats. They didn't even take time to form a line. Messy and worn out, they started the fight anyway. Civilis was there too, counting on his enemy's mistakes as much as on his own men's courage. Among the Romans, fortunes swung back and forth. The loudest mutineers turned out to be cowards. Some remembered their recent win. They held their ground, struck the enemy, and cheered on themselves and the men beside them. Once their line re-formed, they reached out their hands to the besieged and urged them not to miss their chance. The besieged were watching everything from the walls. Then they burst out of every gate. By chance, Civilis's horse fell and threw him to the ground. A report that he was wounded or killed spread through both armies. Everyone believed it. His own men were gripped by terror. Their enemies got a huge boost. But Vocula did not chase the fleeing enemy. He built up the camp's rampart and towers instead, as if another siege were coming. Think about that. He had spoiled victory too often. People rightly suspected he preferred the war.

4:35 What wore the Roman armies down most? Running low on supplies. The legions' baggage went to Novesium with a crowd of non-fighters. Their job was to bring grain back overland, since the enemy held the river. The first column got through safely. Civilis wasn't strong enough yet. Then he heard that grain-gatherers were going to Novesium again, with cohorts as an escort. Picture it. Those cohorts strolled along as if the country were at peace. Few soldiers near the standards. Weapons packed in the wagons. Everyone wandering wherever he liked. Not a great plan. Civilis attacked in formation, after sending men ahead to seize the bridges and the narrow spots in the road. The fight stretched along the long column, and nobody knew who would win until night broke it up. The cohorts went on to Gelduba. The camp there stayed as it was, held by the soldiers left behind. Everyone could see the grain-carriers would be in real danger on the way back, loaded down and rattled. So Vocula added a thousand picked men to his army, from the 5th and 15th legions, the ones besieged at the Old Camp. They were an unruly lot who hated their officers. More of them left than had been ordered. On the march, they grumbled out loud. No more hunger. No more treachery from the legates (the generals who commanded legions). The men left behind had a complaint of their own: they'd been abandoned when part of the legions was pulled away. So the mutiny pulled in two directions. Some called for Vocula to come back. The others refused to return to the camp.

4:36 Civilis besieged the Old Camp. Vocula fell back to Gelduba, then to Novesium. Civilis took Gelduba. Soon after, he won a cavalry clash close to Novesium. Win or lose, the soldiers kept getting riled up to bring down their commanders. The legions had grown with the arrival of the men from the 5th and 15th. Now they demanded a bonus, because they had learned that Vitellius had sent money. After a short delay, Hordeonius handed it over in Vespasian's name. That was like throwing fresh wood on a fire. The men threw themselves into wild living: feasts, and gatherings at night. Their old anger at Hordeonius came roaring back. No legate or tribune (a senior officer) dared get in their way. The darkness seems to have wiped out any shame they had left. They dragged Hordeonius out of bed and killed him. They planned the same for Vocula. He put on a slave's clothes and slipped off in the dark. Nobody recognized him.

4:37 Then the rage cooled. Fear crept back in. The soldiers sent centurions with letters to the states of Gaul, begging for troops and pay. And the men themselves? A leaderless mob, headstrong, jumpy and lazy. When Civilis got close, they snatched up weapons without thinking, dropped them just as fast, and ran. The setback split them. The men from the Upper army began pulling their cause away from the others. Still, images of Vitellius went back up in the camp and in the nearby Belgian cities. Vitellius was already dead. (Yes, really.) Then the 1st, 4th and 22nd legions changed their minds. They were sorry, and they followed Vocula. In front of him, they swore the oath to Vespasian again, and he led them to break the siege of Mogontiacum. The besiegers, a mixed army of Chatti, Usipii and Mattiaci, had already pulled out. They were stuffed with loot, and it had cost them blood. Roman soldiers caught them on the road, scattered and unaware. Meanwhile the Treveri threw up a wall and a rampart across their territory. They fought the Germans there, and both sides lost heavily. Then the Treveri rebelled. They wrecked their own fine record of service to the Roman people.

4:38 Vespasian, for the second time, and Titus began their terms as consuls. They were still away from Rome. The city was gloomy and full of fear. The real dangers weren't enough. Panic over things that weren't real set in too. Rumors said Africa had revolted, and that Lucius Piso was stirring up a revolution there. Piso was the proconsul (a governor the Senate sent to run a province) of that province, and he was nowhere near a troublemaker by nature. And the grain? Stuck. A brutal winter was holding the ships back. Ordinary people bought their food day to day, and grain was their one real worry in public life. So in their fear, they believed the coast had been shut and the shipments held back. Fear does that. The Vitellianists, who hadn't given up their side, kept the rumor growing. Even the winners didn't mind it. Not even foreign wars could fill their greed. No civil war victory ever had.

4:39 January first. Julius Frontinus, the city praetor, had called the Senate together. The senators voted thanks and praise to the legates, the armies and the allied kings. Then the jobs got shuffled. Tettius Julianus lost his praetorship. The charge: he had deserted his legion when it switched sides to Vespasian. Plotius Griphus got it. Hormus was given the rank of Roman Knight. Then Frontinus resigned, and Domitian, the Caesar, took the praetorship, with his name at the top of letters and edicts. Just a name on paper. The real power was in Mucianus's hands. Domitian did venture plenty of acts of his own, either because his friends pushed him or because he felt like it. Mucianus was scared, and two men scared him most: Primus Antonius and Arrius Varus. They were fresh and famous for what they had just done. The soldiers adored them, and the people even liked them, since they had been cruel to no one beyond the battlefield. Was Antonius plotting? There was a report that he had urged Scribonianus Crassus to take over the government. Crassus stood out, with distinguished ancestors and a brother's honors. Antonius would have had plenty of backers if Crassus had said yes. He said no. Crassus was not easily tempted, even by a sure thing, and he was very careful about risk. So Mucianus could not crush Antonius in the open. He smothered him instead. He piled praise on him in the Senate. He loaded him with secret promises and dangled Nearer Spain, empty since Cluvius Rufus left. At the same time he showered Antonius's friends with posts as tribunes and prefects. Then, with Antonius's head full of hope and ambition, Mucianus went after his strength. The 7th legion loved Antonius most passionately, so Mucianus sent it off to winter quarters. The 3rd legion was close to Arrius Varus, and it went back to Syria. Part of the army was marched into Germany. Troublemakers gone. The city looked normal again, with its laws and its magistrates doing their duties.

4:40 Domitian's first speech in the Senate was short and modest. He mentioned his father and brother being away, and his own youth. He carried himself well, and nobody knew his character yet, so all that blushing passed for modesty. Sure it did. Domitian proposed restoring Galba's honors. Curtius Montanus moved that Piso's memory be honored too. The senators ordered both. The Piso one went nowhere. Next, commissioners were picked by lot. Their list of jobs was long. They had to return property seized in the war. They had to check the bronze law tablets that had fallen down from age and put them back in place. They had to clear the calendar of the flattery that had defaced it lately. And they had to set a limit on public spending. Tettius Julianus got his praetorship back once people learned he had fled to Vespasian, and Griphus kept his. After that came a rematch. The case between Musonius Rufus and Publius Celer would be heard again. Publius was convicted. Amends were made to the spirit of Soranus. The day was about public justice, but it handed out personal praise too. Musonius, for one, seemed to have run a fair prosecution. Opinions split on Demetrius, who professed the Cynic philosophy. Why? He had defended a man who was plainly guilty, more to win favor than to do right. Publius himself had no courage in his danger and no words to say. Ouch. With the signal for revenge on the informers given, Junius Mauricus asked Domitian to let the Senate see the imperial records. Then they could learn who had asked for which prosecutions. His reply: the emperor had to be asked about a matter like that. Not exactly a yes.

4:41 Then came the oath. The Senate's leading members started it, and the Senate drew it up. Every magistrate swore it eagerly, and so did each other senator when his turn to vote came. They called the gods as witnesses. Nothing had been done with their help to harm anyone's safety. And they had taken no reward or honor from citizens' disasters. Easy words for the innocent. Those who felt guilty got nervous and changed the words of the oath in all kinds of ways. The senators approved of that scruple about the oath and called out the lying under oath (perjury). Call it a public shaming. It hit hardest on three men: Sariolenus Vocula, Nonius Attianus and Cestius Severus. All three were notorious for turning people in again and again under Nero. Sariolenus also faced a recent charge: he had tried the same thing under Vitellius. The Senate kept making threatening gestures at him until he left the chamber. Gone. Next they turned on Paccius Africanus and hounded him too. The charge: he had pointed out the Scribonius brothers to Nero, to be destroyed. Those brothers were famous for their loyalty to each other, and for their wealth. Could Africanus admit it? He didn't dare. Deny it? He couldn't. So he turned on Vibius Crispus, who was wearing him down with questions. He mixed in things he couldn't defend. Share the guilt, and the hatred slides off.

4:42 Vipstanus Messalla had a big day. Messalla was too young to be a senator. He pleaded for his brother, Aquilius Regulus, anyway, and it won him great fame for family loyalty and for speaking. Regulus was hated more than anyone, ever since the Crassus and Orfitus families fell. He seemed to have jumped into that prosecution on his own, while still quite young. People saw it this way: he wasn't dodging danger, he was after power. Crassus's wife, Sulpicia Praetextata, was there too, with her four children. If the Senate heard the case, they were ready to take revenge. The stakes were high. So Messalla defended neither the case nor the accused. Instead he put himself between his brother and the dangers, and some senators were moved. Curtius Montanus hit back with a savage speech. He went so far as to say that after Galba's death, Regulus had paid the man who killed Piso. He even said Regulus had bitten the dead man's head. Montanus said: "Nero didn't force you into that, Regulus. All that savagery bought you neither rank nor safety. Some men would rather wreck others than risk themselves, and we can put up with their excuses. But you? Your father's exile kept you safe, and so did the splitting of his property among his creditors. You were too young for office, and Nero had no reason to want you or fear you. Bloodlust and a hunger for rewards drove you. Nobody even knew your talent yet, and you had never tried it in any defense. Still you soaked yourself in noble blood. You snatched a former consul's property from the Republic's grave. Seven million sesterces made you fat, and priestly honors made you shine. And you crushed innocent boys, famous old men and noblewomen in one ruin. You even blamed Nero for being slow. He was wearing himself and his informers out one house at a time. One word could have destroyed the whole Senate. Keep him, Senators! Protect a man with advice that clever. Then every age gets its teacher. Our young men can copy Regulus, like our old ones copy Marcellus and Crispus. Even failed wickedness finds imitators, so picture it flourishing and thriving. Today he is only of quaestor's rank, and we don't dare offend him. Will we dare when he is a praetor and a consul? Do you think Nero was the last of the tyrants? The men who outlived Tiberius and Caligula thought so too. Then a worse and crueler one showed up. We aren't afraid of Vespasian, because his age and moderation reassure us. But examples outlast characters. We've gone limp, Senators. Once Nero was dead, the Senate demanded that the informers and the tyrant's agents be punished by ancient custom. We are not that Senate. The day right after a wicked emperor falls is the best chance there is."

4:43 Montanus got a roar of approval from the Senate. Helvidius Priscus smelled his chance to bring Marcellus down too. First he praised Cluvius Rufus. Cluvius was every bit as rich and every bit as famous for speaking, yet under Nero he had never put anyone in danger. Then Helvidius leaned on Eprius Marcellus with that comparison and a direct accusation. The senators' tempers blazed. Marcellus saw it coming and made as if to leave the chamber. "We're leaving, Priscus," he said, "and we leave you your Senate. Play the king, with Domitian himself here." Vibius Crispus followed him. Both men were furious, but their faces told different stories: Marcellus glared and Crispus smiled. Friends rushed in and pulled them back. Meanwhile the fight kept heating up. Many good men lined up on one side. A few powerful men on the other dug in with stubborn hatreds. Fine way to spend a day. Quarreling ate every hour of it.

4:44 Domitian opened the next Senate meeting with a plea. Put aside grief, anger and the harsh needs of earlier times. Then Mucianus took over. He spoke at length in favor of the informers. He also warned the men who were reviving cases they had started and dropped, and he did it in a soft, pleading voice. Once people pushed back, the senators gave up the freedom they had just started to use. That was fast. Mucianus did not want the Senate's decision to look ignored, or everything done under Nero to look like it got off scot-free. So he sent two men of senatorial rank back to the same islands. They were Octavius Sagitta and Antistius Sosianus, and both had left their places of exile. Octavius had an affair with Pontia Postumia, who refused to marry him, and in a fit of passion he couldn't control, he killed her. Sosianus's twisted ways had been deadly for many people. The Senate had convicted both men and banished them with a harsh decree. Others were allowed to come home, but these two stayed under the same punishment. Did that cool the hatred toward Mucianus? It did not. Sosianus and Sagitta were worthless, even if they came back. What people really feared was something else. The informers' cleverness. Their money. A power built on dirty tricks.

4:45 For a little while, the Senate felt like one body. A hearing held by the old rules did it. Senator Manlius Patruitus said a crowd had beaten him up in the colony of Sena, on the magistrates' orders. And it didn't stop there. While he stood in front of everyone, people staged a fake funeral around him. Wailing. Weeping. Insults and abuse hurled at the whole Senate. Picture standing in the middle of that. The accused were called in, and after the case was heard, the guilty were punished. The Senate also passed a decree warning the common people of Sena to behave themselves. Around the same time, the people of Cyrene brought charges against Antonius Flamma, and he was convicted under the law on extortion. Cruelty got him exiled.

4:46 Meanwhile, the army nearly mutinied. Men Vitellius had discharged had rallied to Vespasian, and they wanted their old place in the Praetorian Guard, the emperor's bodyguard in Rome. Soldiers picked from the legions, hoping for the same thing, demanded the pay they had been promised. Getting rid of the Vitellianists, Vitellius's old soldiers, would cost a great deal of blood. Keeping so many men would cost a fortune. Either way, trouble. Mucianus went into the camp to look closely at each man's service. He lined up the victorious army in full decorations and weapons, with small gaps between the groups. Then the Vitellianists were marched out almost naked. These were the men who surrendered at Bovillae, as I already told you. Others had been rounded up in the city and around it too. Mucianus ordered them split up. The German and British soldiers, and any from other armies, had to stand off by themselves. The first sight stunned them all. Across from them stood what looked like a battle line, bristling with spears and weapons. They were boxed in, unarmed and filthy. Then the pulling and shoving began, and fear ran through every man. The Germans were the most terrified. To them, being split up meant they were marked for slaughter. They grabbed their comrades by the chest and hung on their necks. They begged for last kisses. They begged not to be left alone, or made to suffer a different fate for the same cause. They called on Mucianus, then on the absent emperor, and finally on heaven and the gods. Then Mucianus stepped forward and called them all soldiers bound by the same oath, serving the same emperor. Their fear, he showed them, was false. The victorious army even shouted in support of the beaten men's tears. That ended the day. A few days later, Domitian spoke to the Vitellianists. By then they felt braver, and they greeted him with new confidence. They turned up their noses at the land offered to them. They begged for service and pay. Prayers, yes. But the kind nobody could refuse. So they were let into the Praetorian camp. Then men of the right age, or with enough years served, were discharged with honor. Others were dismissed for misconduct. One at a time, in small batches. The safest way to weaken a crowd's unity? Exactly that.

4:47 Poor, or just pretending to be? Either way, the Senate voted to borrow sixty million sesterces (Roman coins) from private people. Pompeius Silvanus was put in charge of the loan. Not long after, the need went away, or the pretending stopped. Next, Domitian proposed a law, and the consulships Vitellius had handed out were canceled. Flavius Sabinus got a funeral with the honors given to a former censor, the official who reviewed the citizens' ranks. Both were big lessons in the twists of life. Fortune always mixes the highest honors with the lowest disgrace. Always.

4:48 Africa's governor was killed around this time. His name was Lucius Piso, and he was the proconsul there. I'll tell that killing as accurately as I can. First, a step back. A few earlier events show how deeds like this come about. Under the divine Augustus and Tiberius, the legion in Africa and the auxiliary troops guarded the empire's borders. Both answered to the proconsul. Simple. Then Caligula came along. He was restless by nature, and he was scared of Marcus Silanus, who was governing Africa then. So Caligula took the legion away from the proconsul and gave it to a legate, an officer he sent out for exactly that job. Now two men shared the power to hand out favors equally. Their orders overlapped and tangled, like two cooks fighting over one kitchen. Rivalry was practically invited. An ugly contest made it worse. Legates grew more powerful the longer they served. Or maybe the lesser man simply cared more about winning. The best proconsuls cared more about staying alive than about power.

4:49 Valerius Festus ran the legion in Africa back then. He was young, with expensive tastes and wild ambition, and he was nervous because of a family connection to Vitellius. Did he try to push Piso into revolt during their many talks? Or did Piso turn down his offers? Nobody knows, since nobody else was in the room. And after Piso died, most people leaned toward flattering the killer. Of course they did. One thing is certain: the province and the soldiers were hostile to Vespasian. Some Vitellianists, backers of Vitellius, had fled the city. They kept telling Piso that the Gauls were wobbling and Germany was ready. He was in danger himself, they said, and a man already suspected in peacetime was safer in war. Meanwhile Claudius Sagitta, who commanded the Petrian cavalry wing, got there first on a fast voyage. He beat Papirius, a centurion (an army officer) sent by Mucianus. Sagitta warned Piso that the centurion carried orders to kill him. Piso's cousin and son-in-law, Galerianus, was dead, and his only hope was bold action. He had two ways to dare it. Grab weapons right away. Or sail to Gaul and offer himself as a leader to the Vitellianist armies. Piso wasn't moved by any of it. Then the centurion reached the port of Carthage. He shouted cheerful greetings to Piso in a loud voice, as if Piso were an emperor. Then he told the people who met him, stunned by how strange it was, to shout the same. The gullible crowd rushed to the forum, the public square, and demanded to see Piso. Picture the noise. Joy and shouting threw everything into chaos. Nobody cared about the truth. Everyone wanted to flatter. Piso stayed indoors, either because Sagitta had warned him or from natural modesty, since he wouldn't trust himself to the crowd's excitement. He questioned the centurion instead. The man had been hunting for a charge against Piso and a way to kill him. So Piso had him executed. It was less about saving his own life than anger at the killer. This man had helped murder Clodius Macer, and now he'd come back to kill the proconsul, hands still bloody from the legate's blood. Then Piso scolded the people of Carthage in an anxious edict. He even stopped his usual duties and shut himself in his house, in case new trouble flared up by chance. Trouble found him anyway.

4:50 Festus heard everything. The crowd's uproar. The centurion's execution. Reports came in, true and false, and rumor blew them up the way it always does. His answer was cavalry, sent to kill Piso. The riders rode hard and burst into the proconsul's house in the dim light before dawn, swords drawn. Most of them had never seen Piso, because Festus had picked Carthaginian and Moorish auxiliaries for the job. Near the bedroom, they ran into a slave by chance and asked who he was and where Piso was. The slave told a noble lie. He said he was Piso. They cut him down at once. Piso himself died soon after, because someone there knew his face: Baebius Massa, one of the procurators of Africa. (A procurator was an official who ran money and property for the emperor.) Even then, Massa's name meant death to good men. It would keep coming up among the causes of the troubles we soon suffered. Remember it. Festus had waited at Adrumetum to see how it turned out. Now he rushed to the legion and had Cetronius Pisanus, the camp prefect, put in chains. The cause was private quarrels. But Festus called him Piso's henchman. He punished some soldiers and centurions, and he rewarded others. Neither group had earned it, but he wanted people to think he had crushed a war. Quite a show. Then he settled the quarrels between the people of Oeea and the Leptitani. They had started small, with raids on grain and cattle between country folk. By now, armies and battle lines were fighting them out. Outnumbered, the people of Oeea called in the Garamantes, a wild tribe that got rich robbing its neighbors. That put the Leptitani in a tight spot. Their fields were ravaged far and wide. They trembled behind their walls. Then cohorts and cavalry wings arrived. They scattered the Garamantes and won back all the loot. All except one part: what the raiders had sold to tribes farther away, after roaming through hamlets no one could reach. That loot stayed gone.

4:51 Good news poured in after the battle of Cremona. People of every rank reached Vespasian with word that Vitellius had fallen. They had braved the winter sea with equal daring and luck. Envoys from King Vologesus showed up too, offering forty thousand Parthian horsemen. Vespasian felt proud and glad, because allies were courting him with help he didn't even need. Who wouldn't enjoy that? He thanked Vologesus, then told him to send envoys to the Senate and to know that peace had come. Now Vespasian's eyes were on Italy and the city. A bad report reached him about Domitian. The charge: he was going past the limits of his age and of what a son was allowed. So Vespasian gave the strongest part of his army to Titus. Titus was to finish what was left of the Jewish war.

4:52 They say Titus talked with his father for a long time before leaving. He begged him not to be set off too easily by what accusers said. He asked him to stay open-minded and forgiving toward his son. A big family, Titus said, defends imperial power better than legions and fleets ever could. Friends? Time wears them down, shifts them and loses them, and so do changes in fortune and sometimes their own wants or mistakes. But a man's blood can't be cut away from him, least of all a ruler's. Others share in a ruler's wins, but his troubles touch only the people closest to him. Even brothers won't keep harmony unless the parent sets the example. Titus's brotherly feeling delighted Vespasian, though he still wasn't reconciled to Domitian. He told his son to cheer up. Build the state through war and arms, he said, and Vespasian would see to peace and the family. Then he loaded the fastest ships with grain and sent them out onto the still rough sea. The city was in real danger. Its storehouses held no more than ten days' grain. Then Vespasian's supplies came to the rescue. Just in time.

4:53 Who would rebuild the Capitol? Lucius Vestinius. He was only a Roman Knight, but his good name put him among the leading men. He called in the soothsayers, and their plan was simple. Haul the leftover pieces of the old shrine off to the marshes. Then build the new temple on the very same footprint. Why? The gods did not want the old form changed. On the 21st of June, under a clear sky, the whole space for the temple was wrapped in ribbons and garlands. In marched soldiers with lucky names, holding branches of good luck. Then came the Vestal Virgins, with boys and girls whose fathers and mothers were both still alive. They washed the place with water from springs and rivers. Next the praetor Helvidius Priscus stepped up, with the pontiff Plautius Aelianus reciting the words for him. Helvidius cleansed the area with a pig, a sheep and a bull. He laid the entrails on a turf altar. Then he prayed to Jupiter, Juno, Minerva and the gods who guard the empire. Make the work succeed, he asked. And lift up, with divine help, the homes that human devotion had begun for them. He touched the ribbons around the stone and the ropes tied to it. At that very moment, everyone pulled. The other magistrates, the priests, the Senate, the Knights and a big part of the people dragged the huge stone along, eager and joyful. Picture it. Silver and gold went into the foundations everywhere, along with the first fruits of metals. They were raw, just as they come out of the earth, never melted in a furnace. The soothsayers had warned that stone or gold meant for another use would spoil the work if it went in. The building was made taller. That was the only change religion allowed. People thought it was just what the old temple had been missing. Taller. Nothing else.

4:54 Vitellius was dead, and the war had doubled. The news raced through Gaul and Germany. Civilis dropped the disguise and charged straight at the Roman people. The Vitellianist legions, the ones loyal to Vitellius, would rather be slaves to foreigners than have Vespasian as emperor. Think about that. The Gauls took heart. They figured the Roman armies were failing everywhere. Then the rumors started. The Sarmatae and Dacians had the winter camps in Moesia and Pannonia under siege, people said. False. Made-up stories about Britain went around too. But nothing hit the Gauls like the burning of the Capitol. Now they were sure the end of the empire was at hand. Gauls had captured the city once, but Jupiter's seat had stayed untouched, and the empire lived on. This time it had burned. The Druids chanted, with the empty superstition of prophets, that the deadly fire was a sign of heaven's anger. Power over human affairs, they said, would pass to the peoples beyond the Alps. Another rumor said the leading men of Gaul, sent by Otho against Vitellius, had made a pact before they left. They would not fail the cause of freedom. They would act if one civil war after another and troubles at home wore the Roman people down. A rumor, yes. But a hopeful one.

4:55 The plot stayed hidden until Hordeonius Flaccus was murdered. Then the messengers got busy. They ran back and forth between Civilis and Classicus, commander of the Treveri cavalry wing. Who was Classicus? Top of the heap in rank and wealth. He came from a royal line, with an origin famous in peace and war. He bragged that his ancestors had been enemies of the Roman people, not friends. Two more men joined him. Julius Tutor was a Treveran, and Julius Sabinus was a Lingonian. Vitellius had put Tutor in charge of the Rhine bank, while Sabinus was vain, and an imagined family tie swelled his head even more. His claim? His great-grandmother had an adulterous affair with the divine Julius, back when Julius was campaigning in Gaul. Sure she did. Tutor and Sabinus held secret talks to sound out their countrymen. They picked accomplices who seemed suitable and let them in on the plot. Then they met in a private house in Colonia Agrippinensis. The city's official policy was against any such plot, so a private house it was. Some Ubii and Tungri joined in anyway. But the real weight lay with the Treveri and Lingones. They could not stand waiting around to talk it over. They shouted over each other. Rome was tearing itself apart with quarrels. The legions had been cut down. Italy was devastated, and Rome itself was being captured at that very moment. Every army was tied up in its own wars. So put garrisons on the Alps, they said. Then Gaul, with her freedom firmly established, could decide how far she wanted her power to reach. Freedom first. Then power.

4:56 Everyone agreed, out loud and all at once. The survivors of the Vitellianist army were a harder question. Most wanted them dead. They called them troublemakers, disloyal men with the blood of their own generals on their hands. But the case for mercy won. Take away all hope of a pardon, and you would stir up stubborn resistance. Better to pull them into the alliance. Kill only the legates, the commanders of the legions, and the rest of the crowd would join easily. They were guilty, and they would hope to go unpunished. That was the first plan. Agents went all over Gaul to stir up war. Meanwhile the plotters pretended to obey, so they could crush Vocula when he least expected it. Some people did warn Vocula. But he lacked the strength to put the movement down. His legions were thin on men and could not be trusted. He had doubtful soldiers all around him and hidden enemies too. Not a great spot. His best choice, he thought, was to answer pretense with pretense and use the same tricks being used on him. So he went down to Colonia Agrippinensis. There Claudius Labeo bribed his guards and escaped. As I said, he had been captured and sent out of the way to the Frisii. Labeo promised that if he got a force, he would go into Batavian territory. There he would win the more influential part of that nation back to the Roman side. He got a small force of infantry and cavalry. But he did not dare try anything against the Batavians. Instead he drew in some Nervii and Betasii and raided the Canninefates and Marsaci. He fought like a thief, not a soldier.

4:57 The Gauls tricked Vocula, and he marched out against the enemy. Not far from the Old Camp, Classicus and Tutor went ahead. They said they were scouting. Some scouting. They were making deals with the German leaders. Then, for the first time, they split from the legions and built their own camp, with its own wall around it. Vocula pleaded. Civil wars had not shaken Rome so badly that the Treveri and Lingones could look down on it, he said. Loyal provinces still stood. So did the winning armies, the empire's good luck, and the gods who take revenge. That was how Sacrovir and the Aedui had been smashed in single battles long ago. Vindex and the Gauls had gone down the same way more recently. Anyone who broke a treaty faced the same powers and the same fate. Julius and Augustus, he said, had understood these people far better. It was Galba, and the cut in their tribute, that filled them with hostility. Now they were enemies because slavery was easy on them. Rob them and strip them bare, and they would be friends again. Bold words. But Classicus and Tutor stuck to their betrayal, so Vocula turned his march around and fell back to Novesium. The Gauls set up camp in the fields two miles away. Centurions and soldiers kept going back and forth between the camps, and bribes bought them. The goal was a shocking first: a Roman army swearing loyalty to foreigners. And the proof of that huge crime? The death or chains of its legates. Most people told Vocula to run. He would not. He decided to stay and face it. So he called an assembly and spoke.

4:58 "I have never worried more about you, or less about myself. Let them get ready to destroy me. I'm glad. Death at the enemy's hands looks like the end of my misery, and with all these evils around me, I welcome it. But for you, I feel shame and pity. Nobody is lining up an enemy army against you. That would at least be a lawful war, the enemy's right. Instead Classicus wants to fight the Roman people with your hands. He waves an empire and an oath of the Gauls in your faces. Maybe our luck and courage have failed us for now. Have the old examples failed too? Remember all those times Roman legions chose death over being pushed off their post. Our allies have often watched their cities wiped out. They burned with their wives and children. All they got for their end was loyalty and reputation. Right now the legions at the Old Camp are starving under siege. Threats can't move them. Promises can't either. We have weapons. We have men. We have excellent camp defenses, and enough grain and supplies for a war of any length. Not long ago we even had money for a bonus. Call it a gift from Vespasian or from Vitellius, if you like. Either way, a Roman emperor gave it to you. You won so many wars. You beat the enemy again and again at Gelduba and at the Old Camp. Afraid of a battle line? That's beneath you. Still, we have the rampart and the walls. We can drag things out until help and armies come rushing in from the nearby provinces. Fine, say I'm the one who displeases you. There are other legates, tribunes, even a centurion or a common soldier. Don't let this monstrous news spread across the whole world: Civilis and Classicus invading Italy with you as their bodyguards. If Germans and Gauls lead you to the walls of the city, will you take up arms against your country? Just picturing that crime makes my soul shudder. Will you stand guard duty for Tutor the Treveran? Will a Batavian give the signal for battle? Will you fill up the ranks of the German bands? How will this crime end, once Roman legions line up against you? Will you wander around as deserters from deserters and traitors from traitors? Hated by the gods, caught between a new oath and an old one? Jupiter, best and greatest, we have honored you with so many triumphs over eight hundred and twenty years. And you, Quirinus, father of the city of Rome. I pray and beg you. Maybe you did not want this camp to stay clean and untouched under my command. Then at least don't let Tutor and Classicus stain it and make it foul. Give the Roman soldiers either their innocence, or a quick repentance that does no harm."

4:59 Hope, fear, shame. The speech stirred all three. Vocula walked off, thinking about his last move. His freedman (a former slave who had been set free) and his slave stopped him. He was rushing toward a most shameful death by his own hand. Classicus did not wait. He sent Aemilius Longinus, a deserter from the 1st legion, to speed up the murder. Chains were enough for the legates Herennius and Numisius. Then Classicus put on the symbols of Roman imperial power and walked into the camp. He was hardened to every crime. Still, he found only enough words to read the oath out loud. No speech. Just the oath. Everyone there swore loyalty to the empire of the Gauls. Vocula's killer got a move up to high rank. Everyone else got a reward based on how much they had helped with the crime. Then Tutor and Classicus split up the work. Tutor killed the tribunes at Mogontiacum, and he drove off the camp prefect. Both had refused. Next he surrounded the Agrippinenses with a strong force. He made them swear the same oath, and so did all the troops along the upper bank of the Rhine. Classicus, meanwhile, told the most corrupt of the men who had surrendered to go to the besieged. They were to offer a pardon if the besieged went along with the new situation. Otherwise: no hope. Hunger, the sword, the very worst. The men he sent set the example themselves.

4:60 Loyalty pulled the besieged one way and hunger pulled them the other. They were stuck between honor and disgrace. While they hesitated, the food ran out. The usual kind and the strange kind both. The pack animals went first, then the horses, and then every other animal that is unclean and disgusting. Need had made them all useful. In the end the men were pulling up bushes and roots, and the grass growing between the stones. Picture that. They had set an example of misery and endurance. Now they stained their splendid fame with a shameful ending, and sent envoys to Civilis, begging for their lives. Nobody listened until they swore loyalty to the Gauls. Then Civilis made a deal over the loot from the camp. He gave them guards to hold back the money, the camp servants and the baggage. Those guards were also to escort the men out, carrying nothing. About five miles along, the Germans jumped up and attacked the column. The column never saw it coming. The toughest fighters died right where they stood, and many more died running in every direction. The rest ran back to the camp. Civilis complained, and scolded the Germans for breaking their word through such a crime. Was it an act? Or could he not hold back their rage? Nobody is sure. They looted the camp and threw torches inside. The fire swallowed everyone who had survived the battle.

4:61 Civilis kept a vow, the kind barbarians make. Ever since he took up arms against the Romans, he had let his hair grow long and dyed it red. Now the legions were slaughtered. Time to cut it off. People also said he handed some of the prisoners to his little son. The boy got to shoot them with a child's arrows and javelins. Civilis did not swear the Gauls' oath himself. He didn't make a single Batavian swear it either. He was leaning on Germany's strength. Say it came to a fight with the Gauls over who ruled. He figured he would win through his famous name and greater power. Munius Lupercus, a legate of a legion, was sent to Veleda among the gifts. Who was she? An unmarried woman of the Bructeri tribe, and she ruled a wide stretch of land. Germans have an old habit of seeing many women as prophetesses. As the superstition grows, those women turn into goddesses in their eyes. Back then, Veleda's power was at its height. She had called the German victories in advance, and the destruction of the legions too. Lupercus never reached her. Killed on the road. A few centurions and tribunes born in Gaul were kept as a pledge of the alliance. The winter camps of the cohorts, cavalry wings and legions were torn down and burned. Only the ones at Mogontiacum and Vindonissa were left.

4:62 The 16th legion got its orders. It and the auxiliaries who had surrendered with it must march from Novesium to the colony of the Treveri. They had a set day to be out of camp. Until then, they stewed. The biggest cowards shook as they thought of the men cut down at the Old Camp. The better ones burned with shame and disgrace. "What a march this will be!" they said. "Who leads us down the road? Everything now sits with the men who decide whether we live or die." Others did not care about the disgrace, and strapped money and their dearest things to their own bodies. Some readied their weapons and buckled on their gear, as if heading into battle. Then the hour to leave came, gloomier than anyone expected. Inside the rampart, their ugliness had been easy to hide. Out on the open plain, in broad daylight, their shame showed. Picture it. The emperors' images had been torn down. The standards were carried without their usual honors, and the Gauls' flags flashed on every side. The column crept along in silence, like one long funeral procession. Claudius Sanctus led them. He had lost an eye, his face was hideous, and his mind was weaker still. Then the other legion joined them from its camp at Bonna, and the disgrace doubled. Ouch. News of the captured legions spread. People who had trembled at the name of Rome not long before came running out of their fields and houses. They poured out everywhere, enjoying the strange sight far too much. The Picentine cavalry wing could not stand the jeering. Sanctus made promises and threats, and they ignored both. They rode off to Mogontiacum. By chance they ran into Longinus, the man who had killed Vocula, and hit him with a shower of javelins. It was a start at paying off their guilt. Nobody else changed course. The legions camped before the walls of the Treveri.

4:63 So what about the colony of the Agrippinenses? Civilis and Classicus were elated by their success, and they wondered whether to let their armies loot it. Their savage tempers and greed for loot pulled them toward wiping the city out. On the other side stood the rules of war. So did the good a name for mercy does men starting a new empire. Not an easy call. Civilis felt one more pull, the memory of a kindness. At the start of the revolt, his son had been arrested in the colony. He was kept in honorable custody. But the tribes across the Rhine hated the city for its wealth and growth. They saw only one way for the war to end. Make the city open to every German, or tear it down and scatter the Ubii too. Open it or tear it down.

4:64 Envoys came from the Tencteri, a tribe on the far side of the Rhine. Their orders were to deliver a message to the council of the Agrippinenses. The boldest envoy gave the speech. Subtle, it was not. "Thanks to our shared gods, and most of all to Mars, you are back in the body and name of Germany. Congratulations! At last you will be free among the free. Until today, the Romans have shut off our rivers and our land. In a way, they shut off the sky too. Why? To stop us from talking and meeting. Worse, we were men born for arms, yet we had to meet unarmed and almost naked, under guard, and for a fee. Insulting, right? So if you want our friendship and alliance to last forever, tear down the walls of your colony. They are fortresses of slavery. Even wild animals forget their courage when you pen them up. Then slaughter every Roman in your territory. Freedom and masters don't mix easily. Throw the property of the dead into one big pile, so nobody can hide anything or split their cause from ours. Let us and you live on both banks, as our ancestors did. Nature opened light and day to all people. In the same way, she opened every land to brave men. Go back to the customs and way of life of your fathers. Break off the pleasures that give the Romans more power over their subjects than weapons do. You are a pure people, unmixed, and you have forgotten slavery. You will live as equals. Or you will rule over others."

4:65 The Agrippinenses asked for time. They were scared of the future, so they could not accept. But the present was too dangerous to say no out loud. So here is what they said: "We reached for freedom too fast, without thinking it through. All we wanted was to join you and the other Germans, our own blood. About the walls: Roman armies are gathering right now, and strong walls keep us safer than a pile of rubble. The foreigners from Italy or the provinces who lived on our land? The war wiped them out, or they ran back home. Other families settled here long ago, married into ours, and had children. This is their homeland. Surely you don't want us to kill our own parents, brothers and children. We cancel the taxes and the fees on trade. Crossings can stay unguarded, but only by day and without weapons, until these new rights harden into custom. Let Civilis and Veleda judge between us. If they approve, we have a treaty." It worked. The Tencteri calmed down. Envoys with gifts went to Civilis and Veleda, and everything was settled the way the Agrippinenses wanted. But the envoys never got to see Veleda or speak to her. Keeping her hidden was meant to make people respect her more. She lived in a tall tower. A relative of hers, picked for the job, carried the questions and answers back and forth. Like a messenger of a god.

4:66 Civilis felt stronger now, with the Agrippinenses on his side. So he set out to win over the neighboring states. Any that said no would get war. He took the Sunuci and organized their young men into cohorts. Then Claudius Labeo blocked him. He had thrown together a force of Betasii, Tungri and Nervii in a hurry. His edge was his position. Earlier, he had grabbed the bridge over the river Mosa. The fight in the narrow pass was a toss-up. Then Germans swam across and struck Labeo from behind. At that same moment, Civilis threw himself into the column of the Tungri. A sudden bold impulse, or a plan? Nobody can say. He shouted: "We didn't take up arms so that the Batavians and Treveri could rule over nations. Arrogance like that is far from us. Accept our alliance. I'm crossing over to you, as your general or as one of your soldiers. You pick." The crowd wobbled. Swords started going back into their sheaths. Then two leading Tungri, Campanus and Juvenalis, handed the whole tribe over to him. Bad day for Labeo, who fled before anyone could surround him. Civilis also took the Betasii and Nervii under his protection and added them to his forces. Huge resources were his now. The states were either shaken or leaning his way on their own.

4:67 Julius Sabinus made his move. He threw down the monuments of the treaty with Rome and had himself hailed as Caesar. Then he drove a huge, ragged mob of his own people against the Sequani, a neighboring state still loyal to Rome. The Sequani did not flinch. They fought, and fortune sided with the better cause. The Lingones were beaten. Sabinus ran from the battle he had charged into so rashly, as scared as he had been reckless. Then came his big idea. He wanted word of his death to spread, so he set fire to the country house where he was hiding. Yes, the house he was hiding in. People bought it. They believed he had died there by his own hand. He hadn't, and I'll tell you how in the right place. Nine years hidden and alive. Friends who stood by him. And his wife Epponina, whose example was something to see. For now, the Sequani's win knocked the push out of the war. Slowly the Gallic states came to their senses and began to think about what was right, and about treaties. The Remi took the lead. They announced across Gaul that delegates should meet in a joint assembly. Freedom or peace: which did Gaul want?

4:68 Rome heard everything in a worse light, and Mucianus lost sleep over it. The generals were excellent men, and he had already chosen Annius Gallus and Petilius Cerialis. But could they carry the whole war? Maybe not. And the city could not be left without a ruler, with everyone afraid of Domitian's wild desires. Primus Antonius and Arrius Varus were under suspicion too, as I said. Varus commanded the Praetorian Guard, so he still had power and weapons. Mucianus removed him from the post. Then, so Varus wouldn't be left without consolation, he made him head of the grain supply. Some consolation. Domitian wasn't unfriendly to Varus, so Mucianus had to soften his mind. His answer was Arrecinus Clemens, who got the command of the Praetorian Guard. Clemens was tied to Vespasian's house by marriage. Domitian was very fond of him, and Mucianus kept making the case: Clemens's father had filled that post with distinction under Caligula, the same name would please the soldiers, and Clemens, though a senator, could handle both jobs. The city's most distinguished men were chosen to go along, and others got in through influence. Domitian and Mucianus both got ready, but not in the same spirit. Domitian was in a hurry, full of hope and youth. Mucianus kept finding delays to rein in his fiery companion. His fear was simple. If Domitian reached the army, youthful wildness and bad advisers might wreck the chances for war and for peace. So the legions marched into Gaul. The victorious 8th, 11th and 13th went. So did the 21st, from the Vitellianist army, and the 2nd, made up of new recruits. Some crossed the Pennine and Cottian Alps, and some took the Graian. The 14th legion was called in from Britain, and the 6th and 1st came from Spain. Word of the coming army reached the Gallic states, which were already leaning toward gentler advice. So they met in the land of the Remi. The envoys of the Treveri were already waiting, led by Julius Valentinus, the fiercest promoter of the war. He came with a prepared speech, and out poured every complaint people make against great empires, plus heaps of insults against the Roman people. He loved trouble and was good at stirring up revolts. His ranting pleased many. It worked.

4:69 Then someone had to talk sense. Julius Auspex, one of the leading men of the Remi, spoke up for Rome. He praised its power and the blessings of peace. Cowards can start a war, he said. The bravest are the ones who have to risk their lives fighting it. And the Roman legions were almost overhead. Picture that. He held back the sensible with appeals to respect and loyalty, and the young with warnings of danger and fear. So the crowd praised Valentinus's spirit but followed Auspex's advice. Everyone agrees the Treveri and Lingones had lost standing with the other Gauls. They had sided with Verginius when Vindex rose up, and the rivalry between the provinces put many people off too. Who would be head of the war? Where would the right to lead come from, and the auspices? And if it all went well, which city would be the seat of power? There was no victory yet. The quarrels had started anyway. Some boasted about their treaties, some about their wealth and strength, and others about how old their people were. They were sick of the future, so they settled for the present. Letters went to the Treveri in the name of the Gallic states, telling them to lay down their arms. Pardon could be had, the letters said, and people stood ready to plead for them if they were sorry. Valentinus blocked the way again and shut his state's ears. He was better at making speeches than at getting ready for war.

4:70 The rebels had started something they weren't ready for. The Treveri, the Lingones and the other rebel states acted as if the danger were small. Even their leaders weren't working together. Civilis was wandering the trackless wilds of the Belgae, hunting for Claudius Labeo. He wanted to capture him or drive him out. Classicus lounged around most days, enjoying an empire as though he had already won it. Tutor was slow too. He should have posted garrisons along the upper bank of the Rhine and in the steep passes of the Alps. Not a great plan. Meanwhile Rome's side was moving. The 21st legion came in by way of Vindonissa. Sextilius Felix came through Raetia with auxiliary cohorts. Both forces broke into the province. A cavalry wing called the Singularians joined them, one that Vitellius had raised and that had since gone over to Vespasian. Its commander was Julius Briganticus, the son of Civilis's sister. He hated his uncle, and his uncle hated him back. Family feuds like that are usually the bitterest kind. Tutor beefed up the Treveri's forces with a fresh draft of Vangiones, Caeracates and Triboci, plus veteran infantry and cavalry. He also had legionaries. He had corrupted them with hope or beaten them down with fear. At first they killed a cohort that Sextilius Felix had sent ahead. Then the Roman generals and army got close. Suddenly those legionaries went back to their duty in an "honorable" desertion, and the Triboci, Vangiones and Caeracates followed them. Tutor kept the Treveri behind him, dodged Mogontiacum and fell back to Bingium. He trusted the spot, because he had smashed the bridge over the river Nava. It wasn't enough. The cohorts Sextilius led attacked and found a ford. Tutor was betrayed and beaten. The blow stunned the Treveri. The common people threw down their weapons and scattered through the fields. Some leading men wanted to look like the first to quit the war. They fled to states that had never given up the Roman alliance. Of course they did. Now the legions. I told you earlier how they were moved from Novesium and Bonna into Treveri territory. They swore loyalty to Vespasian on their own while Valentinus was away. Then he came back in a rage, set on throwing everything into chaos and ruin again. The legions pulled out to the Mediomatrici, a friendly state. Valentinus and Tutor dragged the Treveri back into war. They also killed the legates Herennius and Numisius, so the bond of crime would grow tighter and hope of pardon would shrink. No way back.

4:71 Then Petilius Cerialis reached Mogontiacum. People's hopes went up. He was itching for a fight, and he was better at despising enemies than at guarding against them. His fierce words set the soldiers on fire. The moment they could meet the enemy, he promised, there would be no delay for battle. Next he sent the Gallic recruits home to their states with a simple message: the legions were enough to defend the empire. The allies could go back to the work of peace. Relax, he said. The war was as good as over now that Roman hands had taken it on. Sure it was. The Gauls grew more obedient. With their young men home again, they bore the taxes more easily. And since they were looked down on, they were readier to do their duty. Funny how that works. Then the news reached Civilis and Classicus. Tutor beaten. The Treveri cut down. Everything going the enemy's way. They panicked and hurried to gather their scattered forces. Over and over, messengers went to Valentinus with a warning: do not risk everything on one battle. All of this made Cerialis move faster. He sent men to the Mediomatrici to turn the legions there against the enemy by a shorter route. He gathered the soldiers at Mogontiacum, along with the ones he had brought. In three days' march he reached Rigodulum, where Valentinus was already waiting with a large force of Treveri. Mountains and the river Mosella walled the place off, and he had added ditches and barricades of stones. Did it matter? Not to Cerialis. He ordered his infantry to break in and sent his cavalry up the hill, because he despised an enemy thrown together in a hurry. Whatever the position gave them, he judged, his own men's courage would outweigh it. The climb slowed for a moment while missiles flew overhead, but then came hand-to-hand fighting. The enemy was knocked loose and thrown headlong, like a building falling down. Part of the cavalry rode around by easier ridges. They captured the most noble of the Belgae, and Valentinus, the enemy's leader, was among them. The battle was over.

4:72 The next day Cerialis marched into the colony of the Treveri. His soldiers wanted to wipe the whole city out. Listen to them. "Classicus and Tutor call this place home. Their crime trapped our legions and cut them down. And Cremona? What had it done to earn that fate? It was ripped out of Italy's heart for delaying the winners one night. Yet here on the German border sits a city untouched, gloating over the plunder of armies and the murder of generals. Give the loot to the treasury. Fire and the ruins of a rebel colony will satisfy us, because they pay us back for all those wrecked camps." Cerialis feared the shame of being thought to have taught his soldiers wild cruelty, so he held back their anger. They obeyed. The civil war was over, and they were gentler toward foreigners. Then something else caught their attention: the sad sight of the legions summoned from the Mediomatrici. Those men stood with their eyes on the ground, heavy with the knowledge of their disgrace. When the armies met, nobody greeted anybody. The legions gave no answer to men who tried to comfort or encourage them, and they hid in their tents. They even avoided daylight. Danger hadn't stunned them. Neither had fear. Shame had. Even the winning soldiers were stunned. They didn't dare speak or plead aloud, so they asked for pardon with tears and silence. Finally Cerialis soothed them. Fate had driven it all, he kept saying, through the quarrels of soldiers and generals, or the enemy's tricks. They should count that day as the first of their service and their oath. Neither the emperor nor he, he said, would remember their earlier crimes. Then they were taken into the same camp. An order went out to every company. No throwing mutiny in a comrade's face during a quarrel. No throwing defeat either.

4:73 Next came a meeting, because he called the Treveri and the Lingones together and spoke to them. "I've never worked at being a good speaker. I've shown Rome's courage with weapons. But with you, words count most. You judge good and bad by what troublemakers say, not by what things really are. So I'll say a few things, now that the war is beaten, and you'll gain more from hearing them than I will from saying them. Roman generals and emperors came into your land and the rest of Gaul, and greed had nothing to do with it. Your ancestors called them in, worn out by quarrels that ran all the way to ruin. The Germans had been brought in to help, and they put slavery on friend and enemy alike. How many battles did we fight against the Cimbri and the Teutones? How hard did our armies work, and how did the German wars end? You know well enough. We didn't settle on the Rhine to protect Italy. We did it so that no second Ariovistus could seize the kingdom of Gaul. Do you really think Civilis, the Batavians and the tribes from across the Rhine hold you dearer than their ancestors held your fathers and grandfathers? Hardly. The Germans have always had the same reason to cross into Gaul. Lust. Greed. A love of changing homes. They want out of their marshes and wastelands, and they want this rich soil, and you along with it. Freedom and fine-sounding names? Those are like a coat of paint on the same old plan. Nobody has ever wanted someone else's slavery and rule for himself without using those very words. Nobody."

4:74 "Gaul always had kings and wars, until you came under our law. We were provoked again and again, but we used the right of victory in one way only. We added to your burden just what it takes to protect the peace. Think about it. Nations can't have peace without armies, armies can't exist without pay, and pay can't happen without taxes. Everything else we share equally. You often command our legions yourselves, and you rule these provinces and others. Nothing is kept apart from you or shut off. The benefits of the good emperors reach everyone alike, even people far away. Cruel ones land hard on whoever is nearby. So put up with your rulers' wild living or greed. Treat it like a bad harvest, too much rain, or any other evil of nature. There will be vices as long as there are men, but they don't last forever, and better times make up for them. Unless, of course, you hope for a milder rule under Tutor and Classicus. Or you think armies to keep out the Germans and Britons will be raised with lower taxes than now. Will they? Suppose the Romans were driven out (the gods forbid). What would follow, except wars among all the nations? This structure grew together over eight hundred years of good fortune and discipline. Tear it down, and you wreck the people doing the tearing, like a roof falling on the men who pulled out its beams. But the greatest danger is yours. You have gold and wealth, and those are the main causes of wars. So love peace and honor it. Love and honor the city too, the one we hold by the same right, winners and losers alike. Let fortune's lessons, good and bad, teach you. Don't choose stubbornness and ruin over obedience and safety." Words like these calmed the Treveri and Lingones and lifted their spirits. They had braced for much harsher treatment.

4:75 The Treveri were still held down by the army that had won when Civilis and Classicus wrote to Cerialis. The letters said Vespasian was dead, though somebody was keeping the news quiet. Rome and Italy, they said, were worn out from civil war. Mucianus and Domitian? Empty names with no strength behind them. Like offices with nobody sitting in them. Cerialis could have the rule of Gaul, the letters said, and they would settle for the borders of their own states. Or he could have a fight, and they wouldn't say no to that either. Cerialis never answered. He sent the messenger and the letters straight to Domitian. Meanwhile the enemy closed in from every side, with their forces split up. Plenty of people blamed Cerialis for letting them join up. He could have cut them off while they were apart. Only then did the Roman army dig a ditch and throw up a rampart around its camp. Until that day it had camped there with no protection at all. Reckless.

4:76 The Germans couldn't agree on a plan. Civilis wanted to wait. The tribes from across the Rhine were coming, and their terrifying name alone would wear down what was left of Rome's strength. The Gauls? Just loot for the winners. Still, the Belgae, the real strength, stood with them, openly or in their hearts. Tutor said waiting only helped Rome. Armies were gathering from every side. A legion had crossed over from Britain, others had been called in from Spain, and more were on the way from Italy. These weren't fresh recruits but old soldiers who knew real war. And the Germans Civilis was hoping for? Nobody could command them or rule them, because they did whatever they felt like. Not exactly reliable. Worse, the Romans had more money and gifts, the only way to win those Germans over. Nobody was so eager to fight that he would pick danger over a rest, when the pay came out the same. Strike now, Tutor said, and it looked different. Cerialis had only the leftovers of the German army, legions bound by treaties to the Gauls. They had just beaten Valentinus's messy crowd, which they never expected to do. That would feed their recklessness, and their general's, and they would dare again. This time they would run into Civilis and Classicus, not some inexperienced kid who practiced speeches and meetings more than swords and armor. One look at the two of them, and the fear would come flooding back. Running away. Starving. Begging for their lives, the way they had so many times as prisoners. The Treveri and Lingones were not held in line by goodwill, and once their fear passed, they would grab their weapons again. Classicus ended the argument by backing Tutor. They acted at once.

4:77 The attack came in three parts. The Ubii and Lingones held the center. The Batavian cohorts took the right wing, and the Bructeri and Tencteri took the left. Some of the enemy came over the mountains, and the rest came along the road between the hills and the river Mosella. They hit so fast that Cerialis never saw it coming. He hadn't spent the night in camp, and when word reached him he was still lying in bed in his bedroom. The fighting had started, they said, and his men were losing. Picture it. Not how a general wants to start his day. He snapped at the messengers for panicking. Then the whole disaster was right in front of his eyes. The legions' camp had been broken into and the cavalry scattered. The enemy held the middle of the bridge over the Mosella, the bridge that joins the colony to the land beyond. Cerialis stayed calm. He grabbed the fleeing men with his own hand. Then he charged ahead with no armor on, straight into the flying missiles. It was lucky recklessness, and it worked, because the bravest men rushed to join him. He won the bridge back and held it with a handpicked force. Then he returned to the camp. Scattered groups from the legions captured at Novesium and Bonna stood there. Few soldiers were left around the standards, and the eagles were almost surrounded. Burning with anger, Cerialis shouted: "It isn't Flaccus or Vocula you're deserting. There's no treachery here. I've nothing to excuse, except that I rashly believed you had forgotten your treaty with the Gauls and remembered your oath to Rome. I'll be counted with Numisius and Herennius. Then all your commanders will have been brought down, either by their own soldiers or by the enemy. Go, tell Vespasian. Or tell Civilis and Classicus, since they're nearer. Tell them you left your commander on the battlefield. Legions are coming. They won't leave me unavenged, and they won't leave you unpunished."

4:78 Every word was true. The tribunes and prefects heaped the same blame on their men. The soldiers formed up by cohorts and companies, because the line had no room to spread out. Enemies were everywhere. The fight raged inside the rampart, with tents and baggage in the way. It was like a brawl in a crowded hallway. Tutor, Classicus and Civilis each ran their own part of the battle. They fired up their men: the Gauls for freedom, the Batavians for glory, the Germans for loot. For a while the enemy won everywhere. Then the 21st legion, which had more space than the others, packed itself into a tight block. It stopped the charge, and soon it drove the enemy back. And then the winners suddenly changed their minds and ran. Why? The gods had a hand in it. The winners said themselves that some cohorts had scared them. Those cohorts had scattered at the first charge, but now they were regrouping on the hilltops and looked like fresh reinforcements. But what really stopped them was an ugly scramble for plunder among the winners themselves. They forgot all about the enemy. Great timing. Cerialis had nearly thrown everything away through carelessness. Now he fixed it with firmness. Riding his luck, he captured the enemy's camp and wrecked it the very same day.

4:79 The soldiers got no long rest. The Agrippinenses were begging for help. They also offered pledges of the alliance: Civilis's wife and sister, and Classicus's daughter, who had been left in their care. In the meantime they had slaughtered the Germans scattered in their houses. That brought fear, and it made their pleas for help real. Cerialis had to hurry, before the enemy could rebuild its strength and gather itself for hope or revenge. Civilis was heading that way too, and he was far from weak. His most fired-up cohort was still in one piece, made up of Chauci and Frisii. It was stationed at Tolbiacum, on the Agrippinenses' border. Then sad news turned him aside. What had happened to that cohort? A trick. The Agrippinenses had laid on a lavish feast, with plenty of wine, until the Germans fell asleep. Then they locked the doors, set the houses on fire, and burned the Germans inside. The cohort was gone. At the same time Cerialis came to the rescue by a fast march. Another fear boxed Civilis in. The 14th legion, joined by the fleet from Britain, might strike the Batavians where the Ocean, the open sea, surrounds them. But the legate Fabius Priscus, a senior commander, marched the legion overland into the land of the Nervii and Tungri. Those states surrendered. The Canninefates attacked the fleet on their own, and most of the ships were sunk or captured. The same Canninefates scattered a mob of Nervii, who had risen up on their own to fight for the Romans. Classicus won a battle too, against cavalry that Cerialis had sent ahead to Novesium. Small losses, but lots of them. Bit by bit, they chipped away at the recent victory's reputation. The shine was wearing off.

4:80 Around the same days, Mucianus had Vitellius's son killed. His claim: the trouble would never end unless he stamped out the seeds of war. He also refused to let Primus Antonius join Domitian's companions. Why? Mucianus was uneasy about how popular Antonius was with the soldiers. He was uneasy about the man's pride too, which couldn't stand equals, let alone superiors. So Antonius went to Vespasian. The welcome was not what he had hoped for. But it was not hostile either. The emperor was pulled two ways. On one side stood the services of Antonius, who had without doubt finished the war. On the other stood the letters of Mucianus. Meanwhile, others went after Antonius as hostile and puffed up. They piled on charges from his earlier life. And Antonius didn't help himself. He kept causing offense with his arrogance, going on and on about everything he had earned, like a kid waiting for a gold star. He called other men cowards, and he called Caecina a prisoner and a man who had surrendered. Bit by bit, people started to think of him as a lightweight, someone not worth much. Friends on the surface. Not underneath.

4:81 Vespasian was stuck in Alexandria for months. He was waiting for the summer winds to blow on their regular days, and for a dependable sea. Strange things happened during the wait, and they showed heaven was on his side and the gods seemed to be leaning his way. One man from the common people of Alexandria had sick eyes, and everyone knew it. He threw himself at the emperor's knees and begged, groaning, for a cure for his blindness. The god Serapis had told him to. This people worships Serapis above all other gods, and they are devoted to superstitions of every kind. The man wanted the emperor to wet his cheeks and the rims of his eyes with spit from the emperor's own mouth. Another man had a sick hand. The same god backed him, and he asked the emperor to step on the hand with his foot. Picture it. At first Vespasian laughed and brushed them off. But they kept at it. He worried about looking like a fool. Then their pleading, and the words of his flatterers, gave him hope. Back and forth. In the end he told the doctors to judge. Could human help fix blindness and weakness like that? The doctors gave different answers. For the first man, the power of sight hadn't been eaten away. It would come back if the things blocking it were removed. For the second, the joints had slipped into a bent position. A healing force could make them whole again. The doctors added their own thoughts. Maybe the gods wanted this, and the emperor had been chosen as their servant. Either way, a cure would bring the emperor the glory. A failure would leave the poor sufferers with the ridicule. Easy odds. So Vespasian decided his luck was wide open and nothing was too unbelievable now. He did what he was told, wearing a cheerful face, while the crowd stood on tiptoe. The hand was back in use at once. Daylight shone again for the blind man. Everyone who was there still tells the story of both. Nobody gains anything by lying now.

4:82 After that, Vespasian wanted more. He felt a stronger urge to visit the god's holy seat and ask about the empire's affairs. He had everyone kept out of the temple. Inside, he focused on the god. Then he glanced back, and there stood Basilides, one of the leading Egyptians. Vespasian knew him very well, and he knew Basilides was sick and held back many days' journey from Alexandria. Impossible. So Vespasian quizzed the priests. Had Basilides come into the temple that day? He asked people he ran into if they'd seen the man in the city. Finally he sent out horsemen. They came back with the answer: at that very moment, Basilides had been eighty miles away. Vespasian took it as a divine appearance. And the name Basilides told him what the answer meant.

4:83 Where did this god come from? Roman writers haven't made that well known yet. The Egyptian priests tell it their own way. King Ptolemy was the first Macedonian to make Egypt's power safe. He was busy adding walls, temples and religious rites to Alexandria, a brand new city. Then a dream came. A young man showed up in it, extraordinarily good looking and bigger than any human. He told Ptolemy to send his most trusted friends to Pontus and bring back his statue. The kingdom would get good luck from it. And the city that received the statue would become great and famous. At that moment, Ptolemy saw the young man lifted into the sky in a huge blaze of fire. The sign and the wonder shook him. So he told his night visions to the Egyptian priests, since understanding such things was their custom. But they knew little about Pontus or foreign lands. Ptolemy asked Timotheus instead, an Athenian of the Eumolpid family. He had called Timotheus from Eleusis to run the sacred rites. What was this religion, and which god was it? Timotheus questioned people who had traveled to Pontus. He found out that a city there was called Sinope. Not far from it stood a temple, long known among the locals as the temple of Jupiter of the underworld. A woman's statue stood beside it, and most people called it Proserpina. Then Ptolemy's nerve gave out. Kings scare easily. Once he felt safe again, pleasure mattered more to him than religion. Slowly he let the whole thing slide and turned to other concerns. Until the same vision came back. This time it was scarier and pushier. It announced ruin for him and his kingdom if he didn't carry out the orders. So he had envoys and gifts made ready for King Scydrothemis, who ruled the people of Sinope then. As they were about to sail, he told them to visit the Pythian Apollo. The sea was kind to them. Apollo's answer was crystal clear. Bring back the statue of Apollo's father. Leave his sister's behind.

4:84 At Sinope, the envoys handed over everything their king had sent. Gifts, requests, orders. All of it went to Scydrothemis. He didn't know what to do. Part of him dreaded the god's power. Part of him feared the people, who were against the plan. The envoys' gifts and promises kept tugging him back the other way. Three years dragged by like this. Ptolemy never gave up. He kept begging, raised the rank of the envoys, sent more ships and piled on more gold. Then a threatening figure appeared to Scydrothemis and told him to stop dragging his feet on the god's plans. He still hesitated. So the disasters came, and the sicknesses, and the clear anger of the heavens. Each day was worse than the last. The pressure wore him down. He called an assembly and laid out everything: the god's orders, his own visions and Ptolemy's, and the trouble closing in. The crowd turned on the king. They envied Egypt, they feared for themselves, and they surrounded the temple. The story most people tell goes like this. The god himself, of his own free will, boarded the ships that had been pulled up on the shore. Strange to say, they crossed that huge stretch of sea and landed at Alexandria on the third day. A temple big enough for such a city went up at a place called Rhacotis. A small shrine to Serapis and Isis had stood there since long before. That's the most popular account of how the god began and how he got here. I know some tell it differently. They say he was brought from Seleucia, a city in Syria, under the Ptolemy of the third generation. Others name the same Ptolemy but say the god came from Memphis. Memphis was once a famous city, the pillar of old Egypt. So who is he, really? Many say Aesculapius, because he heals sick bodies. Some say Osiris, the oldest god of those peoples. Quite a few say Jupiter, ruler of everything. Most of all, people say Pluto, the god of the underworld, going by the signs on his statue. Or they're only guessing.

4:85 Good news beat them to the Alps. Before Domitian and Mucianus got near, word came about the Treveri. The best proof of the win? The enemy leader, Valentinus. His spirit was not broken one bit, and his face still showed the pride he had fought with. He got a hearing only so people could see what kind of man he was. Then he was sentenced. At his execution, someone taunted him that his country had been taken. Valentinus said he took that as a comfort in dying. Then Mucianus pulled out a plan he had kept hidden for a long time, and he made it sound brand new. The enemy's strength, he said, had been broken by the kindness of the gods. So it would hardly look right for Domitian to step into someone else's glory with the war almost over. If the empire's stability or Gaul's safety were at stake, the Caesar belonged on the battle line. But the Canninefates and Batavians? Leave them to lesser commanders. Domitian could stay in Lugdunum and show the power and good fortune of imperial rule from close by. Why get mixed up in small dangers? He would not be missing when a bigger one came. How convenient.

4:86 Everyone saw through the trick. But showing respect meant not catching him at it, so they came to Lugdunum. From there, Domitian is believed to have sent secret messages to Cerialis. The point was to test Cerialis's loyalty: would Cerialis hand over the army and the command if Domitian showed up in person? Was Domitian planning a war against his father? Or gathering money and men against his brother? Nobody knew. Cerialis dodged the whole thing with careful tact and brushed it off as the empty, childish wish of a boy. Meanwhile Domitian saw the older officers looking down on his youth. So he gave up even the modest government duties he used to carry out. He put on a show of simplicity and modesty, and buried himself in secrecy. He also pretended to love books and poetry. Quite a performance. It hid his real mind and kept him out of his brother's jealous sight. But he had misread his brother, whose nature was different and milder. He took it the opposite way. Completely backward.
