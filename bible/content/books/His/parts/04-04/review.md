# Review: Tacitus, Histories, Book 4 (His, part 04-04), translation only

Scope (Matt's instruction for this pass): the local model's draft Conversational English Translation (`plain.txt`, 86 verses) checked verse by verse against the Church and Brodribb base (`node tools/base-lines.js His 4`) and the Latin in `texts/orig/His.json` key `lat`. Notes, topics, variants, chapter pages and context are not reviewed. `node tools/build.js --check His/04-04` shows no error that points at a plain.txt line (its one error is the expected missing chapter page).

## Verdict

The draft is not usable as it stands. Its voice is mostly right (contractions, short sentences, no curly quotes), but it is a heavy paraphrase that in many verses gets who does what to whom wrong, drops whole thoughts, and adds facts the author never wrote: Lucius Vitellius surrenders "to Otho" (4:2); the Senate makes Vespasian and Titus "joint emperors" (4:3); Domitian is called "emperor" (4:2, 4:40); an invented "Clemens" joins a conspiracy (4:55); Regulus is said to have "paid Otho" and Montanus attacks the wrong man (4:42); a "second story" is added to the Capitol (4:53); Festus "watches Piso die" (4:50). Several speeches are reversed (the Roman side "has more cash" in 4:76, the Gauls are "fighting for Rome" in 4:17, "that chance is gone" in 4:42, "Cerialis' men" loot in 4:78), and the chunked 75-word pieces leave seams: doubled passages (4:83, 4:25, 4:32), unclosed quotes in 22 verses, and speakers who slide into "we" (4:15, 4:56, 4:65). Every verse needs at least a light edit. The findings below give the exact replacement wording for the serious ones. The local model's own flag file marks only length and none of these errors.

Count of numbered findings: **0 critical, 86 major, 95 minor (total 181)**. No finding is critical because the draft invents no source or reading; the major ones are meaning errors and dropped or added facts that must be fixed before the notes are written.

## Findings (in verse order, then whole-part items)

Fix wording is a model; the fixer may adjust the English as long as the thought matches the base and the Latin. Every source check was done in this session against `base-lines.js` output and `texts/orig/His.json`; where a finding rests on the Latin, the Latin words are quoted. Where I give no Latin, the finding rests on the English base alone.

1. [major] 4:1: Broken quotation and glue. `They'd say, "Vitellianists might be in here. Here, they started busting into homes.` The quote never closes and "Here, they started busting into homes" is a mangled seam. Base: they pretended Vitellianists might be hidden there; this was the first step to breaking into private houses and, if resisted, a pretext for slaughter. Fix: `They'd claim Vitellianists might be hiding there. That was how the home break-ins started, and if anyone resisted, they had their excuse to kill.`
2. [major] 4:1: Last sentence drops half the thought. Base: the worst men can do the most in stirring up tumult, but peace cannot be set up without virtue. Fix: `Bad men are at their best stirring up trouble, but keeping the peace takes good character.`
3. [minor] 4:1: "when Vitellius and Otho's soldiers were in charge" replaces "the license of the Vitellianist and Othonianist soldiery" and loses the faction names the brief says to use. Fix: `until people looked back with regret even on the wild behavior of the Vitellianist and Othonianist soldiers, which had once seemed so hateful.`
4. [minor] 4:1: "They'd done a great job causing trouble" adds sarcasm. Base: the party leaders were energetic in kindling civil strife but could not check the abuse of victory. Fix: `The party leaders had worked hard to kindle the civil war, but they couldn't stop their troops from abusing the victory.`
5. [major] 4:2: Wrong who-does-what. Latin: "nec cunctatus est Vitellius seque et cohortis arbitrio victoris permittere" (Lucius Vitellius surrenders to the victor). Draft: "handing himself and his troops over to Otho" is false and invented. Fix: `Vitellius didn't hesitate. He handed himself and his troops over to the mercy of the winner.`
6. [major] 4:2: Garbled demand. Latin: the frightened country, ready for servitude, demanded that Lucius Vitellius with his cohorts be cut off as he came back from Tarracina and that the last of the war be stamped out. Draft: "willing to accept Vitellius' rule. They begged for him to be stopped at Tarracina" reverses the attitude and the sense. Fix: `The terrified country was ready to bow to the new rule and kept demanding that Lucius Vitellius and his cohorts be cut off as they came back from Tarracina, so the last sparks of the war could be stamped out.`
7. [major] 4:2: "Domitian took over as emperor" is wrong. Base and Latin: he had received the name and residence of Caesar. Fix: `Domitian had taken the title of Caesar and moved into the Caesars' residence, but he hadn't turned to the work of ruling yet. He played the emperor's son with drunken parties and affairs instead.` Delete "hitting the ground running" ("debauchery and intrigue" in the base; Latin "stupris et adulteriis" is sexual misconduct, so "partying hard and plotting" also misses "adultery" -- PG-13 level, state it plainly: `with scandalous affairs`).
8. [minor] 4:2: "like they were loot from a battlefield" drops the name Cremona. Fix: `from the emperor's household as if they were the spoils of Cremona`.
9. [minor] 4:2: "Everyone wanted the war to end" is an invented summary; delete (the demand is in the Tarracina sentence). "Arrius Varus was Prefect of the Praetorian Guard" fine but capitalize consistently (see the spelling item). "Ill-starred arms" dropped: `threw down their unlucky weapons, as angry as they were afraid`.
10. [minor] 4:2: "Just as cruel as his brother" -> Latin "par vitiis" (equal in vices); "more caution" -> Latin "vigilantior" (more alert). Fix: `He was as rotten as his brother, though he'd been sharper while his brother ruled. He hadn't so much shared his brother's rise as been dragged down by his fall.`
11. [major] 4:3: Wrong ruler. Latin: unrest "contumacia adversus principem" (defiance of the emperor, i.e. Vespasian). Draft: "not because they opposed Otho". Fix: `but because of rivalries between the towns, not defiance of the new emperor.`
12. [major] 4:3: "The Senate made Vespasian and Titus joint emperors" is wrong. Latin: "ipsi consulatus cum Tito filio, praetura Domitiano et consulare imperium decernuntur". Fix: `The Senate gave Vespasian the consulship along with his son Titus, and gave Domitian the office of praetor with a consul's authority.`
13. [major] 4:3: Verginius Capito's slave: draft says hanged "where Vitellius' gift of knighthood had once been displayed". Latin: "patibulo adfixus in isdem anulis quos acceptos a Vitellio gestabat" (hung on the gallows wearing the very rings he got from Vitellius). Fix: `hung on a gallows still wearing the knight's gold rings Vitellius had given him.` Also keep the narrator's aside: `the man I've already named as the one who betrayed Tarracina`.
14. [major] 4:3: Vespasian's letter. Latin: "ea prima specie forma; ceterum ut princeps loquebatur, civilia de se, et rei publicae egregia". Draft: "it seemed like a regular imperial decree" is invented and kills the contrast (first look versus speaking as emperor). Also "just plain inspiring" is editorializing. Fix: `At first glance it looked like a general's letter, written as if the war were still going on. But he spoke like an emperor: modest about himself, and excellent about the state.` (The "general's letter" wording fits "tamquam manente bello scriptae"; if the fixer prefers, say `At first glance it looked like a letter from the front.`)
15. [minor] 4:3: "A civil war had been raging for years" adds "for years". "gave Domitian a high-ranking praetor position" is vague. Fix: delete "for years"; and as above for Domitian.
16. [major] 4:4: Meaning reversed. Base: "Their dislike, however, was concealed; their adulation was open enough." Draft: "They might not like each other, but they're hiding it well." Fix: `They hid their dislike of Mucianus, but their flattery was out in the open.`
17. [minor] 4:4: The quoted complaint never closes its quote, and "speaking like an emperor" bends "speak like a public man".
