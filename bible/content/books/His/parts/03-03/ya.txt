3:1 Things were looking up for the Flavianist leaders. They were even getting along. At Petovio, where the 13th legion had its winter quarters, they sat down to plan the campaign. Should they block the passes of the Pannonian Alps and wait for every last soldier to catch up? Or charge straight at the enemy and fight for Italy? The waiters wanted to drag things out, and they had reasons. The German legions were strong and famous for it. Vitellius had just joined them, they said, with the best troops from Britain. The Flavianists' own numbers couldn't match even the legions the Germans had beaten. And beaten men lose some nerve, however loudly they talk. Hold the passes, and Mucianus would show up with the eastern armies. Vespasian also had the sea, his fleets and loyal provinces, where he could stock up for what would amount to a second war. Sounds sensible, doesn't it? Waiting would pay off. Fresh troops would arrive. Nothing they had would be lost.

3:2 Primus Antonius pushed hardest for war. Speed, he said, would help their side and finish Vitellius. Bold talk. Then he laid it out. "The winners aren't confident. They're careless. Nobody keeps those troops under arms or in camps. They lounge in towns all over Italy, and the only people they scare are the families who house them. They've gulped down pleasures they never had before, and they gulp like rough men would. The circus, the theaters and the delights of the capital have drained their strength, or sickness has worn them out. Still, give them time, and even these men will toughen up once war preparations begin. Germany, where their strength comes from, isn't far. Britain is just across a strait. Gaul and Spain are close, and either one can send men, horses and tribute. Add Italy itself and the wealth of the capital. If they decide to strike first, they have two fleets and the Illyrian sea wide open. So what good are our mountain passes? Why drag the war into another summer? Where will we find money and supplies while we wait? Look at what we have instead. The Pannonian legions were cheated, not beaten, and they're rushing to get revenge. The Moesian armies brought us their full strength. Count soldiers instead of legions, and we're stronger. We don't have their wild living, either, and our defeat even did our discipline good. And the cavalry? Not beaten even that day. Luck was against them, but they still broke the Vitellianist lines. Two squadrons, one Moesian and one Pannonian, smashed through the enemy then. Now sixteen squadrons will charge under joined standards. The crash, the noise and the dust cloud will bury and flatten riders and horses who've forgotten how to fight. Unless someone stops me, I'll carry out my plan myself. You, whose luck has never run out, can hold the legions back. Light cohorts will be enough for me. Soon you'll hear that Italy has been thrown open and Vitellius's power is shaken. Then you'll be glad you followed. You'll walk in the footsteps of victory."

3:3 He said all this with blazing eyes and a harsh voice. He wanted as many people as possible to hear, and centurions and some regular soldiers had pushed their way into the meeting. The words poured out like a flood. Even the careful, cautious types were swept along. The crowd called him the one real general in the army. They sneered at everyone else's lazy waiting. He had earned that name at the very first assembly. Vespasian's letters were read out there. Plenty of men used vague words they could twist later. Antonius didn't. He made it plain that he had joined the cause openly. That gave him weight with the soldiers. Whatever came of this, their crime or their glory, he would share it with them.

3:4 Who came next in influence? Cornelius Fuscus, a procurator (an official who ran money and property for the emperor). He had made a habit of attacking Vitellius harshly. If they lost, he had no hope left. Tampius Flavianus was another story. He was cautious, by nature and by age. The soldiers suspected he still felt a family tie to Vitellius. He had run off when the legions started stirring, then come back on his own. People believed he had been waiting for a chance to betray them. They were wrong. Flavianus had left Pannonia, reached Italy and gotten out of danger. Then his taste for upheaval dragged him back. He took up the title of legate again and jumped into the civil war. Suspicion stuck anyway. Fuscus was the one who pushed him to do it. He didn't need Flavianus's talents. He wanted a former consul's name, like a coat of fresh paint on a party that was just rising. The name, not the man.

3:5 Meanwhile, a message went to Aponius Saturninus. Hurry up with the Moesian army, it said, so the crossing into Italy would be safe and useful. The provinces also needed protection from barbarian tribes. So the chiefs of the Iazyges Sarmatae, who ran that nation's government, were brought into the army as comrades. They offered their common people and their cavalry, the one thing they're really strong in. The offer was turned down. Why? Nobody wanted them starting some scheme of their own in the middle of the civil chaos. Nobody wanted them tossing out right and duty for bigger pay from the other side. Too risky. Sido and Italicus, kings of the Suevi, were won over instead. They had been loyal to the Romans for a long time. Their people were faithful to any trust they were given. Raetia was hostile. Its procurator, Portius Septimius, stayed loyal to Vitellius and couldn't be bought. So auxiliary troops were posted on the flank, and Sextilius Felix went to hold the bank of the river Aenus, which runs between Raetia and Noricum. He had Aurius's cavalry wing, eight cohorts and the young men of Noricum. Neither side tried a battle there. All that setup, and no fight. The fate of the two parties would be decided somewhere else.

3:6 Arrius Varus went with Antonius into Italy. Antonius was in a rush, and he took veteran detachments from the cohorts and part of the cavalry. Varus was a tough, energetic soldier. Serving under Corbulo and his successes in Armenia had built that name. But people said he had slandered Corbulo's fine qualities in private talks with Nero. That was a shameful way to win favor. It got him made chief centurion. The success he bought that way soon led to his ruin. Primus and Varus took Aquileia. The people nearby welcomed them with glad hearts, and so did the towns of Opitergium and Altinum. At Altinum they left a guard to face the Ravenna fleet. Nobody knew yet that the fleet had already walked out on Vitellius. Next they won Patavium and Ateste to their side. There they learned that three of Vitellius's cohorts and the Sebosian cavalry wing were camped at Forum Alieni. The enemy had built a bridge over the river there, and reports said they were unprepared. So Primus and Varus decided to hit them at once. They struck at dawn and caught most of the men without weapons. The order was to kill only a few and use fear to push the rest into switching sides. Some surrendered on the spot. Most broke down the bridge and cut off the men chasing them. The chase ended there.

3:7 News of the win spread fast. Two legions, the 7th (Galba's) and the 13th (the Gemina), saw the campaign tilting toward the Flavianists. They hurried to Patavium under the legate Vedius Aquila, eager for the fight, and rested there a few days. Minucius Justus, camp prefect of the 7th legion, had been running things more strictly than a civil war allows. The soldiers were furious. So he was pulled out of their reach and sent to Vespasian. Then came something people had wanted for a long time, and it got a bigger meaning than it deserved. Antonius ordered Galba's statues put back up in every town. They had been knocked down in the troubles of the times. He thought it would look good for the cause. People would believe the party was friendly to Galba's rule. They would also believe it was coming back to life. Cheap, easy and effective.

3:8 Where to make their base? Verona looked best. Wide plains stretched around it, perfect for cavalry fights, and cavalry was their strongest arm. Stealing a rich colony from Vitellius would bring profit and glory both. They took Vicetia just by marching through. Small prize, since the town had only modest strength. But it stung, because Caecina had been born there, and the enemy's commander had just lost his hometown. Verona gave them more, backing the cause with its example and its money. So the army settled between Raetia and the Julian Alps. That shut the route, and the German armies could not come through. Did Vespasian know about any of this? Either he knew nothing, or the army went against his orders. He ordered the war to stop at Aquileia and wait for Mucianus. His reasons sounded good. Vespasian held Egypt, the key to the grain supply, plus the money of the richest provinces. Cut Vitellius's army off from pay and food, and it would starve into surrender. Mucianus pushed the same plan in letter after letter. He called it a victory with no blood and no tears, and things like that. But he was hungry for glory. He wanted the credit for the whole war. Those distances were huge, though. The advice kept arriving late, after everything was settled. Too late.

3:9 Antonius struck first. He hit the enemy's outposts without warning, and it turned into a small fight, more a test of nerve than anything else. Both sides pulled back even. Soon Caecina dug in at a strong camp between Hostilia, a Veronese village, and the marshes of the Tartarus river. Good spot. The river covered his back, and the marsh covered his sides. Had Caecina been loyal, the Vitellianist forces together could have crushed two Flavianist legions, and the Moesian army had not joined those yet. Or they could have driven them out of Italy in shame. Instead he stalled. Delay after delay. He threw away the early chances of the war, sending scolding letters to men he could have driven off by force. And he kept it up until his messengers had nailed down the terms of his treason. Meanwhile Aponius Saturninus arrived with the 7th legion, the one named for Claudius. Its commander was the tribune Vipstanus Messalla. He came from a famous family and was a standout himself. He alone had fought this war with honest methods. Caecina wrote to this force, which was nowhere near as big as the Vitellianists' army. It had only three legions so far. He scolded them for foolishly picking up arms again for a lost cause. He praised the courage of the German army. He mentioned Vitellius briefly and plainly, and he did not insult Vespasian once. Nothing in it could win the enemy over or scare them. So how did the Flavianist leaders answer? They did not defend their earlier loss. They spoke very highly of Vespasian and sounded sure of their cause. They said their army could be counted on. To them, Vitellius was already the enemy. They promised the tribunes and centurions that whatever privileges Vitellius had given them were safe. And they openly pushed Caecina to switch sides. The whole army heard both letters read aloud. Its confidence shot up. Caecina sounded humble, like a man scared of upsetting Vespasian. The Flavianist leaders? They sounded like men laughing at Vitellius.

3:10 Two more legions marched in soon after, the 3rd and the 8th. Dillius Aponianus led the 3rd, and Numisius Lupus led the 8th. Their plan was to show off their strength by walling Verona with a military wall. Bad luck. The stretch facing the enemy had been given to Galba's legion to build. Then allied cavalry appeared in the distance. The men took them for enemies, and panic broke out for no reason. Scared of a betrayal, the soldiers grabbed their weapons. Their rage fell on Tampius Flavianus. Nobody had any proof of a crime. Not a scrap. They had simply hated him for a while, and now they wanted him dead, as fast and wild as a whirlwind. They yelled that he was Vitellius's relative, that he had betrayed Otho, and that he had pocketed their bonus. He got no chance to defend himself. He held out his hands, begging. Mostly he lay flat on the ground, clothes ripped, chest and face heaving with sobs. That only made the mob angrier. His terror looked to them like proof of guilt. Aponius tried to speak, and they shouted him down. The other officers drowned in the roaring. Only Antonius could reach the soldiers. He was skilled, knew how to calm a crowd, and had real authority. Still, things got uglier, and insults turned into weapons and fists. Antonius ordered Flavianus chained. The soldiers saw through the trick. They scattered the guards around the platform and got ready for the worst violence. So Antonius stepped into the way with his sword drawn. He swore he would die by the soldiers' hands or by his own. Any face he knew, or any man wearing a military medal, he called by name and begged for help. Then he turned to the standards and the gods of war. He prayed that they send the madness and the infighting into the enemy's armies instead. Slowly, the mutiny ran out of steam. At sundown each man went back to his own tent. Flavianus left that night. On the road, a letter from Vespasian found him and got him out of danger. Close call.

3:11 Next, the legions turned on Aponius Saturninus, as if a plague had infected them. He led the Moesian army. This time was worse. They were even more savage. Their anger had not flared up after a long day of hard work, the way it did before. It blew up at midday, after some letters went public. People believed Saturninus had written them to Vitellius. The soldiers used to compete over courage and discipline. Now they competed over who could be rudest and wildest. Both armies demanded Aponius's execution, just as loudly as they had demanded Flavianus's. The Moesian legions reminded everyone that they had helped the Pannonians get their revenge. And the Pannonians, as if the other mutiny had let them off the hook, were glad to do it all again. The soldiers marched to the gardens where Saturninus was staying. Primus, Aponianus and Messalla tried everything. But the hiding place saved him more than they did. He had hidden in the furnaces of a bathhouse that happened to be out of use. Soon he sent away his lictors (the attendants who carried the rods of his rank) and left for Patavium. Two ex-consuls, men who had already served a term as consul, were gone. That left Antonius alone with power over both armies. His colleagues gave way, and the soldiers' favor swung his way. Some people believed Antonius had started both mutinies by trickery. Why? So he could have the whole war to himself, they said. Maybe.

3:12 The Vitellianists were just as shaky, and their divisions ran even deeper. Common soldiers were not the problem. Their generals were, because the generals betrayed them. Lucilius Bassus, prefect of the Ravenna fleet, saw his men wobbling. Many came from Dalmatia and Pannonia, provinces held for Vespasian. So he brought them over to the Flavianist side. He chose night for the betrayal. Only the men switching sides met at headquarters. Everyone else stayed in the dark. Bassus himself stayed home, maybe from shame, maybe from fear, and waited to see how it turned out. The captains of the warships charged in, shouting, and attacked the images of Vitellius. A few who fought back were cut down. Most of the men, as usual, loved change and were ready to join Vespasian. Then Bassus stepped forward and openly backed the move. The fleet picked Cornelius Fuscus as its prefect, and he hurried over to join them. Lucilius was put under honorable arrest and carried off on Liburnian ships as far as Adria. There Vivennius Rufinus, prefect of a cavalry squadron stationed there, threw him in prison. Then Hormus spoke up, and the chains came off at once. Hormus was one of the emperor's freedmen, a former slave who had been set free. He counted as one of the generals too. A former slave, a general. Rome could be strange.

3:13 The fleet had defected. Caecina moved fast. He wanted privacy. So he called the top centurions (the company commanders of a legion) and a few soldiers to headquarters, while everyone else was scattered on military duties. There he praised Vespasian's courage and the strength of his side. Then he laid out the damage. The fleet had switched sides. Supplies were short, and Gaul and Spain were against them. Nothing in the capital could be trusted. He made Vitellius sound as bad as he possibly could. The conspirators in the room set the example, and the rest were too stunned by the strangeness of it all to argue. So they swore loyalty to Vespasian. Vitellius's images came down, and messengers raced off to tell Antonius. Done, right? Not even close. The secret was out. The treason got out through the camp, and the soldiers came running back to headquarters. They saw Vespasian's name on the standards and Vitellius's images lying on the ground. First came a gloomy silence. Then the rage burst out all at once. "Has the glory of the German army really fallen this low?" they shouted. "No battle. Not even a wound. And we hold out our hands to be tied and hand over our weapons? What legions are we facing, anyway? Beaten ones, that's what. The men of the 1st and the 14th were the only real strength Otho's army had, and they aren't even here. We beat and flattened even them on these very plains! And for what? So thousands of armed men could be handed over like a herd of slaves for sale, a gift to the exile Antonius. Of course one fleet joining him is worth eight legions! That's what Bassus and Caecina decided. First they robbed the emperor of his palaces, gardens and money. Now they rob him of his soldiers too. And us? We have never seen hard work or bloodshed. We must look cheap even to the Flavianists (Vespasian's side). So what do we say when people ask about our victories and our defeats?"

3:14 Then everybody joined in, each man roaring out his own anger. The 5th legion led the way. They went to put Vitellius's images back up and to put Caecina in chains. They chose two leaders: Fabius Fabullus, the legate (the emperor's officer who commanded a legion) of the 5th, and Cassius Longus, the camp prefect (the officer in charge of the camp). They happened to run into soldiers from three Liburnian ships (fast, light warships). Those men knew nothing about the plot and were innocent of it. Wrong place, wrong time. The furious soldiers massacred them. Then they left the camp and broke down the bridge. They fell back on Hostilia, then on Cremona, to join the 1st Italica and the 21st Rapax legions. Caecina had sent those two ahead himself, with part of the cavalry, to hold Cremona.

3:15 Antonius heard all this and made his call: strike first. Hit the enemy armies now, while their spirits were divided and their forces split. Hit before their leaders got their authority back. Hit before the soldiers got their obedience back, and before the joined legions got their confidence back. Antonius judged that Fabius Valens had left Rome and would speed up once he heard about Caecina's treason. Fabius was loyal to Vitellius and no stranger to war. Antonius also feared an enormous German force coming through Raetia. Vitellius had called up auxiliaries (non-citizen soldiers who served beside the legions) from Britain, Gaul and Spain too. That was a plague of war in the making. Unless Antonius, dreading exactly that, hurried into battle and won first. So he did. He marched his whole army to Bedriacum, two days' march from Verona. The next day he kept the legions there to fortify the position. Then he sent the auxiliary cohorts into Cremona's territory. The excuse was gathering supplies. The real point was a taste of plunder for the soldiers. So whose farms were they raiding? Their own countrymen's. Antonius himself went out with 4,000 cavalry. He went to the eighth milestone (eight Roman miles out) from Bedriacum, so they could raid more freely. As usual, the scouts ranged farther out.

3:16 Late morning, about the fifth hour, a rider came galloping in. The enemy was on the way, he said. A small group was out in front, but you could hear the racket of the whole army from far away. Antonius was still deciding what to do. Arrius Varus did not wait. He was eager to prove he was useful, so he grabbed the boldest of the cavalry and charged out. He drove the Vitellianists (Vitellius's men) back, killing only a few. It looked good for a while. Then more of the enemy rushed up, and the luck of the day flipped. The men who had chased hardest were now the last ones running. Antonius had not hurried any of this on. He had seen it coming. He urged his men to fight with good courage. He pulled his squadrons off to the two flanks. That left an open path down the middle, so Varus and his riders could get back through. The legions got the order to arm. Word went out across the fields: drop the looting and join the battle at the nearest point. Meanwhile Varus, terrified, plunged into his own men and spread his fear to them. The fresh troops were driven back along with the wounded. Their own panic and the narrow roads tangled them up, like a crowd jammed in a doorway. It was a mess.

3:17 In the panic, Antonius did it all, the way a steady general or a brave soldier would. He rode straight at the frightened men and held back the ones giving way. Wherever the fighting was worst, or there was any hope, there he was. His judgment, his hands, his shouts: they marked him out. The enemy knew him, and his own men could see him. Finally his fury boiled over. He ran a fleeing standard-bearer (the soldier who carried a unit's standard, its emblem) through with a spear. Then he grabbed the standard and turned it on the enemy. Shamed, no more than a hundred horsemen made a stand. The place helped them. The road was narrower there. A bridge over a stream had been broken down. And the stream, with its shifting channel and steep banks, slowed the flight. Call it necessity or luck. Either way, it pulled the failing side back together. The Flavianists closed up in tight ranks and hit the Vitellianists, who were scattered and reckless now. Panic spread through them. Antonius pressed the shaken men and cut down anyone in his way. The rest of his men, each according to his own nature, stripped the dead, took prisoners and grabbed weapons and horses. Shouts of victory pulled back the men who had been running across the fields, and they rejoined the winners. Suddenly the winners had their runaways back.

3:18 Where were the legions? Four miles from Cremona stood the standards of two of them, the Italica and the Rapax. They had marched that far while their cavalry's first charge was going well. Then the luck changed. Would they open their ranks to let their own fleeing men in? No. Would they march out and attack an enemy worn out by such a long chase and fight? No again. Luck had beaten them. While they were winning, they had hardly valued their general. Losing, they felt the lack of him badly. The victorious cavalry charged the wobbling line. Then the tribune Vipstanus Messalla came up behind with the Moesian auxiliary troops. They had been rushed up, but long service had made them as good as legionaries. Horse and foot together broke through the line of the legions. Cremona's fortifications were close, and that gave them more hope of escape. It also took the strength out of their resistance. Escape looked near, and the fight drained away.

3:19 Antonius held back. His cavalry and their horses had fought hard, and he kept thinking about how tired and wounded they were, even in victory. Then the evening shadows deepened, and the whole Flavianist army caught up. The men stepped over heaps of corpses and the signs of a fresh slaughter. In their minds the war was already won. They wanted Cremona, now. Either the beaten side gave in, or they would storm the town. That was the reason they gave, and it sounded good. But what was each man really muttering to himself? The colony sat on flat ground, which made it easy to hit. In the dark they'd be just as bold, and free to loot. By daylight the town would be begging for peace. Then all their work and wounds would earn them one thing: the empty pleasure of showing mercy and being praised. Cremona's wealth, meanwhile, would end up in the pockets of the legates and prefects. A town taken by storm paid the soldiers. A town that surrendered paid the generals. Fair, right? The centurions and tribunes, the officers, got shoved aside. Nobody's voice could carry over the noise, because the troops were banging their weapons together. They would smash through all discipline unless someone led them where they wanted to go. Cremona, or chaos.

3:20 Antonius pushed in among the companies. His presence and authority quieted them down. Then he spoke, and he started with this: "Nobody here will lose glory or reward from me," he said. "You've earned both. But soldiers and generals have different jobs. Soldiers bring eagerness for battle. Generals serve the cause with foresight and advice, and more often with waiting than with rushing in. I helped win this victory with my sword and my own sweat, as much as I could. Now I owe you what a general owes: careful judgment and good advice. So look at what's ahead. Darkness. A town we don't know. The enemy behind the walls, and ambushes waiting everywhere. Even if the gates stood wide open, we shouldn't go in blind, because we scout first, and in daylight. Do we really want to storm a town when we can't see where the ground is level? Or how high the walls are? Or whether to attack with catapults and javelins, or with siege works and covered approaches?" Then he turned to the men, one by one. Had they brought axes and spades and everything else you need to storm a town? They admitted they hadn't. Awkward. So he answered, "Can swords and spears break through walls and undermine them? Suppose we must build an embankment and take cover under mantlets and wicker screens. Do we stand there baffled, like a thoughtless mob, gaping at the towers and the enemy's defenses? Wouldn't one night's wait be smarter? Our artillery and machines will arrive, and we'll bring a strength that is sure to win." At the same time, he sent the traders and camp followers off to Bedriacum with the freshest cavalry. Their errand was food and whatever else was needed.

3:21 Mutiny was close, because the soldiers could not stand the waiting. Then some cavalry rode right up to the walls of Cremona and grabbed a few stragglers from the town. Those men talked, and the news was bad: six of Vitellius's legions were coming, along with the whole army quartered at Hostilia. They had marched thirty miles that very day. Word of their comrades' defeat had reached them, so they were getting ready to fight, and they would arrive soon. That alarm finally opened ears that had been shut to the general's advice. Funny what a scare can do. The 13th legion took its place on the raised causeway of the Via Postumia. To its left, out on the open plain, stood the 7th (Galba's). Next came the 7th (Claudius's), covered in front by a field ditch, because that was the lay of the land. On the right, the 8th legion held an open path. Then the 3rd, hemmed in by thick brush. That was the order of the eagles and standards. The soldiers, though, were jumbled together in the dark, wherever chance had dropped them. The Praetorian standard stood beside the 3rd. Auxiliary cohorts held the wings. Cavalry covered the sides and rear. Up front, the Suevian kings Sido and Italicus moved around with picked men of their people. Neat on paper. A mess in the dark.

3:22 It was a blunder. The Vitellianists should have stayed put at Cremona, eaten, and slept. With their strength back, they could have beaten down and overrun the Flavianists the next day. That enemy was worn out by cold and hunger. Instead, with no leader and no plan, they ran into them at about nine at night, the third hour. The Flavianists were already in position and waiting. Ouch. How was the Vitellianist column lined up? I wouldn't dare say for sure. Anger and darkness had scattered it, though others tell us the 4th, the Macedonian, held the right wing. In the center stood the 5th and 15th, joined by smaller units of the 9th, 2nd and 20th legions from Britain. The 16th, 22nd and 1st filled the left, and men of the Rapax and Italica legions were mixed into every company. Cavalry and auxiliaries picked their own spots. All night the fight swung back and forth. Uncertain. Brutal. Deadly to one side, then the other. Courage was no help. Strength was no help. Even good eyesight was no help. The weapons were the same on both sides. Soldiers kept asking for the watchword, over and over, until both armies knew it. The standards got hopelessly mixed, since each group kept dragging off captured ones this way and that. The 7th legion, raised only recently by Galba, took the worst of it. Six chief centurions were killed, and some standards were taken. One man saved the eagle: Atilius Verus, chief centurion of the first company, who killed many of the enemy. He died doing it.

3:23 Antonius's line was wobbling. So he called in the Praetorians. They took over the fight and pushed the enemy back, then got pushed back themselves. The Vitellianists had dragged their artillery up onto the raised causeway of the road. From there, missiles could fly from a clear, open spot. At first the shots scattered and hit the trees, and the Flavianists were fine. Then came trouble. One engine of extraordinary size, belonging to the 15th legion, began knocking down the Flavianist ranks with huge stones. Left alone, it would have spread destruction far and wide. Two soldiers stopped it. Splendid, daring work. They grabbed shields from the heap of bodies, so nobody recognized them. Then they cut the engine's ropes and balancing gear. Both were run through on the spot, which is why their names are lost. But the deed is not in doubt. For a long time, luck went to neither side. It stayed that way until late in the night, when the moon rose. It showed the armies, and it fooled the eye. The moon was kinder to the Flavianists, since it sat behind them. Their men and horses threw bigger shadows, so the enemy's missiles, aimed at what looked like bodies, fell short. The Vitellianists got the opposite. Their faces were lit up, and they stood exposed and unaware. The men throwing at them might as well have been hidden. Easy targets.

3:24 Antonius could finally recognize his men, and they could recognize him. He used the moment to fire them up. Scolding shamed some. Praise and encouragement stirred many. All of them got hope and promises. Picture a general going down the line. To the Pannonian legions he put a question: why had they taken up arms again? "This is the ground," he said. "Here you can wash off the shame of before and win your glory back." Next came the Moesian troops. He called on them as the leaders and starters of the war. "Why bother?" he told each unit as he reached it. "Challenging the Vitellianists with threats and big talk is pointless if you can't face their fists or even their faces." He gave the 3rd legion a longer speech, reminding them of old achievements and recent ones. Under Marcus Antonius, they had beaten the Parthians, and under Corbulo, the Armenians. Lately, the Sarmatae. Then his fury turned on the Praetorians. "You civilians," he said. "If you don't win, what other general will take you in? What other camp? Your standards and weapons are right there. The losers get death. You've already used up every disgrace there is." Ouch. A shout went up on every side. And the men of the 3rd legion saluted the rising sun, as is the custom in Syria.

3:25 A vague rumor spread. Maybe the general slipped it in on purpose. It said Mucianus had arrived and the two armies had greeted each other. The Flavianists charged like men who had just been joined by fresh troops. Across from them, the Vitellianist line was thin. Nobody was in command. Each man was pulled together or apart by his own impulse or fear. Antonius felt them give way. He drove at them with a dense column, and their loose ranks shattered. Wagons and artillery blocked the way, so nobody could re-form. The winners rushed after them and spread out along the road. One killing in that slaughter stood out: a son killed his father. I'll give you the story and the names on the word of Vipstanus Messalla. Julius Mansuetus, a Spaniard, joined the legion Rapax. At home he left a young son. When that boy was grown, Galba drafted him into the 7th legion. By chance, the son met his father. He wounded him and knocked him down. As he searched the half-dead man, he recognized him, and was recognized in turn. He hugged the dying man. Crying, he begged his father's spirit to be kind to him. It should not turn away in horror, as from a father-killer. "This guilt is shared by all," he said. "How small a part of a civil war is one soldier!" Then, at once, he lifted the body and dug a grave. He gave his father the last duty. The men nearby noticed, then more of them. Amazement, complaining and cursing of this savage war ran through the whole army. It changed nothing: they went on killing and stripping their relatives, in-laws and brothers as eagerly as ever. They said a crime had been committed. And they committed it.

3:26 Cremona was a nasty surprise. In the war with Otho, the German soldiers had camped right against the city walls. They had thrown a rampart around the camp and then made it even stronger. The victors stopped cold at the sight, and their generals had no idea what to order. Attack? The army had just worked a whole day and night, and no reserves were close. Risky. March back to Bedriacum? That trip was so long nobody could bear it, and the victory would come to nothing. Build a camp with the enemy this near? Dangerous too. One sudden rush from the town could catch them scattered and busy with the work. Chaos. What worried the generals most was their own soldiers. Those men could take danger better than they could take waiting, and careful plans left them cold. Recklessness gave them hope. And the loot? In their minds, it paid for all the killing, the wounds and the blood.

3:27 Antonius leaned toward the bold option too. He ordered his men to form a ring around the rampart. At first both sides traded arrows and stones from a distance. The Flavianists got the worst of it, since the missiles fell on them from above. Next, Antonius handed each legion a share of the rampart and its gates. Dividing the job would show the brave from the cowardly, he figured, and the rivalry would fire up their pride. The 3rd and the 7th, Galba's legion, took the stretch nearest the Bedriacum road. The 8th and the 7th Claudiana, Claudius's legion, took the right side. And the 13th? Their own rush carried them to the Brixian gate. Some men fetched spades and picks from the nearby fields, and others brought hooks and ladders, which cost a little time. Then shields went up over their heads, and they advanced in a thick tortoise, like a roof of locked shields. Both sides fought the Roman way. The Vitellianists rolled heavy stones down on them. They poked at the loose, shifting tortoise with spears and poles, until the joined shields came apart and drained, mangled bodies lay in heaps. The attack would have stalled right there. So the generals pointed the worn-out soldiers toward Cremona. Those soldiers brushed off every word of encouragement as if it were empty. Cremona was another matter.

3:28 Was sending them at Cremona the plan of Hormus, as Messalla reports? Or is Gaius Plinius, who blames Antonius, the better witness? I can't settle it. I can say this much: neither Antonius nor Hormus fell short of his reputation or his past life, however foul the crime. By now wounds and blood meant nothing to the men. They dug under the rampart and beat on the gates. Leaning on each other's shoulders, they climbed over a second tortoise and grabbed the enemy's weapons and arms. Healthy men, wounded men, half-dead men and dying men tumbled together. Every shape of dying. Every picture of death.

3:29 The 3rd and the 7th fought hardest. Antonius drove straight at that same spot with his handpicked auxiliaries. The Vitellianists couldn't hold out against such stubborn, united attackers. Their missiles bounced off the tortoise, so at last they hurled their artillery engine down on the men climbing up. For a moment it worked. It flattened and buried whoever it hit. Then it fell, and it dragged the battlements and the top of the rampart down with it. That backfired. A tower beside it buckled under the flying stones. While the 7th shoved forward in a wedge, a soldier of the 3rd hacked through the gate with axes and swords. Who got inside first? Every writer agrees: Gaius Volusius, a soldier of the 3rd legion. He scrambled up the rampart and knocked down everyone who tried to stop him. Then he stood in plain sight, yelling and waving that the camp was taken. The rest poured in. The Vitellianists were already panicking, and they threw themselves off the rampart. Corpses covered the ground between the camp and Cremona's walls.

3:30 Then the fight changed shape. Cremona had steep walls, stone towers and gates with iron bars. Armed soldiers stood ready inside, and a big crowd of townsfolk backed the Vitellianists. On top of that, a huge part of Italy had come for the fair held those same days. For the defenders, that crowd was backup. For the attackers, it was a pile of loot waiting to happen. Grim math. So Antonius ordered the loveliest buildings outside the city set on fire. He wanted to see whether losing their property would push the Cremonans to switch sides. He packed the houses by the walls with his bravest soldiers. Those houses stood taller than the walls themselves, and from up there the soldiers drove the defenders back. Beams. Tiles. Torches.

3:31 The legions were forming their tortoise, and others were hurling weapons and stones, when the Vitellianists' nerve began to crack. The officers gave in first. The higher the rank, the faster the surrender. They feared that if Cremona were destroyed too, there would be no mercy. The victors' anger would not land on the penniless crowd. It would land on tribunes and centurions, men whose deaths could bring a profit. And the ordinary soldier? He didn't worry about the future, and being a nobody kept him safer. So he kept holding out. Some wandered the streets and others hid in houses, and even after they stopped fighting, none of them would ask for peace. The leading men of the camp took down Vitellius's name and images. They struck the chains off Caecina, who was still in chains then, and begged him to speak up for their cause. He refused, swollen with pride, so they wore him down with tears. It was the last of their misfortunes: so many brave men begging a traitor for help. Then they held out branches and wool bands from the walls, the signs of people asking for mercy. Antonius ordered the shooting stopped, and they carried out the standards and eagles. A sad line of unarmed men followed, eyes on the ground. The victors gathered around them. At first they hurled insults and made threatening swings. Then they watched the prisoners offer up their faces to be insulted. The beaten men put up with everything, all their fierceness gone. And the victors suddenly remembered that these were the same men who, not long before at Bedriacum, had held back in victory. But then Caecina came forward as consul, pushing the crowd aside. He wore his bordered robe, with his lictors (the attendants who carried the rods of his rank) around him. The victors blazed up. They threw his arrogance and cruelty in his face. They threw his treason at him too, since crimes like these are so hated. Antonius stepped between them. He gave Caecina an escort for protection and sent him off to Vespasian.

3:32 Things were rough for the common people of Cremona. Armed men shoved them around, and they came within a hair of being massacred. Only the generals' pleading calmed the soldiers down. Then Antonius called an assembly. He praised the winners to the skies and spoke kindly of the losers. And Cremona? Not a word, one way or the other. The soldiers already craved plunder, and that greed was built right into them. An old grudge pushed the army the same way. People believed the Cremonans had backed the Vitellianists, Vitellius's side, in Otho's war too. Later, the 13th legion had been left behind to build an amphitheater. The townspeople, as cheeky as city crowds get, roasted the soldiers with insulting jokes. Ouch. The anger grew for more reasons. Caecina had put on a gladiator show there. The place had become the seat of the war once again. The townspeople had sent food to the Vitellianists in the field. And some women who charged off to the battle out of party zeal had been killed. Then the fair made the colony, already rich, look even richer, like gold in a shop window. The other generals went barely noticed. Antonius stood in plain view, thanks to his success and his fame. He hurried to the baths to wash off the blood. When he complained that the water was lukewarm, a slave shot back that it would soon be hot enough. Somebody overheard. That one quip put all the blame on Antonius, as if he had given the signal to burn Cremona. The city was already in flames.

3:33 Then forty thousand armed men burst in, and behind them came a bigger crowd still: camp servants and sutlers, traders who followed the army, even readier for lust and cruelty. Rank protected no one. Neither did age. Rape got mixed in with killing, and killing with rape. Very old men and women past their prime were dragged around for sport. They were worthless as loot. A grown girl, or someone striking to look at? The grabbing tore that person apart. In the end, the plunderers were driven to kill each other. Men hauling off money or heavy gold temple gifts for themselves were cut down by stronger men. Some skipped what lay in plain sight. They hunted for hidden wealth, dug up buried treasure, and used whips and torture on the owners. They carried torches. Once the loot was out, they tossed those torches for fun into the emptied houses and the stripped temples. The army was a mess of languages and temperaments, with Roman citizens, allies and foreigners all mixed in. Every man wanted something different and had his own idea of what was right. Nothing was off limits. Cremona satisfied them for four days. Everything sacred and everyday sank into the flames. Only the temple of Mefitis outside the walls still stood. Maybe where it stood saved it. Maybe the power of the god did.

3:34 Cremona's end came 286 years after it was founded. It had been founded when Tiberius Sempronius and Publius Cornelius were consuls, with Hannibal threatening Italy. The city was built as a shield against the Gauls across the Padus, and against any other army rushing over the Alps. Many colonists, handy rivers, rich soil, and ties and marriages with the neighboring peoples all helped. Cremona grew and thrived, and foreign wars never touched it. Civil wars? A different story. Antonius was ashamed of the outrage, and the hatred aimed at him kept growing. So he ordered that nobody should keep a Cremonan as a prisoner. Too late. All of Italy had agreed not to buy such slaves, so the plunder was already worthless to the soldiers. The captives began to be killed. When word got out, relatives and in-laws quietly paid to buy their own people back. Later, the rest of the people returned to Cremona. The townspeople rebuilt the forums and temples with great generosity, and Vespasian pushed them on. The city stood again.

3:35 Blood and rot soaked the ground, and the stench was awful. The army could not camp long beside the ruins of the buried city. So the soldiers marched on to the third milestone. There they hunted down the frightened Vitellianists who had scattered and sent each man back to his own standards. The beaten legions were split up across Illyricum, so they couldn't turn shaky while the civil war was still going on. Messengers then took the news of the victory to Britain and Spain. Julius Calenus, a tribune, went to Gaul. Alpinius Montanus, a cohort prefect, went to Germany. Montanus was a Treveran, and Calenus was an Aeduan. Both had been Vitellianists themselves, so they were walking proof of the victory. At the same time, garrisons took over the passes of the Alps. Germany was suspected of arming to help Vitellius, and nobody was taking chances.

3:36 Meanwhile Vitellius hid his worries under a pile of luxury. Caecina had left, and a few days later Vitellius shoved Fabius Valens off to war. Then he did nothing. No preparations. No speeches or drills to pump up the troops. Not exactly war-ready. He wouldn't show his face to the public. He hid in the shade of his gardens, like a sleepy animal that lies there as long as someone keeps feeding it. Past, present, future: all of it slipped out of his head. While he lounged, drained, in the grove at Aricia, two blows hit him. Lucilius Bassus betrayed him, and the fleet at Ravenna deserted. Soon came news about Caecina, a mix of joy and pain. He had revolted, and the army had put him in chains. In that sluggish mind, joy beat worry. Back in the city, Vitellius celebrated loudly, and in a packed meeting he piled praise on the soldiers for being loyal. Then he ordered Publilius Sabinus, prefect of the Praetorian Guard, into chains. The reason given? Friendship with Caecina. Alfenus Varus took his place.

3:37 Then Vitellius gave the Senate a speech. It was written to sound grand, and the senators lifted him up with carefully chosen flattery. Lucius Vitellius was first with a savage motion against Caecina. The others put on a show of outrage. A consul had betrayed the state. A general had betrayed his emperor. A man loaded with so much wealth and so many honors had betrayed his friend. They spoke as if complaining for Vitellius. Really, they were airing their own grievances. Not one speech said a word against the Flavianist leaders. Instead, they blamed the armies for mistakes and for not thinking ahead. They tiptoed around Vespasian's name, hesitating and dodging it. Then a man turned up who flattered his way into the one day left of Caecina's consulship. Giver and receiver both got mocked. On October 31, Rosius Regulus took office, and then he resigned. The experts noticed something odd: nobody had ever named a substitute consul without revoking the office or passing a law. Still, Caninius Rebilus had been consul for a single day under Gaius Caesar as dictator. The spoils of the civil war had to be enjoyed in a hurry. Quick work.

3:38 Junius Blaesus's death became famous, and notorious too. Here is the story as it reached me. Vitellius lay badly sick, and from the Servilian gardens he spotted a tower in a house nearby. It blazed with lamps all night, and he asked why. Caecina Tuscus, he was told, was throwing a banquet, and Junius Blaesus was the guest of honor. Then the story grew: the fancy setup, and the guests, loose and wild. Some blamed Tuscus and the others, but Blaesus took the worst of it. The charge? Having a great time while the emperor lay sick in bed. Some men watch the anger of princes like hawks, and they saw their chance. Vitellius was irritated. Blaesus could be taken down. So the job of informer went to Lucius Vitellius. Lucius hated Blaesus out of petty rivalry. Blaesus had a fine reputation, and that put him ahead of Lucius, a man stained with every kind of disgrace. Lucius threw open the emperor's bedroom. He hugged the emperor's son to his chest and dropped to his knees. Vitellius asked why he was so upset. Lucius said it wasn't fear for himself. He had come with prayers and tears for his brother and his brother's children. "Why fear Vespasian?" he said. "So many German legions stand in his way. So many provinces, full of courage and loyalty. And a vast stretch of land and sea keeps him far off. The enemy is right here, in the heart of the city. He brags about his Junii and Antonii ancestors. He claims imperial blood, and he puts on a friendly, splendid show for the soldiers. Everyone's looking his way. And you, Vitellius, don't care about friends or enemies. You're coddling a rival who watches your troubles from his dinner table. His joy came at a bad time, and he has to pay with a night of mourning and death. Then he'll know and feel that Vitellius lives and rules, and has a son if fate takes Vitellius."

3:39 Vitellius was trapped between a crime and his fear. Wait too long to kill Blaesus, and his own downfall might come first. Order the killing in the open, and the hatred would be terrible. Either way, bad. So he chose poison. His glee gave him away when he visited Blaesus. Someone even overheard a savage remark, and I'll give you his exact words. He bragged that he had feasted his eyes on his enemy's death. Now look at Blaesus. He had noble birth and polished manners, and he was stubborn about loyalty. While Vitellius's side was at its strongest, Caecina and the leading men came courting him. They already looked down on Vitellius. Blaesus turned them down again and again. He was upright and calm. He wanted no sudden honors, and certainly not the throne. Still, he couldn't escape people thinking him worthy of it.

3:40 Meanwhile, Fabius Valens crawled along, much slower than a man marching to war should. He dragged a long, soft train of concubines and eunuchs. Then news came fast: Lucilius Bassus had betrayed the Ravenna fleet. Hurry the march, and Valens could have gotten ahead of Caecina, who was still wobbling. He could also have reached the legions before the big fight. Some people had a plan: take your most loyal men, skip Ravenna, and sneak along hidden trails to Hostilia or Cremona. Others wanted something louder: call the Praetorian cohorts from the city and blast through with a strong force. Valens wasted his chance on pointless delay and debate. Then he rejected both plans. In a crisis, the middle path is the worst choice. Naturally, he took it. He wasn't brave enough. He didn't think ahead enough.

3:41 He sent Vitellius a letter begging for help. Three cohorts came, plus a British cavalry regiment. Too many to slip past unseen. Too few to break through. Even in that much danger, Valens couldn't shake his bad name. People believed he went after forbidden pleasures. They said he dishonored his hosts' homes with adultery and seduction. He had power and money. He also had the last wild cravings of a fortune falling apart. When the infantry and cavalry finally arrived, his bad plan was plain to see. A force that small couldn't break through the enemy, even if every man was loyal. Were they loyal? Nobody could be sure. Shame and respect for their general held them in line for now. But that's a weak chain for men who fear danger and don't care about disgrace. Because he feared them, he sent the cohorts on ahead to Ariminum. He ordered the cavalry to guard his rear. Valens himself turned off toward Umbria and then Etruria, with a few companions who had stuck with him through the bad times. There he heard how the battle of Cremona ended. So he came up with a new plan that was not cowardly at all. If it had worked, it would have been terrible. He wanted to seize ships, land anywhere in the province of Narbonensis, and stir up the Gauls, the armies and the peoples of Germany. A brand new war.

3:42 After Valens left, Ariminum's garrison lost its nerve. Cornelius Fuscus marched up with his army and sent Liburnian ships to patrol the nearest shores. Then he boxed the town in, by land and by sea. Umbria's plains fell. So did the stretch of Picenum along the Adriatic. Italy was now cut in two. Vespasian held one side of the Apennine ridges and Vitellius the other. And Valens? He set out from the bay of Pisa, and a dead calm or a wrong wind drove him into the harbor of Hercules Monoecus. Marius Maturus was close by. He was procurator of the Maritime Alps (an official who ran money and property for the emperor). He stayed loyal to Vitellius, and he hadn't dropped his oath, though everyone around him was against Vitellius. Maturus welcomed Valens politely. Then his advice scared Valens out of rashly entering Gallia Narbonensis. Meanwhile, fear was cracking the loyalty of everyone else. Another procurator, Valerius Paullinus, was an energetic officer who had been Vespasian's friend before Vespasian rose to power. And he had made the nearby communities swear loyalty to Vespasian.

3:43 Paullinus had riled up every man Vitellius had thrown out of the army. Those men were fighting the war on their own. Paullinus also held Forum Julii, the key to the sea, with a garrison. It was his hometown, so his word carried weight there. The Praetorians respected him, too, since he had once been a tribune among them. The locals pitched in, out of town pride and hope for his future power. The preparations were solid, and rumor made them sound even bigger. The news hit the wobbling Vitellianists hard. Fabius Valens ran back to his ships with four bodyguards, three friends and as many centurions. Maturus and the others could stay and swear loyalty to Vespasian, if they wanted. Was the sea safer for Valens than the coast or the towns? It was. Still, he couldn't see what was coming. He knew what to run from far better than whom to trust. Bad weather drove him onto the Stoechades, islands that belonged to the people of Massilia. There, Liburnian ships sent by Paullinus caught him.

3:44 With Valens captured, everything swung to the winner. Spain went first. The 1st legion, the Adjutrix, still remembered Otho and hated Vitellius for it, and it dragged the 10th and the 6th along. Gaul didn't hold back. In Britain, people liked Vespasian. He had commanded the 2nd legion there under Claudius, and he had made a name in the war. That goodwill brought the province over, and it also stirred up the other legions there. Many of their centurions and soldiers owed their promotions to Vitellius. Swap an emperor you've already tested for a new one? Not a happy thought.

3:45 Those quarrels and constant rumors of civil war lifted the Britons' spirits. Their leader was Venutius. He was fierce by nature and hated the Roman name, and on top of that he had his own bitter grudge against Queen Cartismandua. She ruled the Brigantes, and her noble birth made her strong. Her power had grown since she captured King Caractacus by trickery. People thought she had helped decorate the victory parade of Claudius Caesar. Then came the money and the luxury that success brings. She dumped her husband Venutius and took his armor-bearer Vellocatus as her husband, giving him a share of the kingdom. The scandal shook her house at once. The people stayed loyal to the husband. The adulterer had nothing behind him but the queen's lust and cruelty. Predictably, Venutius brought in extra troops. At the same time the Brigantes themselves rebelled, and Cartismandua was in extreme danger. She asked the Romans for help. Our infantry and cavalry fought a string of battles, with mixed success, but they pulled the queen out of danger. Venutius got the kingdom. We got the war.

3:46 Germany blew up at the same time, and Rome came close to falling. Careless generals, mutinous legions, attacks from outside, and allies who betrayed us: it all piled on at once. I'll tell that war soon, with its causes and its results, because it ran on longer. The Dacians stirred too. Never trust them. With the army pulled out of Moesia, they had nothing to fear. At first they only watched. Then they saw Italy blazing with war and everything turning on everything else. Was that their moment? It was. They stormed the winter quarters of the cohorts and cavalry units and seized both banks of the Danube. Next they planned to wipe out the legions' camp. Mucianus stopped them by sending the 6th legion. He knew about the victory at Cremona. He did not want a foreign enemy crashing in from two sides at once, Dacians and Germans breaking in from different directions. Once again, the good luck of the Roman people showed up. It brought Mucianus and the forces of the East right there. It also finished what we had settled at Cremona in the meantime. Fonteius Agrippa was pulled out of Asia, where he had governed for a year as proconsul (a governor who ran a province for one year). He was put in charge of Moesia, with extra troops from the Vitellianist army. Scatter that army across the provinces. Tie it down in foreign wars. That was part of the plan, and part of the peace. Convenient.

3:47 The other nations got restless too. Pontus first. A barbarian slave who had once run the royal fleet suddenly put the region under arms. His name was Anicetus. Once he had been very powerful, a freedman (a former slave who had been set free) of Polemon. Then the kingdom became a Roman province. He couldn't stand the change. So he raised the tribes along Pontus in Vitellius's name and bribed the neediest with hopes of plunder. With a force nobody should sneer at, he struck Trapezus out of nowhere. The Greeks had founded that old, famous city on the far edge of the Pontic coast. A cohort stationed there was cut down. It had once belonged to the royal forces and had since been given Roman citizenship. The men carried Roman-style standards and weapons, yet they kept the laziness and looseness of Greeks. Anicetus burned the fleet, too. Then he slipped away over an empty sea. Why was it empty? Mucianus had taken the pick of the Liburnian ships (fast, light warships) and all the soldiers off to Byzantium. Not a good week for Trapezus. Meanwhile the barbarians roamed the water with contempt, in ships they had built overnight. They call these boats camarae. The sides are narrow, the belly is wide, and no bronze or iron holds them together. In rough weather the crews add planks to the sides as the waves rise. Finally the boat is shut in like a house. It rolls along with a prow at each end and oars that can be switched. Landing from either direction? Easy and safe.

3:48 Vespasian took notice. From the legions he picked troops, and for a leader he chose Virdius Geminus, a soldier with a proven record. Geminus fell on the enemy while they were scattered, greedy for plunder. He drove them back onto their ships. Then he fitted out Liburnian ships fast and chased Anicetus to the mouth of the river Cohibus. There Anicetus was safe. The king of the Sedochezi protected him, because Anicetus had won that king over as an ally with money and gifts. At first the king defended his suppliant with threats and weapons. Then someone put the price in front of him: betray Anicetus, or face war. Barbarian loyalty being what it is, shaky, the king agreed. Anicetus would die. The refugees would be handed over. That ended the slave war. Vespasian was thrilled, with everything flowing beyond his prayers. Then word of the battle of Cremona reached him in Egypt. He hurried on to Alexandria all the faster. His plan was to squeeze Vitellius's broken armies with hunger. Rome depends on outside help for its food, and that would hurt it too. He was also getting ready to invade Africa, on the same coast, by land and sea. The aim was to cut off the grain. His enemies would run short, then quarrel. Hunger first. Then the fights.

3:49 Cremona changed Primus Antonius. While the empire changed hands and the whole world shook, he stopped behaving nearly so well. Maybe he figured the war was as good as won and the rest would come easily. Or maybe success exposed his greed, his pride and his other hidden vices. Either way, he swaggered through Italy as if it were a conquered country. He made a fuss over the legions as if they were his own. Every word and every move built his own road to power. How? By filling the soldiers with a taste for doing whatever they pleased. He offered the legions the posts of the centurions who had been killed, to be filled by their vote. They voted for the most unruly men. Soldiers stopped answering to their generals. The generals got dragged along by the soldiers' violence. Antonius soon turned these mutinous practices, which wrecked discipline, into plunder for himself. Mucianus was on his way, and Antonius didn't fear him at all. That was a worse mistake than scorning Vespasian.

3:50 Winter was close. The Padus had soaked the fields. Still, the army marched on, traveling light. At Verona they left the standards and eagles of the victorious legions. They left the men weighed down by wounds or age, and plenty of men who weren't hurt at all. Why? The war looked beaten already. The cohorts, the cavalry units and the men picked from the legions seemed like enough. The 11th legion had joined them. It had hesitated at first. Now that things were going well, it worried about having stayed away. Six thousand Dalmatians, newly drafted, marched with them. Pompeius Silvanus, a former consul, was in command. But the real force behind the plans was the legion's legate (the officer who commanded a legion), Annius Bassus. He steered Silvanus while looking respectful. Silvanus was slow at war and wasted the days of action in talk. Bassus quietly got everything done. The best marines of the Ravenna fleet signed on too. They demanded service in the legions. Dalmatians filled up the fleet. Then the army and its generals halted at Fanum Fortunae, the Temple of Fortune. Nobody was sure of the overall plan. The Praetorian cohorts had marched out of Rome, they had heard, and they thought garrisons held the Apennines. The shortage in a land worn down by war frightened them too. So did the mutinous soldiers, who kept shouting for the clavarium (the word for the bonus). Neither money nor grain had been provided. Haste and greed made it worse. Whatever could be seized was being seized.

3:51 This one I have on the best authority. Among the victors, right and wrong counted for so little that one ordinary cavalry trooper stepped up and said he had killed his brother in the last battle. Then he asked the generals to pay him for it. Yes, pay him. Human law gave them no way to honor that killing. The logic of war gave them no way to punish it. So they stalled. What he had earned, they said, was too big to be paid at once. Nothing more is recorded. Still, the earlier civil wars had a horror like it. At the Janiculum, in the fight against Cinna, a soldier on Pompey's side killed his brother. When he learned what he had done, he killed himself. Sisenna tells it that way. Remorse cut deeper in our ancestors' day, just as glory did. Their shame over crimes was sharper, and so was their pride in virtue. Old examples like these, and others, I will bring up from memory. When the subject and the moment ask for goodness, or for comfort against evil, they belong. More on that later.

3:52 The generals had a plan. Antonius and the party's other generals would send the cavalry ahead first. Its scouts would comb all of Umbria for an easier way up the Apennine ridges. Then the eagles, the standards and every soldier at Verona would be called up. Supply convoys would fill the Padus and the sea. Sounds simple. But some of the generals were stalling, because Antonius was already too much for them and they hoped for surer things from Mucianus. And Mucianus? He was nervous about such a fast win. Unless he took Rome himself, he figured he would get no share of the glory. So he kept writing to Primus and Varus, and his advice zigzagged. Push on, he said. Then wait, since waiting has its uses. He worded it so that he could claim a win or dodge a loss, whatever happened. To Plotius Griphus he sent blunter orders. Vespasian had recently moved Plotius into the senate class and given him a legion. Mucianus wrote to every other man he could count on too. They all wrote back to slam the rush of Primus and Varus. Mucianus loved it. Then he forwarded the letters to Vespasian, so that Antonius's plans and deeds would not get the credit Antonius was hoping for. Mucianus made sure of that.

3:53 Antonius took it badly. Whose fault was it? Mucianus's, Antonius said, since those accusations had made his own dangers look cheap. And Antonius could not keep his mouth shut. He talked too much and never learned to obey. So he wrote Vespasian a letter that was far too cocky for an emperor. It took a sneaky swipe at Mucianus, too. Here is what it said: "I drove the Pannonian legions into arms. I roused the Moesian commanders with my urging. I held steady and broke through the Alps, seized Italy, and cut off the German and Raetian auxiliaries. Vitellius's legions were divided and scattered. I smashed them with a storm of cavalry first, then with infantry strength through a day and a night. That was the finest deed of all, and it was mine. The fall of Cremona? That belongs to the war. Civil strife in the old days cost the state more and brought down many cities. I serve my emperor not with messages and letters but with my own hand and arms. I won't stand in the way of the glory of those who settled Dacia meanwhile. They cared about the peace of Moesia. I cared about Italy's safety and security. Because I urged it, the Gauls and the Spains, the strongest part of the world, turned to Vespasian. But my labors are wasted if only those who weren't there for the danger win the rewards of danger." Mucianus missed none of it. Bitter quarrels grew out of this. Antonius fed them openly, and Mucianus fed them slyly. That made them harder to end.

3:54 Cremona had broken Vitellius's forces. His answer was to hide the news. It was a dumb move, and it put off the fixes, not the troubles. If he had confessed and asked for advice, he would still have had hope and strength. Instead he made up a story that everything was fine, and his lies only made things worse. Nobody around him said a word about the war. Talk was banned across the city. So people who would have told the truth spread even worse stories, because the truth was forbidden. The enemy's generals helped inflate the rumors too. They sent Vitellius's captured spies back, but only after leading them around to see how strong the winning army was. Vitellius questioned every one of them in private and then ordered them killed. One man stood out: a centurion, Julius Agrestis, who showed amazing steadiness. He spent a long time trying to fire Vitellius up with courage, and it got him nowhere. Not a flicker. So he talked Vitellius into sending him to look at the enemy and see what had happened at Cremona. He didn't try to fool Antonius with secret scouting. He told Antonius what the emperor had ordered and what he himself planned to do, and he asked to see everything. Guides showed him the battlefield, the ruins of Cremona and the captured legions. Agrestis went back to Vitellius. Vitellius said none of it was true, and he even claimed Agrestis had been bought off. "Since you need strong proof," Agrestis said, "and my life or death can't do you any good now, I'll give you something you can believe." Then he left. He proved his words by killing himself. Some sources say Vitellius had him killed. Even they tell the same story of his loyalty and steadiness.

3:55 Vitellius woke up, as if from a sleep. He ordered Julius Priscus and Alfenus Varus to hold the Apennines. Fourteen Praetorian cohorts went with them, along with all the cavalry units. A legion pulled from the fleet came too. Picked horses and picked soldiers, so many thousands of armed men that they were strong enough to go on the attack. Another leader would have used them that way. The rest of the cohorts went to his brother Lucius Vitellius, to guard the city. Vitellius himself cut back on none of his luxury. Not one bit. His confidence was draining away, so he rushed the elections, naming consuls years in advance. He handed out treaties to allies and Latin rights to foreigners. Some people got off tribute, and others got a break on duties. In short, he tore the empire apart. No thought for the future. The mob gaped at the size of his gifts, and the dumbest people bought them with money. The wise saw those gifts as empty. Nobody could give or take them without wrecking the state. At last the army at Mevania demanded him. A huge train of senators came along. Ambition pulled in many. Fear pulled in even more. Vitellius gave in and went to the camp, his mind unsure. He was at the mercy of untrustworthy advice.

3:56 The signs came while he was talking to the troops. Strange to say, foul birds flew over him in a cloud so thick it hid the daylight. Then came a dreadful sign. A bull broke away from the altar, knocked over the sacrifice gear and was stabbed far away. It was not killed the way sacrifice animals normally are. But the biggest sign of all was Vitellius himself. He knew nothing about soldiering and had no plan. He kept asking other people things. Which order should the column march in? How should the scouting be done? How far should he push the war, or drag it out? With every message, his face and his walk gave him away. He was shaking, and then he got drunk. Sick of camp life, and hearing that the Misenum fleet had defected, he went back to Rome. He was scared of each new wound and didn't care about the biggest danger. Think about that. He could have crossed the Apennines with his army's strength still in one piece. There he could have attacked, out in the open, an enemy worn down by winter and by running out of supplies. Instead he scattered his forces. Wonderful. His sharpest soldiers were handed over to be butchered or captured. They were the ones who stayed stubborn to the very end. The most experienced centurions disagreed. They would have told him the truth if anyone had asked. His closest friends kept them away. The emperor's ears had been trained so that anything useful sounded harsh. Pleasant, and harmful. That was all he took in.

3:57 One bold man swung the fleet at Misenum. That's civil war for you. Claudius Faventinus was a centurion (a company commander) whom Galba had thrown out in disgrace. He forged letters from Vespasian that offered a reward for treason, and the fleet revolted. Its commander, Claudius Apollinaris, was shaky in his loyalty and half-hearted at treachery. Not a great combination. Then Apinius Tiro, a former praetor who happened to be at Minturnae, offered to lead the rebels. The rebels pushed the towns and colonies to join in. Puteoli was dying to back Vespasian. Capua stayed loyal to Vitellius. So local rivalry got mixed into the civil war, like an old grudge at a family dinner. Vitellius picked Claudius Julianus to calm the soldiers down. Julianus had run the Misenum fleet recently, with an easy hand. He got a city cohort and some gladiators as backup. When the two camps faced each other, Julianus switched to Vespasian's side with hardly a pause. Together they took Tarracina and held it. Its walls and position kept it safe. Not their own skill.

3:58 Vitellius heard the news and acted. He left part of his forces at Narnia with the prefects of the Praetorian Guard. Then he sent his brother Lucius against the war rolling through Campania, with six cohorts and 500 cavalry. Vitellius was sick at heart, but the soldiers' eagerness and the people's shouts for weapons cheered him up. He gave a cowardly mob the false name of an army and legions, though that mob would dare nothing beyond words. His freedmen (former slaves who had been set free) pushed him on. The more famous his friends were, the less faithful they were. So he ordered the tribes called together and swore in everyone who gave a name. So many came that he split the sign-up work between the two consuls. He told the senators to hand over slaves and silver by weight. The Roman Knights (the wealthy class just below the senators) offered their service and money. Even freedmen demanded the same job, without being asked. It began in fear. It turned into real goodwill, and most people pitied Vitellius. Not so much him, though, as the fate of the emperor's seat. And the man himself? He worked his face, his voice and his tears to squeeze out pity. He promised plenty and went overboard, the way frightened people do. He even asked to be called Caesar, a title he had refused before. Why now? Superstition about the name, for one. And when people are scared, wise advice and crowd rumor sound the same. But a plan born of thoughtless impulse starts strong and fades. The senators and Knights drifted away bit by bit. At first they hesitated and slipped off when he wasn't around. Then they walked away with scorn, in front of anyone. Vitellius was ashamed his effort had failed. So he stopped asking for what nobody would give.

3:59 Italy was terrified. Vitellius had seized Mevania, and the war looked newly strong. Then he left in a panic, and that handed the Flavians a clear boost. The Samnites, Peligni and Marsi woke up. They were jealous that Campania had gotten ahead of them. Like people who had just switched sides, they threw themselves into every job of the war. Meanwhile the army was crossing the Apennines in foul winter weather. Nobody was even fighting them, and they could barely drag themselves through the snow. Brutal. And they saw how much danger they would have faced if luck hadn't turned Vitellius back. Luck had helped the Flavian generals as often as good planning had. There they met Petilius Cerialis. He had slipped past Vitellius's guards in country clothes, helped by his knowledge of the land. He was a close relative of Vespasian, with a solid name in war, so the generals took him in. Many say Flavius Sabinus and Domitian could have escaped too. Antonius's messengers got through in various disguises. They showed the pair a place to go and a force to protect them. Sabinus pleaded poor health, too weak for hardship and daring. Domitian had the nerve. The trouble was the guards Vitellius had put on him. They promised to join his flight, but people feared they were plotting against him. And Vitellius himself planned nothing cruel against Domitian. He had his own family ties to think about.

3:60 At Carsulae, the Flavian leaders stopped to rest for a few days. They were waiting for the legions' eagles and standards to catch up. The camp spot was a beauty. It had a wide view, a safe supply line and rich towns at its back. The leaders also hoped to talk with the Vitellians, ten miles away, and to see some of them defect. The soldiers hated that, because they wanted victory, not peace. They wouldn't even wait for their own legions. Those legions, the soldiers figured, shared in the loot and not the danger. Patience wasn't their thing. So Antonius called a meeting. Vitellius still had soldiers, he told them. Give those men time to think and they'd be unsure. Push them to give up hope and they'd turn fierce. "The start of a civil war has to be left to luck," he said. "Victory comes from plans and reason. The Misenum fleet and the loveliest coast of Campania have already broken away from Vitellius. Out of the whole world, he has only the land between Tarracina and Narnia. We won enough glory at Cremona. We also earned too much hate for wrecking it. Don't crave capturing Rome more than saving it. Your rewards will be bigger and your honor far greater. All you have to do is win safety for the Senate and the Roman people without bloodshed." That settled them.

3:61 Soon the legions arrived. The army swelled, and fear and rumor swelled with it. The Vitellian cohorts wobbled. Nobody had to push them. Many raced each other to switch sides. They wanted to hand their companies and squadrons to the winner like a gift, and earn favor for later. Of course they did. Those defectors told the Flavians that Interamna, on the nearby plains, was held by 400 cavalry. Varus went at once with a light force. He killed a few who fought back, but more threw down their weapons and begged for pardon. Some ran back to camp and spread fear everywhere. They blew up the enemy's courage and numbers with rumors, to soften the shame of losing their post. Among the Vitellians, disgrace cost nothing. Deserters got rewards. Loyalty turned upside down. What was left? A contest in treachery. Tribunes and centurions (company commanders) kept deserting. The rank-and-file soldiers dug in, stubborn in their loyalty to Vitellius. Then Priscus and Alfenus left the camp and went back to Vitellius. Their shame at treason freed everyone else of theirs.

3:62 Fabius Valens was dead. He was killed while held in custody at Urbinum. His head was shown to the Vitellian cohorts so they would stop hoping. They had believed Valens reached the German provinces and was raising armies there, old and new. The sight crushed them. The Flavian army went wild with joy and took his death as the end of the war. So who was this man? Valens was born at Anagnia, into a family of Roman Knights (the wealthy class just below the senators). He had loose habits, but real talent. He wanted a name for refinement and chased it through wild living. Under Nero, at the Juvenalia games, he acted in farces. At first it looked as if he had no choice, but later he chose it, with more skill than decency. As legate (the officer who commanded a legion), he first backed Verginius, then smeared him. He killed Fonteius Capito, either after corrupting him or because he couldn't corrupt him. Galba's traitor. Vitellius's loyal man. Other people's treachery made him look good.

3:63 Hope was gone on every side. So the Vitellianist soldiers chose to switch sides, and to keep some dignity while they did it. They marched down under their standards and banners to the plains below Narnia. The Flavianist army was waiting. Its ranks stood thick along the road, armed and alert like an army about to fight. Picture it. The Vitellianists walked right into the middle, and while they stood there surrounded, Primus Antonius spoke to them kindly. Then came the sorting. Some were ordered to stay at Narnia. The rest were ordered to stay at Interamna. A few of the winning legions stayed behind to watch them. Quiet men got no trouble from those legions. Anyone who tried to rebel would have been crushed. Meanwhile Primus and Varus kept the messages flowing to Vitellius, again and again. The offer: safety, money, and a quiet retreat in Campania. All he had to do was lay down his arms and hand himself and his children over to Vespasian. Mucianus wrote letters with the same terms. Vitellius mostly believed them. He talked about how many slaves he would take and which house he would pick on the coast. He had gone so numb that he would have forgotten he was ever emperor. Only the others remembered.

3:64 Next, the leading men of the state went to work on Flavius Sabinus, the prefect of the city. They took him aside in private. They urged him to grab a share of the victory and the fame. Their pitch went like this. "You have your own soldiers in the city cohorts. The watch cohorts won't let you down either. We have slaves of our own. Our side has good luck, and winners find everything easy. So why hand the glory to Antonius and Varus? Vitellius has only a few cohorts left, and gloomy news from every side has rattled them. Crowds change their minds fast. Step up as leader, and those same flattering crowds will cheer for Vespasian instead. Vitellius was never up to the job, even when things went well. Now that he's falling, he's weaker still. Whoever takes the city gets the credit for ending the war. It would be right for you to keep the empire for your brother. And Vespasian should rank everyone else below you." Easy to say.

3:65 Sabinus was old and frail, and these words did not fire him up. But the whispers followed him anyway, and some people said that envy and rivalry were making him hold back his brother's rise. Why would they think so? Flavius Sabinus, the older brother, had outranked Vespasian in influence and money while both were private citizens. People believed he had helped prop up his brother's shaky finances. But only a little, and only by taking Vespasian's house and land as security. On the surface the two got along. Underneath, people feared grudges. There was a kinder reading. The gentle man simply shrank from blood and killing. That was why he kept talking peace with Vitellius, and laying down arms on certain terms. They met often at home, and then, as the story goes, they struck a deal in the temple of Apollo. Two men heard the words: Cluvius Rufus and Silius Italicus. Others watched from a distance and noticed the faces of both men. Vitellius looked downcast and fallen. Sabinus did not look like he was gloating. He looked like a man who felt pity.

3:66 Suppose Vitellius could have talked his own people into giving up, as easily as he gave up himself. Then Vespasian's army would have marched into the city without bloodshed. Easy, right? It was not. The more loyal a man was to Vitellius, the harder he fought peace and terms, and they pointed to the danger and the shame of it. A winner's mood, they said, was nothing to trust. Then they spoke to Vitellius himself. "Vespasian isn't so arrogant that he'd let you live as a private man. Even the losers wouldn't stand for it. So even pity is dangerous. You are old. You've had your fill of good luck and bad. But what name and standing will your son Germanicus have? Right now they promise you money, a household, and a cozy retreat in Campania. Once Vespasian grabs the throne, though, nobody will feel safe. Not him, not his friends, not even his armies, until every rival is gone. Fabius Valens was a prisoner they kept in reserve, in case things went bad. Even so, he was too dangerous for them. Primus and Fuscus will have no power over you, and neither will Mucianus, the very picture of that party. Their only power will be the right to kill you. Caesar did not leave Pompey alive, and Augustus did not leave Antony alive, unless Vespasian has a nobler spirit. Remember, he used to depend on your father, back when your father was Claudius's partner. So at least live up to your father: his censorship, his three consulships, all the honors of your famous house. If nothing else, let despair make you bold. The soldiers still stand firm. The people are still fired up. Nothing worse can happen than what we're rushing into on our own. If we lose, we die. If we surrender, we die. All that matters is our last breath, and whether it comes in mockery and insult or in courage."

3:67 Vitellius would not hear brave advice. Pity and worry swamped his mind. He feared that if he kept fighting stubbornly, the winner would show less mercy to his wife and children. He also had an old, worn-out mother. She had died a few days earlier, at a lucky moment. Her death spared her from watching her house fall to ruin. All she had gotten from her son's reign was grief and a good name. Then, on December 18, Vitellius heard that the legion and cohorts who had surrendered at Narnia had switched sides. He left the Palace. He wore dark mourning clothes, and his household wept around him. His little son rode in a litter, like a funeral procession. The people's voices were flattering, but their timing was awful. The soldiers said nothing, and the silence felt like a threat.

3:68 Hardly anyone could watch this and feel nothing. Only a short time before, he had been lord of the whole human race. Now a Roman emperor was leaving the seat of his fortune, walking out through the people and the city to give up his power. Nobody had ever seen such a thing, or even heard of one. Think about the others. Caesar the dictator was brought down by sudden violence, Caligula by a hidden plot. Night and an unknown countryside hid Nero's escape. Piso and Galba, you could say, fell in battle. Now look at Vitellius. There he stood in his own assembly, among his own soldiers, with even the women looking on. His few words matched the sorrow of the moment. He was stepping down for the sake of peace and the state, he said. All they had to do was remember him and pity his brother, his wife, and his young, innocent children. Then he held out his son and asked the crowd to look after the boy, one person at a time and then everyone together. Choking on tears, he finally unfastened the dagger from his side and offered it to the consul standing beside him, Caecilius Simplex. It stood for handing back the power of life and death over citizens. The consul refused. The assembly roared no. So Vitellius left, as if to put the symbols of power in the temple of Concord and go on to his brother's house. The shouting got louder. People blocked the way to that private house and called him back to the Palatine. The other road was cut off. Only the one to the Sacred Way stayed open. Out of ideas, he went back to the Palatine. The rumor that he was giving up the throne had beaten him there. And Flavius Sabinus had sent written orders to the tribunes of the cohorts, telling them to keep their soldiers under control.

3:69 The rumor spread fast: Vitellius was giving up power. Flavius Sabinus had already written to the tribunes (the officers who commanded the cohorts), telling them to keep their soldiers in line. It felt as if the whole state had dropped into Vespasian's lap. The leading senators crowded into Sabinus's house. So did most of the Roman Knights (the wealthy class just below the senators), all the city soldiers, and the watch. The common people were in a mood, and the German cohorts were making threats. Sabinus had gone too far to turn back. Every man feared for himself, since scattered and weaker, they'd be easy targets if the Vitellianists hunted them down. So they shoved the hesitating Sabinus toward taking up arms. Advice? Everybody had some. Risk? Hardly anyone wanted a share. His armed escort came down by Lake Fundanus, and the most eager Vitellianists ran straight into it. A small fight broke out in the sudden uproar, and the Vitellianists came out ahead. In the alarm, Sabinus took the safest choice he had and seized the citadel of the Capitol. His force was a mixed bag of soldiers, some senators, and some Knights. Who were they all? Hard to say, because after Vespasian won, plenty of people claimed they'd been on his side. Funny how that works. Some women went through the siege with them too. The most notable was Verulana Gratilla. She hadn't come for children or relatives. Only the war. The Vitellianist soldiers ringed the trapped men but guarded them carelessly. So at nightfall Sabinus brought in his own children and his brother's son Domitian. He sent a messenger along a route nobody was watching. The message to the Flavianist generals: the besieged were surrounded and in trouble unless help came. Sabinus kept so quiet that night he could have walked away unharmed, and Vitellius's soldiers were fierce in a fight. Watching and waiting? They didn't much care for the work. Then a sudden winter downpour made it hard to see or hear. A bad night to be a sentry.

3:70 Dawn came, and nobody had struck a blow yet. Sabinus sent Cornelius Martialis, a former chief centurion, to Vitellius. He carried instructions and a complaint: Vitellius was breaking the agreement. The message was blunt. "It was obviously a show. You pretended to give up power so you could fool all those important men. Why else walk from the Rostra (the speakers' platform in the Forum) to your brother's house? That house overlooks the Forum and grabs the eyes of the crowd. Why not the Aventine, your wife's family home? A private man avoiding every sign of power would have gone there. Instead you went back to the Palatine, the very stronghold of power. From there an armed column marched out. The busiest part of the city was strewn with the bodies of innocent people. Even the Capitol was not spared. I'm only a civilian, one senator among many. Vespasian and Vitellius were settling their quarrel with legion battles, captured cities, and surrendered cohorts. Spain, Germany, and Britain were deserting. And Vespasian's brother? He stayed loyal to you until you invited him to talk terms. Peace and harmony help the beaten, but for the winners they only look good. Do you regret the agreement? Then don't draw your sword on me, the man you tricked with treachery. Don't go after Vespasian's son either. He's barely grown. What do you gain by killing one old man and one young man? Go meet the legions and fight there for supreme power. Everything else will follow the battle." Vitellius was flustered, and he answered with a few words in his own defense and blamed the soldiers. Their fiery rage was more than his calm could handle. Then he told Martialis to slip out through a hidden part of the Palace, the emperor's home. Otherwise the soldiers might kill him as the messenger of a hated peace. Vitellius could neither order nor forbid. He was no longer an emperor. Just the reason for the war.

3:71 Martialis had barely made it back to the Capitol when the soldiers arrived, furious. No leader, every man on his own. They marched fast past the Forum and the temples hanging over it. Then they drove their line up the opposite hill, all the way to the first gates of the citadel. Picture the slope. Long ago, porticoes (covered walkways lined with columns) stood on the right side as you climbed. The defenders scrambled onto the roofs and buried the Vitellianists under rocks and roof tiles. The attackers had nothing but swords, and sending for catapults and missiles would take too long. So they threw torches onto a projecting portico and followed the fire. They would have broken through the scorched gates of the Capitol. But Sabinus tore down statues on every side, the glories of their ancestors. He heaped them in the entrance like a wall. The attackers simply tried other ways up. One group went through the grove of the Asylum. Another climbed the Tarpeian rock, where a hundred steps lead up. Both attacks caught the defenders off guard. The closer and fiercer one came through the Asylum. Nobody could stop the climbers as they scaled the joined buildings. Built up high during a long peace, those buildings had tops level with the ground of the Capitol. Then fire hit the roofs. Who threw it? Nobody is sure, the attackers or the besieged. The more common story blames the besieged, who were pushing back the climbers who had gotten ahead. From there the fire slipped to the porticoes beside the temples. Then the eagles holding up the pediment (the triangular gable over the temple front) caught flame. They were old wood, and they fed it. The Capitol burned to the ground with its gates shut. Nobody defended it. Nobody looted it.

3:72 It was the saddest and most shameful blow the Roman republic had taken since the city was founded. No foreign enemy did it. The gods would have been kind to us, if our ways had allowed it. Instead, the seat of Jupiter Best and Greatest fell to the madness of emperors. Our ancestors had founded it with proper auspices (the right omens, taken by watching birds), as a pledge of empire. Porsenna couldn't touch it when the city surrendered, and the Gauls couldn't when it was captured. It had burned once before, in civil war. That was one person's crime. Now it was openly besieged and openly burned. And why? What were the reasons for taking up arms? What did such a disaster cost? Were we fighting for our country? King Tarquinius Priscus had vowed to build it during the Sabine war, and he laid the foundations. He built more in hope of future greatness than to match the still modest means of the Roman people. Later Servius Tullius built it higher, with eager help from the allies. Then Tarquinius Superbus did the same, using enemy spoils from the capture of Suessa Pometia. But finishing it was saved for freedom. After the kings were driven out, Horatius Pulvillus dedicated it in his second consulship. It was magnificent, and the Roman people's huge later wealth decorated it rather than made it greater. After 415 years it burned, in the consulship of Lucius Scipio and Gaius Norbanus. Then it was rebuilt on the same spot, and Sulla, the winner, took over the work but never dedicated it. His luck denied him that one thing. Lutatius Catulus's name stayed on it, through all the great works of the Caesars, right down to Vitellius. And now that temple was burning.

3:73 The disaster scared the people inside the walls far more than the people attacking them. Vitellius's soldiers were nobody's fools. They were crafty, and they stayed steady when things got murky. The other side? Panic. Their commander was slow and lazy, and he looked like someone had stolen his wits. He couldn't use his tongue. He couldn't use his ears. Other people's advice never steered him, and he couldn't carry out a plan of his own. The enemy's shouts yanked him back and forth. He banned what he had just ordered, then ordered what he had just banned. Not exactly leadership. And you know how a lost cause goes: everybody gave orders, and nobody followed them. In the end the men threw down their weapons and hunted for places to run and hide. The Vitellianists (Vitellius's supporters) burst in and turned everything to blood, steel and fire. A few soldiers dared to fight. Cornelius Martialis, Aemilius Pacensis, Casperius Niger and Didius Sceva were the best known of them. All were cut down. Flavius Sabinus had no weapon and wasn't even running, but the attackers closed in on him anyway. They did the same to the consul Quintius Atticus. They singled him out because of his empty title and his own vanity. He had posted edicts among the people praising Vespasian and trashing Vitellius. Everyone else slipped away somehow. Some dressed as slaves. Some were stashed among the luggage by loyal clients. A few learned the Vitellianists' watchword, the password they used to know each other, and then asked for it and answered it themselves. Nerve was their hiding place.

3:74 When the attackers first broke in, Domitian hid with the temple-keeper. A freed slave's cleverness did the rest. Domitian pulled on a linen robe and slipped into a crowd of worshippers, and nobody knew him. He then hid at the house of Cornelius Primus, a client of his father's, near the Velabrum. Once Domitian's father held power, Domitian knocked down the temple-keeper's lodging and built a modest chapel to Jupiter the Preserver in its place. Domitian's close call was carved into the altar in marble. Then Domitian took power himself. He built a huge temple to Jupiter the Guardian and put himself right in the god's lap. Quite the upgrade. Back with Vitellius, Sabinus and Atticus arrived loaded with chains. Vitellius met them without one angry word or look. The men who wanted the right to kill them, and a payment for the job, roared anyway. A shout started nearby, and the dirtiest part of the crowd demanded Sabinus's death, mixing threats with flattery. Vitellius stood before the steps of the Palatine, the emperor's residence on its hill, ready to plead for him. The crowd talked him out of it. Sabinus was stabbed and mangled. They cut off his head. His body was dragged to the Gemonian Stairs, where Rome's hated dead were left.

3:75 That was how a man nobody should have sneered at died. Sabinus had served the state for thirty-five years. He was famous at home and in the field. Honest? Fair? Nobody could question it. The only fault rumor pinned on him was talking too much, and that held through seven years governing Moesia and twelve as prefect of the city, the official in charge of order in Rome itself. Near the end, some called him lazy. Many called him moderate and careful with citizens' blood. Everyone agrees on one thing: before Vespasian became emperor, the family's glory rested on Sabinus. I've heard that Mucianus was pleased by the killing. Plenty of people even said peace gained from it, since it ended the rivalry between the two men. One saw himself as the emperor's brother. The other saw himself as his partner in power. But Vitellius refused the crowd's demand to execute the consul. He had cooled down, and in a way he was paying back a favor. When people asked who had set the Capitol on fire, Atticus had offered himself as the guilty one. True confession or a lie that suited the moment? Either way, it looked like he had taken the blame and the crime onto himself. Convenient. It seemed to clear the Vitellianist side.

3:76 Around then, Lucius Vitellius camped near Feronia and threatened to wipe out Tarracina. Inside the town sat the gladiators and the seamen, locked up. They didn't dare leave the walls or take a risk in the open. As I said earlier, Julianus led the gladiators and Apollinaris led the seamen. Both were wild, lazy men who acted more like gladiators than commanders. Guards? None. The weak spots in the walls? Left weak. Day and night they partied, and their noise filled the pleasant stretches of shore. They sent their soldiers off here and there to serve their luxury, and the war came up only at dinner. Some defense. A few days before, Apinius Tiro had left. He had squeezed gifts and money out of the towns, harshly, and all he added to Vitellius's side was ill will. Not strength.

3:77 Then a slave of Vergilius Capito switched sides and went over to Lucius Vitellius. Give him a force, he promised, and he would hand over the citadel. Nobody was guarding it. Late that night the slave put light cohorts (fast, lightly armed troops) on the highest mountain ridges, right above the enemy. The soldiers charged down from there. They came to slaughter, not to fight. They cut down unarmed men and men grabbing for weapons, and others jolted awake out of sleep. Darkness. Panic. Trumpets blaring and the enemy shouting. Nobody could tell what was happening. A few gladiators fought back and made the enemy pay for it. The rest ran for the ships. The same terror waited there, with country people mixed into the crowd. The Vitellianists butchered the country people too, no questions asked. Six Liburnian ships (fast, light warships) escaped in the first uproar, and Apollinaris, the fleet's prefect, was on one. The rest were captured on the beach or sank. Too many people had piled aboard, and the sea swallowed them. Julianus was dragged to Lucius Vitellius, whipped, and had his throat cut right in front of him. Some people blamed Triaria, Lucius's wife. They said she strapped on a soldier's sword and acted proud and cruel amid the grief and ruin of Tarracina's fall. Lucius himself sent his brother a victory message wrapped in laurel. Should he come straight back, he asked, or keep going and finish off Campania? Here's the thing: it was good for Vespasian's side, and good for the state. The soldiers were fresh from a win, and success had made them fierce on top of their natural stubbornness. If they had rushed on Rome, the fight would have been huge, and the city would not have escaped destruction. Lucius Vitellius was infamous, but he had energy. His strength came from vices, not virtues. Like every worst man's.

3:78 Meanwhile, Vespasian's army did nothing. They left Narnia and lazed around Ocriculum for the holiday days of Saturn, the midwinter festival. Why sit still at such a dangerous moment? The reason given was that they were waiting for Mucianus. Some people blamed Antonius. They said he was stalling on purpose, after secret letters from Vitellius. The price for betrayal? A consulship, a marriageable daughter and a rich dowry. Others said that story was made up to please Mucianus. Still others said the generals all had one plan: show the war to the city instead of bringing it there. The strongest cohorts had already deserted Vitellius. With his support cut away, he looked ready to give up the throne. Then Sabinus wrecked everything: first he rushed in, then he lost his nerve. He grabbed weapons without thinking, and he could not defend the Capitol's citadel against just three cohorts. That citadel was the strongest spot in the city, and not even huge armies could storm it. Ouch. But pinning a mistake like that on one man isn't easy. It belonged to everyone. Mucianus held the victors back with letters that could be read two ways. Antonius showed misplaced respect, or maybe he was trying to shove the blame onto someone else. Either way, the blame landed on him. The other generals figured the war was finished, so each left a mark on its ending. Even Petilius Cerialis was too slow. He had been sent ahead with a thousand cavalry to enter the city by the Via Salaria, on cross-country roads through Sabine land. He did not hurry enough until news of the Capitol's siege woke everyone up at once.

3:79 Antonius was late. He reached Saxa Rubra by the Via Flaminia with much of the night already gone, bringing help that came far too late. The news was bad: Sabinus was dead, the Capitol had burned, and the city was trembling. Everything looked grim. Worse, word spread that ordinary people and even slaves were arming for Vitellius. And Petilius Cerialis had lost a cavalry fight. He charged in carelessly, as if chasing a beaten enemy. The Vitellianists met him with foot soldiers mixed in among their horsemen. Picture the place: buildings, gardens, twisting lanes, not far from the city. The Vitellianists knew those streets well. Cerialis's men did not, and it rattled them. His cavalry was split, too. Some riders in the ranks had only just surrendered at Narnia, and they were waiting to see which way luck would go. The cavalry prefect Julius Flavianus was captured. The rest fled in disgrace. The winners did not chase them past Fidenae. A bad day.

3:80 That win fired up the people. The city crowd grabbed weapons. A few had real military shields, but most seized whatever they could find. All of them demanded the signal for battle. Vitellius thanked them and told them to rush out and defend the city. Then the Senate was called. Envoys were picked to go to the armies and urge agreement and peace, in the name of the state. Their luck varied. The ones who met Petilius Cerialis were in terrible danger, because his soldiers rejected the peace terms. The praetor Arulenus Rusticus was wounded. The outrage was worse because it hit more than the titles of envoy and praetor. The man had his own personal dignity, too. His companions were beaten, and the lictor beside him, the attendant who walked before the praetor, tried to push the crowd back and was killed. Only an escort sent by the general saved them. Without it, Roman citizens, right under their own city walls, would have trampled the sacred right of envoys with deadly violence. Even foreign peoples respect that right. Romans nearly broke it anyway. The envoys who went to Antonius got a better welcome. Why? Not because his soldiers behaved better. Because the general had more authority.

3:81 Then a philosopher showed up. Musonius Rufus was a Roman Knight, a member of the wealthy class just below the senators, and he tried to live by the teachings of the Stoics. He had mixed in with the envoys. Walking among the companies, he began lecturing armed men on the blessings of peace and the difference between peace and war. Picture that. Most of the soldiers thought it was a joke. More of them were simply bored. A few were ready to knock him down and trample him. He quit only when the most sensible soldiers warned him and others threatened him. So much for his badly timed wisdom. The Vestal Virgins, the priestesses who tended Rome's sacred hearth fire, also met the soldiers. They carried a letter from Vitellius to Antonius. Vitellius asked for one day to be taken out of the final struggle. Allow a little delay, he said, and everything might be easier to sort out. The Virgins were sent away with honor. The written answer to Vitellius was not so gentle. The murder of Sabinus and the burning of the Capitol had ended all dealings. No deal.

3:82 Antonius still tried to calm the legions. He called an assembly. His plan was to camp by the Mulvian Bridge and enter the city the next day. His reason for waiting was simple. Soldiers all worked up from fighting would show no respect for the people, the Senate, or even the temples and shrines of the gods. The soldiers saw it differently. To them, any delay was an enemy of victory. Then banners flashed across the hills. An unarmed crowd followed them, yet the whole thing looked like a hostile army. The soldiers advanced in three columns, and one went straight along the Via Flaminia in the same order it was standing in. One went along the bank of the Tiber. The third came up the Via Salaria toward the Colline Gate. A cavalry charge scattered the common people. The Vitellianists met the attackers in three groups of their own. Many battles broke out before the city, and most went well for the Flavianists, whose generals planned better. Only one group ran into real trouble. They had turned toward the left side of the city and the gardens of Sallust, through narrow, slippery streets. The Vitellianists stood on the garden walls and kept the climbers off with stones and javelins. That lasted until late in the day. Then cavalry that had broken in through the Colline Gate surrounded them. The two hostile armies also met on the Campus Martius. The Flavianists had luck, plus a victory they had already won again and again. The Vitellianists had despair and nothing else. Beaten, they still gathered again inside the city.

3:83 Now the crowd. The people stood and watched the fighters, like an audience at a game. They cheered one side, then the other, with shouts and applause. Whenever one side gave way, the crowd wanted the men hiding in shops dragged out and killed. Same for anyone who had run into a house. The reason was loot, and that way the crowd got the bigger share. The soldiers were busy with blood and killing, so the spoils went to the crowd. What a sight. The whole city looked savage and ugly. Battles and wounds here. Bathhouses and taverns there. Pools of blood and heaps of bodies, and right beside them prostitutes and men just as shameless. Every kind of wild living and every crime of the bitterest conquest, all at once. You would think the same city was both raving mad and partying. Armed armies had fought inside the city before. Lucius Sulla won twice, and Cinna once. The cruelty back then was no smaller. This time, though, there was an inhuman lack of concern. The pleasures did not stop, not even for a moment. It was like an extra joy added to their holidays. They cheered and enjoyed it, and they did not care about either side. They were glad at the public disasters.

3:84 The camp was the hardest part. The fiercest Vitellianists held it as their last hope, so the winners leaned on it harder, the veteran cohorts most of all. They hauled up everything ever invented for smashing the strongest cities, all at once: the tortoise (shields locked overhead), catapults, earthworks, torches. And they kept shouting that all their toil and danger in so many battles would end with this one job. "We gave the city back to the Senate and the Roman people," they yelled. "We gave the temples back to the gods. But a soldier's honor lives in his camp. That is his country and his home. Don't take it back right away? Then we spend the night under arms." The Vitellianists were outnumbered, and fate was against them. They still made trouble. They spoiled the victory, dragged out the peace, and soaked homes and altars in blood, grabbing the last comfort a beaten side has. Many took their last breath on the towers and battlements, already half dead. Then the gates were wrenched off. The crowd that remained threw itself at the victors. Every one of them fell with his wounds in front, facing the enemy. Even dying, they cared about an honorable end. When the city fell, Vitellius had himself carried out in a small chair. He went through the back of the Palatine to his wife's house on the Aventine. His plan was simple. Dodge the day by hiding, then slip away to his brother's cohorts at Tarracina. Then he changed his mind. That was his nature, and it's what fear does. A man afraid of everything hates whatever is right in front of him. So back he went to the Palatine, huge and deserted. Even the lowest slaves had slipped away or were avoiding him. Picture it. The silence and the empty rooms terrified him. He tried the closed doors and shuddered in the empty chambers. Worn out from his miserable wandering and from hiding in a shameful spot, he was dragged out by Julius Placidus, a tribune (an officer who commanded a cohort). His hands were tied behind his back, and his torn clothes made him a disgusting sight as they led him along. Many jeered. Nobody wept. The ugliness of his end had killed every ounce of pity. Then a German soldier met them and struck out with a hostile blow. Was he aiming at Vitellius, out of anger or to free him sooner from the mockery? Or at the tribune? Nobody ever settled it. He cut off the tribune's ear and was killed on the spot.

3:85 Sword points kept Vitellius moving, and they forced him to lift his face and take the insults. Sometimes he had to watch his own statues fall. Mostly he had to stare at the Rostra (the speakers' platform in the Forum), or at the spot where Galba was killed. At last they drove him to the Gemoniae, the steps where Rome's hated dead were left, where the body of Flavius Sabinus had lain. Then came one remark with some spirit in it. A tribune was insulting him, and Vitellius answered, "Yet I was your emperor." Credit where it's due. Then the wounds piled up, and he fell. Even dead, he got no mercy. The crowd abused his body with the same wickedness it had once used to favor him alive. Same crowd. Same wickedness.

3:86 Luceria was his hometown. He was finishing his 57th year. His consulship, his priesthoods, his name, his place among the leading men: he did nothing to earn any of it. His father's fame handed him all of it. Men who didn't even know him offered him the throne. Is the army's affection won by good qualities? Rarely. It came to him through his laziness. Still, he had frankness and generosity. Without moderation, those turn to ruin. And he believed gifts hold friendships together, the bigger the better, and steady character does not. He deserved friendship more than he got it. Was it good for the state that Vitellius lost? Certainly. But the men who betrayed Vitellius to Vespasian can't take credit for their treachery. They had already deserted Galba. Evening was coming on, and the Senate couldn't be called, because the magistrates and senators were afraid. Some had slipped out of the city, and others hid in their clients' houses. Then nothing hostile was left to fear. Domitian came forward to the leaders of the party, and they hailed him as Caesar. A large crowd of soldiers, armed as they were, walked him to his father's house.
