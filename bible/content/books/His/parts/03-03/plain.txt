## The Flavianist leaders argue: wait in the Alps or strike
3:1 Things were looking up for the Flavianist leaders, and they were working together better. They met at Petovio, the winter quarters of the 13th legion, to talk over their plan for the campaign. The question was whether to block the passes of the Pannonian Alps until all their forces had gathered behind them, or whether the bolder move was to take on the enemy directly and fight for Italy. Those who wanted to wait and drag the campaign out pointed to the strength and reputation of the German legions. Vitellius, they said, had now joined them with the pick of the army from Britain. Their own numbers weren't even equal to those of the legions the Germans had just beaten, and beaten men lose some of their nerve, however loudly they talk. If the Flavianists held the Alpine passes in the meantime, Mucianus would arrive with the forces of the East. Vespasian also had the sea, his fleets, and provinces loyal to him, where he could build up enough supplies for what amounted to a second war. A delay would work in their favor: it would bring fresh forces, and they would lose nothing they already had.
3:2 Primus Antonius, the most energetic promoter of the war, answered that quick action would help their side and be fatal to Vitellius. "The winners have grown careless, not confident," he said. "They aren't even kept under arms or in camps. In every town in Italy they're sunk in laziness, scary only to the families who put them up, and they've gulped down pleasures they weren't used to, as greedily as rough men would. The circus, the theater and the temptations of the capital have drained their strength, or sickness has worn them out. Still, even these men will get their old vigor back as war preparations begin, if you give them time. Germany, where their strength comes from, isn't far away. Britain is separated only by a strait. Gaul and Spain are close, and from either side they can get troops, horses and tribute. They have Italy itself and the resources of the capital. And if they decide to attack first, they have two fleets and the Illyrian sea open to them. So what good will our mountain passes do us? What's the use of dragging the war into another summer? Where will we find money and supplies in the meantime? Why not take advantage of the fact that the Pannonian legions, who were cheated, not beaten, are hurrying to rise up for revenge, and that the Moesian armies have brought us their strength undiminished? If you count soldiers instead of legions, we're stronger, and we don't have their bad habits. Our defeat actually did our discipline good. As for the cavalry, even on that day they weren't beaten. Luck was against them, but they broke through the Vitellianist lines. Two squadrons, one Moesian and one Pannonian, broke through the enemy then. Now the united standards of sixteen squadrons will bury and flatten these horses and riders, who've forgotten how to fight, under the crash and din and storm of their charge. Unless somebody stops me, I'll carry out the plan I'm proposing myself. You, whose luck has never failed, can hold the legions back. The light cohorts will be enough for me. Before long you'll hear that Italy has been thrown open and Vitellius's power is shaken. You'll be glad to follow and walk in the footsteps of victory."
3:3 With flashing eyes and in the fiercest tone, so that as many as possible could hear (centurions and some of the common soldiers had pushed their way into the discussion), he poured out a flood of words like these. He carried away even the cautious and careful, and the crowd praised him as the one man, the one real general in the army, and sneered at the inaction of the others. He had earned this reputation at the very first assembly. After Vespasian's letters were read out, he hadn't used vague language the way many did, words he could twist either way later to suit himself. It was plain that he had committed himself openly to the cause, and so he carried more weight with the soldiers, since he shared with them whatever it would turn out to be, their crime or their glory.
3:4 Next to Primus in influence came the procurator Cornelius Fuscus. He too had made a habit of attacking Vitellius harshly, so he'd left himself no hope if they lost. Tampius Flavianus, cautious by nature and with age, made the soldiers suspect that he still felt a family tie to Vitellius. He had run away when the stirring began in the legions and then come back on his own, and people believed he had been looking for a chance to betray them. In fact Flavianus had left Pannonia, entered Italy and gotten out of danger when his taste for upheaval pulled him back: he took up the title of legate again and joined the civil war. Cornelius Fuscus had urged him to, not because he needed Flavianus's talents, but so that a consular name would give the party's very first moves a respectable front just as it was rising.
## Guarding the passes: allies and the Aenus line
3:5 Meanwhile, so that the crossing into Italy would be safe and useful, a message was sent to Aponius Saturninus to hurry up with the Moesian army. So that the provinces wouldn't be left defenseless against barbarian tribes, the chiefs of the Iazyges Sarmatae, who ran the government of that people, were enrolled in the army. They also offered their common people and their cavalry, the only thing they're strong in. The offer was turned down, so they wouldn't start something of their own in the middle of the civil strife, or throw away all sense of right and duty for a bigger bribe from the other side. Sido and Italicus, kings of the Suevi, were brought over to the cause. They had long been loyal to the Romans, and their people were faithful to any trust given them. Some auxiliary troops were posted on the flank, because Raetia was hostile; its procurator, Portius Septimius, stayed incorruptibly loyal to Vitellius. So Sextilius Felix, with Aurius's cavalry wing, eight cohorts and the native levy of Noricum, was sent to hold the bank of the river Aenus, which runs between Raetia and Noricum. Neither side tried a battle there, and the fate of the two parties was settled elsewhere.
## Antonius invades Italy
3:6 As Antonius hurried to invade Italy with the veteran soldiers of the cohorts and part of the cavalry, Arrius Varus went with him, an energetic soldier. Serving under Corbulo and his successes in Armenia had earned him that reputation. Still, it was commonly said that in private talks with Nero he had slandered Corbulo's fine qualities. The favor he won so shamefully made him a chief centurion, but the success he'd bought that way soon ended in his ruin. Primus and Varus took Aquileia and were joyfully welcomed in the area around it and in the towns of Opitergium and Altinum. At Altinum a force was left to face the Ravenna fleet, since its desertion from Vitellius wasn't yet known. Next they brought Patavium and Ateste over to their side. There they learned that three of Vitellius's cohorts and the Sebonian cavalry wing had taken a position at Forum Alieni, where they had built a bridge over the river. They decided to take the chance to attack this force while it was unprepared, since that had been reported too. They came on it at dawn and killed many before they could get their weapons. The order was to kill only a few and use fear to push the rest into changing sides. Some did surrender at once, but most broke down the bridge and so cut off the men chasing them.
3:7 When this success became known, two legions, the 7th (Galba's) and the 13th (the Gemina), saw the campaign opening in favor of the Flavianists and hurried to Patavium under the legate Vedius Aquila. They took a few days to rest there. Minucius Justus, camp prefect of the 7th legion, who ruled more strictly than a civil war allows, was taken away from the angry soldiers and sent to Vespasian. Something people had wanted for a long time was taken, flatteringly, to mean more than it was worth: Antonius ordered that the statues of Galba, thrown down during the troubles of the times, be put back up in all the towns. He thought it would do honor to the cause if it looked as though they had been friendly to Galba's rule, and as though his party was gaining strength again.
## Verona and Vicetia taken, against Vespasian's orders
3:8 Next came the question of where to base the war. Verona seemed best, surrounded by open plains good for cavalry fighting, where they were strongest. And taking a colony so rich in resources away from Vitellius seemed worth both the gain and the glory. They took Vicetia just by marching through it. That was a small gain in itself (the town has only modest strength), but it counted for a lot when they remembered that Caecina had been born there and that the enemy's commander had lost his hometown. The people of Verona were worth a good deal: they helped the cause with their example and their wealth. The army thus sat between Raetia and the Julian Alps, and had blocked that route so the German armies couldn't come through it. All this was done either without Vespasian's knowledge or against his orders. He ordered the war to halt at Aquileia and wait for Mucianus, and backed the order with an argument: since he held Egypt, which controls the grain supply, and the revenues of the richest provinces, Vitellius's army could be forced to surrender for lack of pay and food. Mucianus said the same in frequent letters, offering as his pretexts a victory that would cost neither blood nor tears and the like, but really he was greedy for glory and wanted to keep all the credit for the war. But with such vast distances, the advice always arrived after the matter was settled.
## Caecina's camp at Hostilia and the letters
3:9 Then Antonius made a sudden attack and broke into the enemy's outposts. After a light skirmish that tested their courage, the two sides parted on equal terms. Soon afterward Caecina built a strong camp between Hostilia, a village of the Veronese, and the marshes of the river Tartarus. His position was safe, with the river covering his back and a marsh covering his flanks. Had he been loyal, the two Flavianist legions, not yet joined by the Moesian army, could have been crushed by the combined Vitellianist forces, or driven back and forced into a shameful retreat out of Italy. But Caecina wasted time with various delays and gave away the early chances of the campaign, scolding in letters the men he could easily have driven out by force, until his envoys had settled the terms of his treason. Meanwhile Aponius Saturninus arrived with the 7th legion, Claudius's. Its commander was the tribune Vipstanus Messalla, a man from a distinguished family and outstanding himself, the only one who had brought honest methods to that war. To this force, which was nowhere near the size of the Vitellianists' (there were only three legions so far), Caecina sent a letter blaming them for recklessly taking up arms again for a beaten cause. At the same time he praised the courage of the German army. He mentioned Vitellius only briefly and in ordinary terms, with no insult to Vespasian. In short, he said nothing that could either win over or frighten the enemy. The Flavianist leaders made no defense of their earlier defeat. They spoke with high praise of Vespasian, confidence in their cause and assurance about their army, and as enemies of Vitellius. They held out to the tribunes and centurions the hope of keeping the privileges Vitellius had granted them, and openly urged Caecina himself to change sides. These letters, read out before the assembled army, raised its confidence, because Caecina had written humbly, as if afraid of offending Vespasian, while their own leaders had written scornfully, as though insulting Vitellius.
## Mutinies against Flavianus and Saturninus
3:10 When two more legions arrived, the 3rd under Dillius Aponianus and the 8th under Numisius Lupus, they decided to show off their strength and surround Verona with a military rampart. By chance the work on the part of the rampart facing the enemy had fallen to Galba's legion. Some allied cavalry were spotted in the distance and were mistaken for enemies, which set off a groundless panic. Fearing betrayal, the men grabbed their weapons. The soldiers' anger fell on Tampius Flavianus. There was no evidence of any crime, but they had hated him for some time, and now, in a kind of whirlwind, they demanded his death. They shouted that he was Vitellius's relative, the man who betrayed Otho, the man who had pocketed their bonus. There was no room for him to defend himself, though he stretched out his hands as a suppliant. Mostly he lay flat on the ground, his clothes torn, his chest and face shaking with sobs. That only made the angry men angrier, as if his terror proved his guilt. Aponius was shouted down whenever he tried to speak. The other officers they drowned out with roaring and yelling. Only to Antonius would the soldiers listen, because he had eloquence, skill at calming a crowd, and authority. When the mutiny got uglier and the soldiers moved from insults and abuse to weapons and fists, he ordered Flavianus put in chains. The soldiers saw through the trick, scattered the men guarding the platform, and prepared extreme violence. Antonius put himself in the way with his sword drawn, swearing he would die either at the soldiers' hands or by his own. Whenever he saw someone he knew or someone marked out by a military decoration, he called him by name to come help. Then he turned to the standards and the gods of war and prayed that they would send that madness and that infighting into the enemy's armies instead of theirs. At last the mutiny wore itself out, and as the day ended each man drifted off to his own tent. Flavianus left that same night, and a letter from Vespasian met him on the way and got him out of danger.
3:11 The legions, as if infected by a plague, turned on Aponius Saturninus, the legate of the Moesian army, all the more savagely because this time their anger flared up, not when they were tired out from work as before, but at midday, after letters were made public that Saturninus was believed to have written to Vitellius. Once they had competed in courage and discipline; now it was a contest in insolence and unruliness, each side demanding Aponius's execution as violently as they had demanded Flavianus's. The Moesian legions reminded everyone that they had helped the Pannonians get their revenge, and the Pannonians, as though the others' mutiny let them off, took pleasure in doing it again. They went to the gardens where Saturninus was staying. What saved him was not so much the efforts of Primus, Aponianus and Messalla, though they tried everything, as the obscurity of his hiding place: he had hidden in the furnaces of a bathhouse that happened to be out of use. Soon he sent his lictors away and left for Patavium. With the two ex-consuls gone, Antonius alone had power and authority over both armies, his colleagues giving way and the soldiers' favor turned toward him. And there were people who believed Antonius had set both mutinies going by trickery, so that he could have the war to himself.
## The Ravenna fleet goes over to Vespasian
3:12 The Vitellianists were just as restless, split by even deadlier divisions that came not from the common soldiers' suspicions but from their generals' treachery. Lucilius Bassus, prefect of the Ravenna fleet, saw that his men were wavering, since many were natives of Dalmatia and Pannonia, provinces held for Vespasian, and he brought them over to the Flavianist side. Night was chosen for carrying out the treason so that the ringleaders alone could meet at headquarters without the rest knowing. Bassus, out of shame or perhaps fear, stayed at home and waited to see how it turned out. The captains of the triremes rushed shouting at the images of Vitellius. A few who tried to resist were cut down. Most, with the usual love of change, were ready to join Vespasian. Then Bassus came forward and openly approved the move. The fleet appointed Cornelius Fuscus as its prefect, and he hurried to join them. Lucilius was placed under honorable arrest and taken by Liburnian ships as far as Adria, where Vivennius Rufinus, prefect of a cavalry squadron stationed there, threw him in prison. His chains came off at once, though, when Hormus, one of the emperor's freedmen, spoke up, since he too counted among the generals.
## Caecina's treason and his army's revolt
3:13 When the fleet's revolt became known, Caecina called the chief centurions and a few soldiers to headquarters, which he picked as the most out-of-the-way part of the camp, while the rest were scattered on military duties. There he praised Vespasian's courage and the strength of his party. He told them that the fleet had changed sides, that they were short of supplies, that Gaul and Spain were against them, and that nothing in the capital could be relied on, making the worst of everything about Vitellius. Then, with the conspirators present setting the example and the rest paralyzed by how strange it all was, he made them swear allegiance to Vespasian. At the same time Vitellius's images were torn down, and messengers were sent to tell Antonius. But when the treason became known through the camp, and the soldiers hurried back to headquarters and saw Vespasian's name on the standards and Vitellius's images thrown on the ground, there was first a gloomy silence, and then all their rage burst out at once. "Has the glory of the German army fallen so low," they cried, "that without a battle, without even a wound, we hold out our hands to be tied and hand over our weapons? What legions are these facing us anyway? Only beaten ones. The men of the 1st and the 14th, the only real strength of Otho's army, aren't even there, and we beat and flattened even them on these very plains, only so that thousands of armed men could be handed over like a herd of slaves for sale, as a gift to the exile Antonius. And of course one fleet joining him is worth eight legions! That's what Bassus and Caecina decided: after robbing the emperor of his palaces, gardens and money, now they rob him of his soldiers too. But what about us, who've seen nothing of hard work or bloodshed, who must look cheap even to the Flavianists? What will we say to the people who ask us about our victories and our defeats?"
3:14 Everyone joined in, each shouting out his own anger. Led by the 5th legion, they went to put Vitellius's images back up and to put Caecina in chains. They picked Fabius Fabullus, legate of the 5th legion, and Cassius Longus, camp prefect, to lead them. They massacred the soldiers from three Liburnian ships who happened to cross their path, men who knew nothing and were innocent of all this. Then they left the camp, broke down the bridge, and fell back on Hostilia and then Cremona, to join the 1st Italica and 21st Rapax legions, which Caecina had sent ahead with part of the cavalry to hold Cremona.
## The cavalry fight on the Cremona road
3:15 When Antonius learned this, he decided to attack the enemy armies while their spirits were divided and their forces separated, before their leaders regained authority, the soldiers regained obedience, and the joined legions regained confidence. He judged that Fabius Valens had left Rome and would speed up once he heard of Caecina's treason. Fabius was loyal to Vitellius and no stranger to war. At the same time he feared an enormous German force coming through Raetia. Vitellius had also called up auxiliaries from Britain, Gaul and Spain, a huge plague of war, unless Antonius, dreading exactly that, hurried into battle and won first. He came with his whole army to Bedriacum, two days' march from Verona. The next day Antonius kept the legions there to fortify the position, and sent the auxiliary cohorts into Cremona's territory, supposedly to gather supplies, so that the soldiers would get a taste of plunder from their own countrymen. He himself advanced with 4,000 cavalry to the eighth milestone from Bedriacum, to give them more freedom to raid. As usual, the scouts ranged farther out.
3:16 It was about the fifth hour of the day, late morning, when a rider galloped in with the news that the enemy was approaching, that a small body was out in front, but that the stir and noise of the whole force could be heard far and wide. While Antonius was deciding what to do, Arrius Varus, eager to prove useful, charged out with the boldest of the cavalry and drove the Vitellianists back with modest losses. But more of them rushed up, the luck of the day turned, and the men who had chased hardest were now the last ones running. Antonius hadn't hurried this on himself; he had seen it coming. He urged his men to go into the fight with good courage, drew his squadrons off to the two flanks, and left an open path in the middle to take in Varus and his riders. The legions were ordered to arm, and word went out across the fields that every man should drop the looting and join the battle at the nearest point. Meanwhile Varus, terrified, plunged into the crowd of his own men and spread his fear to them. The fresh troops were driven back along with the wounded, tangled up by their own panic and the narrow roads.
3:17 In that panic Antonius left out nothing a steady general or a brave soldier could do. He met the frightened men head on and held back those who were giving way. Wherever the struggle was hardest, or there was any hope, there he was, marked out by his judgment, his hand and his voice, known to the enemy and seen by his own men. At last he was carried to such a pitch of fury that he ran a fleeing standard-bearer through with a spear. Then he grabbed the standard and turned it toward the enemy. Shamed by that, no more than a hundred horsemen made a stand. The place helped them: the road was narrower there, and the bridge over a stream that crossed it had been broken down, and the stream, with its shifting channel and steep banks, held up their flight. That necessity, or luck, restored the party's failing fortunes. Closing up in dense ranks, the Flavianists met the Vitellianists, who were now scattered and reckless, and threw them into a panic. Antonius pressed the shaken men and cut down the ones who stood in his way. Meanwhile the rest, each according to his own nature, stripped the dead, took prisoners, and grabbed weapons and horses. Roused by the shouts of victory, the men who had been scattering across the fields in flight rejoined the winning side.
3:18 Four miles from Cremona stood the standards of two legions, the Italica and the Rapax, which had advanced that far while their cavalry's first charge was going well. But when the luck changed, they wouldn't open their ranks to take in their own fleeing men, and they wouldn't advance and attack an enemy now worn out by such a long chase and fight. Beaten by chance, these men had never valued their general when they were winning as much as they now felt the lack of him in disaster. The victorious cavalry charged the wavering line, and the tribune Vipstanus Messalla followed with the Moesian auxiliary troops, who, though rushed up, were as good soldiers as the legionaries thanks to long service. Horse and foot together broke through the line of the legions. The nearness of Cremona's fortifications gave more hope of escape, but it took the strength out of their resistance.
## Antonius holds the army back from Cremona
3:19 Antonius didn't press on. He was thinking of how tired and wounded his cavalry and their horses must be after such a hard-fought battle, even though they'd won. As the evening shadows deepened, the whole strength of the Flavianist army came up. They advanced over heaps of dead and the marks of fresh slaughter and, as if the war were over, demanded to march on Cremona and either accept the surrender of the beaten side or take the town by storm. That was the reason they gave, and it sounded good, but what each man said to himself was this: "The colony sits on level ground and can be taken by assault. If we attack in the dark, we'll be just as bold and have more freedom to plunder. If we wait for daylight, they'll beg for peace, and for all our work and wounds we'll get only the empty satisfaction of showing mercy and being praised, while Cremona's wealth goes into the pockets of the legates and prefects. Soldiers get the plunder of a city taken by storm; generals get the plunder of one that surrenders." The centurions and tribunes were pushed aside. So that no one's voice could be heard, the troops clashed their weapons together, ready to break through all discipline unless they were led where they wanted to go.
3:20 Antonius moved into the companies. When his presence and personal authority had brought quiet, he said, "I wouldn't take their glory or their reward away from men who have earned them so well. But there's a division of duties between an army and its generals. Eagerness for battle suits soldiers, but generals serve the cause by foresight, by advice, and by delay more often than by rashness. I helped your victory as much as I could with my sword and my own efforts. Now I must help you with careful judgment and advice, the qualities that belong to a general. What you'll face is plain enough: the darkness, the unfamiliar layout of the town, the enemy inside the walls, and every chance for ambushes. Even with the gates wide open, we shouldn't enter without scouting the place first, and in daylight. Are we going to start storming the town with no way to see where the ground is level, how high the walls are, or whether we'd attack with catapults and javelins or with siege works and covered approaches?" Then he turned to individual soldiers and asked whether they had brought axes and spades and everything else used in storming towns. When they admitted they hadn't, he answered, "Can any hands break through and undermine walls with swords and spears? And if it turns out we have to build an embankment and shelter under mantlets and wicker screens, will we stand there baffled like a thoughtless mob, staring at the height of the towers and the enemy's defenses? Wouldn't it be better to wait one night until our artillery and machines arrive, and bring a strength that is sure to win?" At the same time he sent the traders and camp followers off to Bedriacum with the freshest cavalry to fetch food and whatever else was needed.
## The night battle outside Cremona
3:21 The soldiers were impatient, and a mutiny had almost broken out, when some cavalry who had advanced to the very walls of Cremona seized some stragglers from the town. From their information it was learned that six of Vitellius's legions and the whole army quartered at Hostilia had marched thirty miles that very day, and having heard of their comrades' defeat, were getting ready for battle and would soon arrive. That alarm opened ears that had been closed to the general's advice. The 13th legion was ordered to take its position on the raised causeway of the Via Postumia. Joined to it on the left, the 7th (Galba's) stood in the open plain, and next came the 7th (Claudius's), protected in front by a field ditch, since that was the lay of the land. On the right was the 8th legion, along an open path, and then the 3rd, hemmed in by thick brush. That was the order of the eagles and standards. The soldiers were mixed together in the darkness wherever chance had put them. The Praetorian standard was next to the men of the 3rd, the auxiliary cohorts were on the wings, and the cavalry covered the sides and rear. Sido and Italicus, the Suevian kings, were maneuvering in the front line with picked men of their people.
3:22 The Vitellianist army should have rested at Cremona and, with its strength restored by food and sleep, beaten down and overrun the next day an enemy worn out by cold and hunger. Instead, with no leader and no plan, it ran into the Flavianists at about the third hour of the night, around nine o'clock, when they were already ready and in position. I wouldn't dare say for sure how the Vitellianist column was lined up, scattered as it was by anger and darkness, though others have passed down that the 4th, the Macedonian, held the right wing, the 5th and 15th with detachments of the 9th, 2nd and 20th legions from Britain held the center, and the 16th, 22nd and 1st filled the left wing. Men of the Rapax and Italica legions were mixed into every company. The cavalry and auxiliaries chose their own places. The fight went on all night, shifting, uncertain and brutal, deadly to one side and then the other. Courage, strength, even good eyesight did no good. Both lines had the same weapons. The watchword, asked over and over, became known to both armies. The standards were all mixed up, as each group dragged off ones captured from the enemy this way or that. The 7th legion, recently raised by Galba, was pressed hardest. Six chief centurions were killed and some standards were taken. The eagle itself was saved by Atilius Verus, chief centurion of the first company, who killed many of the enemy and died at the last.
3:23 Antonius held up the wavering line by calling in the Praetorians. They took over the fight, drove the enemy back, and were then driven back themselves. The Vitellianists had moved their artillery up onto the raised causeway of the road so their missiles could be shot from a clear, open spot. At first the shots scattered and struck the trees without harming the enemy. An engine of extraordinary size belonging to the 15th legion was knocking down the Flavianist ranks with huge stones, and would have spread destruction far and wide if two soldiers hadn't dared a splendid deed. They snatched shields from the heap of bodies, and unrecognized, cut the ropes and the balancing gear of the engine. They were run through at once, and so their names are lost, but the deed itself is not in doubt. Fortune favored neither side until late in the night, when the moon rose and showed the armies, but also fooled the eye. It was kinder to the Flavianists, because it was behind them. Their men and horses threw bigger shadows, and the enemy's missiles, aimed at what looked like bodies, fell short. The Vitellianists, lit up in the face, stood exposed and unaware to men who might as well have been hidden as they threw.
3:24 As soon as Antonius could recognize his men and be recognized by them, he worked to fire them up: shaming some with reproaches, stirring many with praise and encouragement, and all with hope and promises. He asked the Pannonian legions why they had taken up arms again. "Here are the fields," he said, "where you can wipe out the stain of your earlier disgrace and win back your glory." Then he turned to the Moesian troops and called on them as the leaders and starters of the war. "It was pointless," he said, "to challenge the Vitellianists with threats and words, if you can't stand up to their hands or even their looks." He said this to each unit as he came to it. He spoke to the 3rd legion at more length, reminding them of old achievements and recent ones: how under Marcus Antonius they had beaten the Parthians, under Corbulo the Armenians, and recently the Sarmatae. Then, furious, he turned on the Praetorians. "You civilians," he said, "if you don't win, what other general, what other camp will take you in? There are your standards and your weapons, and for the losers there is death. You've already used up every disgrace there is." A shout went up on every side, and the men of the 3rd legion saluted the rising sun, as is the custom in Syria.
3:25 A vague rumor spread, or perhaps one slipped in by the general on purpose, that Mucianus had arrived and the two armies had greeted each other. The Flavianists charged as if strengthened by fresh reinforcements. The Vitellianist line was thinner by now, drawn together or pulled apart by each man's own impulse or fear, since nobody was commanding them. When Antonius sensed they were giving way, he pressed them with a dense column. Their loosened ranks broke apart and couldn't be re-formed, blocked by wagons and artillery. The victors, hurrying to pursue, scattered along the road. The slaughter was all the more remarkable because a son killed his father. I'll give the story and the names on the authority of Vipstanus Messalla. Julius Mansuetus, from Spain, joined the legion Rapax and had left a young son at home. When the boy was grown, Galba drafted him into the 7th legion. By chance he met his father and knocked him down with a wound, and as he searched the half-dead man, he recognized him and was recognized in turn. Embracing the dying man, he begged in a tearful voice that his father's spirit be kind to him, and not turn from him in horror as from a father-killer. "This guilt is shared by all," he said. "How small a part of a civil war is one soldier!" At the same time he lifted the body, dug a grave, and did for his father the last duty. The men nearby noticed, then more of them, and so wonder, complaint and cursing of this savage war ran through the whole army. Still, they killed and stripped their relatives, in-laws and brothers as eagerly as ever. They said a crime had been committed, and they committed it.
## The assault on the Vitellianist camp
3:26 When they reached Cremona, they faced a new and enormous task. In the war with Otho, the German soldiers had built their camp against the walls of Cremona, thrown a rampart around the camp, and then strengthened those works further. The sight stopped the victors, and the generals weren't sure what to order. Starting an assault with an army worn out by a day and a night of work was hard, and with no reserve nearby, risky. Going back to Bedriacum meant a march so long the effort would be unbearable, and the victory would come to nothing. Fortifying a camp with the enemy so close was dangerous too, since a sudden sally could throw them into confusion while they were scattered and busy with the work. Above all they feared their own soldiers, who could stand danger better than delay. Cautious measures didn't please those soldiers; recklessness gave them hope; and their greed for plunder made up for all the killing, wounds and blood.
3:27 Antonius leaned that way himself, and he ordered a ring of men to be put around the rampart. At first they fought from a distance with arrows and stones, and the Flavianists lost more, since the missiles were aimed down at them from above. Then he gave each legion a share of the rampart and the gates, so that dividing the work would show the brave from the cowardly, and the competition itself would stir their pride. The 3rd and the 7th (Galba's) took the stretch nearest the Bedriacum road, and the 8th and the 7th (Claudius's) took the right-hand part of the rampart. The 13th were carried by their own rush to the Brixian gate. There was a short delay while some men brought spades and picks from the nearby fields, and others hooks and ladders. Then, raising their shields over their heads, they moved up in a dense tortoise formation. Both sides used Roman methods: the Vitellianists rolled down heavy stones and probed the loosened, shifting tortoise with spears and poles, until the joined shields came apart and they had laid out the drained and mangled bodies in great numbers. The assault would have stalled if the generals hadn't pointed to Cremona for the worn-out soldiers, who were turning away from their encouragement as if it were empty.
3:28 Whether that was Hormus's idea, as Messalla reports, or whether Gaius Plinius, who blames Antonius, is the better source, I can't easily decide. I can say only that neither Antonius nor Hormus fell short of his own reputation and past life, however foul the crime. Wounds and blood no longer stopped the men from undermining the rampart and battering the gates. Leaning on each other's shoulders and climbing over a second tortoise formation, they grabbed the enemy's weapons and arms. The unhurt and the wounded, the half-dead and the dying, tumbled together in every shape of dying and every picture of death.
3:29 The fiercest fighting was between the 3rd and 7th legions, and Antonius himself bore down on the same spot with picked auxiliaries. The Vitellianists couldn't hold out against this stubborn, united attack, and their missiles slid off the tortoise, so at last they hurled the artillery engine itself down on the men coming up. For the moment it crushed and buried the men it landed on, but in its fall it also dragged down the battlements and the top of the rampart. At the same time an adjoining tower gave way under the volleys of stones, and while the men of the 7th pushed forward in a wedge, a soldier of the 3rd broke through the gate with axes and swords. All the writers agree that Gaius Volusius, a soldier of the 3rd legion, was the first in. He climbed onto the rampart, knocked down those who stood against him, and, standing out in plain sight, shouted and waved that the camp was taken. The rest broke through, while the Vitellianists, already panicking, threw themselves down from the rampart. All the space between the camp and the walls of Cremona was filled with the dead.
## Cremona surrenders
3:30 Then the work took on a new shape. The town had steep walls, stone towers, iron-barred gates, a garrison brandishing weapons, and a crowd of Cremonans that was large and bound to the Vitellianist cause. A great part of Italy had also gathered there for the fair held on those same days. For the defenders the crowd meant help, and for the attackers it meant more plunder to look forward to. Antonius ordered fire to be set to the loveliest buildings outside the city, to see whether the loss of their property would drag the Cremonans into changing sides. He filled the houses near the walls, which rose higher than the walls themselves, with the bravest of his soldiers, and they dislodged the defenders with beams, tiles and torches.
3:31 The legions were already massing into a tortoise formation, and others were hurling weapons and stones, when the Vitellianists' spirits began to droop. The higher a man's rank, the sooner he gave in to fortune, afraid that if Cremona were destroyed too there would be no mercy, and that all the victor's anger would fall not on the penniless crowd but on tribunes and centurions, whose deaths could bring a profit. The rank-and-file soldier, careless of the future and safer for being nobody, kept holding out. Wandering the streets or hiding in houses, they wouldn't ask for peace even when they had stopped fighting. The leading men of the camp removed Vitellius's name and images. They struck the chains off Caecina (for he was still in chains then) and begged him to stand up for their cause. When he refused, swollen with pride, they wore him down with tears. It was the last of their misfortunes, that so many brave men were calling for the help of a traitor. Then they held out suppliants' branches and wool bands from the walls. When Antonius ordered the shooting to stop, they carried out the standards and eagles. A sad column of unarmed men followed, eyes on the ground. The victors stood around them and at first hurled insults and made threatening strikes. Then, seeing the prisoners offer their faces to be insulted and put up with everything as beaten men, with all their fierceness gone, the victors suddenly remembered that these were the same men who, not long before at Bedriacum, had held back in victory. But when Caecina came forward as consul, conspicuous in his bordered robe and his lictors, pushing the crowd aside, the victors blazed up. They threw at him his arrogance and cruelty, and (so hated are such crimes) even his treason. Antonius stood in their way, gave him an escort to protect him, and sent him off to Vespasian.
## The sack of Cremona
3:32 Meanwhile the common people of Cremona were being roughly handled among the armed men, and were close to being massacred when the generals' pleading calmed the soldiers. Antonius called the soldiers to an assembly and spoke grandly of the victors and kindly of the beaten, but said nothing either way about Cremona. Besides the soldiers' built-in greed for plunder, an old hatred pushed the army toward destroying the Cremonans. They were believed to have helped the Vitellianist side in Otho's war too. Later, when the 13th legion was left behind to build an amphitheater, the townspeople, with the cheeky character of a city crowd, had mocked them with insulting jokes. The feeling got worse because Caecina had put on a gladiator show there, because the place was once again the seat of the war, because the townspeople had supplied food to the Vitellianists in the field, and because some women who had gone to the battle out of party zeal had been killed. The time of the fair also made the colony, rich anyway, look even richer. The other generals went unnoticed, but Antonius, by his success and reputation, was exposed to every eye. He hurried to the baths to wash off the blood. A remark was overheard: when he complained that the water was lukewarm, a slave answered that it would soon be hot enough. That slave's quip turned all the blame on him, as if he had given the signal to burn Cremona, which was already in flames.
3:33 Forty thousand armed men burst in, and an even larger crowd of camp servants and sutlers, even more given over to lust and cruelty. Neither rank nor age protected anyone, and rapes were mixed in with killings, and killings with rapes. Very old men and women past their prime, worthless as plunder, were dragged around for sport. If a grown girl or someone of striking looks fell into their hands, the violent grabbing tore the person apart, and in the end it drove the plunderers themselves to kill each other. Men who were carrying off money or heavy gold temple gifts for themselves were cut down by stronger men. Some, passing over what lay in plain sight, searched for hidden wealth and dug up buried treasure, using whips and torture on the owners. They had torches in their hands, and once they had carried the loot out, they flung them for fun into the emptied houses and stripped temples. In an army of mixed tongues and temperaments, with Roman citizens, allies and foreigners in it, every man had different desires and his own idea of right, and nothing was forbidden. Cremona satisfied them for four days. While everything sacred and ordinary was sinking into the flames, only the temple of Mefitis outside the walls remained standing, protected by where it stood or by the power of the god.
3:34 That was the end of Cremona, in the 286th year since its founding. It had been founded in the consulship of Tiberius Sempronius and Publius Cornelius, when Hannibal was threatening Italy, as a bulwark against the Gauls living beyond the Padus and against any other force that might come rushing through the Alps. So through the number of its colonists, the convenience of its rivers, the richness of its soil, and its ties and intermarriages with neighboring peoples, it grew and flourished, untouched by foreign wars and unlucky in civil ones. Ashamed of the outrage, and with hatred of him growing, Antonius issued an order that no one should hold a Cremonan as a captive. The plunder had already been made worthless to the soldiers by the agreement of all Italy, which refused to buy such slaves. So they began to kill them, and when that became known, relatives and in-laws quietly bought their people back. Later the rest of the people returned to Cremona. The forums and temples were rebuilt through the generosity of the townspeople, and Vespasian urged them on.
## After the victory
3:35 The ground, soaked with blood and bad air, wouldn't let the army camp long by the ruins of the buried city. They moved on to the third milestone and rounded up the scattered, frightened Vitellianists, putting each man back under his own standards. The beaten legions were spread out across Illyricum, so they wouldn't play a doubtful part while the civil war was still going. Messengers with the news and the report of the victory were then sent to Britain and Spain. Julius Calenus, a tribune, was sent to Gaul, and Alpinius Montanus, a cohort prefect, to Germany. Montanus was a Treveran and Calenus an Aeduan, and both had been Vitellianists themselves, so they were sent as living proof of the victory. At the same time the passes of the Alps were taken over with garrisons, since Germany was suspected of arming to help Vitellius.
## Vitellius in Rome: Caecina's treason and the Senate
3:36 Meanwhile Vitellius, after Caecina had left and a few days after pushing Fabius Valens off to the war, covered up his worries with luxury. He made no preparations for war, didn't strengthen the soldiers with speeches and drills, and didn't show himself to the public. Hidden in the shade of his gardens, like those sluggish animals that lie torpid as long as you keep feeding them, he had let past, present and future all slip from his mind alike. While he lay idle and drained in the grove at Aricia, he was shaken by the treachery of Lucilius Bassus and the desertion of the Ravenna fleet. Not long after, news came about Caecina, a mix of joy and pain: he had revolted, and the army had put him in chains. In that sluggish mind, joy counted for more than worry. With great rejoicing he came back to the city, and in a crowded assembly he heaped praise on the soldiers' loyalty. He ordered Publilius Sabinus, prefect of the Praetorian Guard, put in chains because of his friendship with Caecina, and put Alfenus Varus in his place.
3:37 Then Vitellius gave the Senate a speech composed for grandeur, and the senators lifted him up with studied flattery. The first savage motion against Caecina came from Lucius Vitellius. The others put on indignation that a consul had betrayed the state, a general his emperor, a man heaped with so much wealth and so many honors his friend, and they spoke as if complaining on Vitellius's behalf while really airing their own grievances. No one's speech said anything against the Flavianist leaders. They blamed the armies for error and lack of foresight, and went around Vespasian's name, hesitating and dodging it. And a man turned up who, to great mockery of both giver and receiver, flattered his way into the one day of the consulship left over from Caecina's term. On October 31 Rosius Regulus took office and resigned it. The experts noted that never before had a substitute been named without the office being revoked or a law passed, though Caninius Rebilus had been consul for a single day under Gaius Caesar as dictator, when the spoils of the civil war had to be enjoyed in a hurry.
## The murder of Junius Blaesus
3:38 In those days the death of Junius Blaesus became well known and notorious. This is the account that has come down to me. Vitellius, ill with a serious sickness, saw from the Servilian gardens a tower in a neighboring house lit up all night with many lamps. When he asked why, he was told that Caecina Tuscus was giving a banquet for many guests, with Junius Blaesus the most honored among them. The rest was exaggerated: the lavish preparations and the guests' loose, wild spirits. Some accused Tuscus and the others, but Blaesus most bitterly, of spending happy days while the emperor was sick. When it became clear enough, to the men who watch keenly for the angers of princes, that Vitellius was irritated and that Blaesus could be brought down, the role of informer was handed to Lucius Vitellius. He was hostile to Blaesus out of petty rivalry, because Blaesus's fine reputation put him ahead of Lucius, who was stained with every disgrace. He opened the emperor's bedroom, hugged the emperor's son to his chest, and fell at his knees. When Vitellius asked why he was so upset, Lucius said he had not come out of any fear of his own or anxiety for himself, but with prayers and tears for his brother and his brother's children. "It's pointless to fear Vespasian," he said, "when so many German legions and so many provinces, with their courage and loyalty, and finally such a vast stretch of land and sea, keep him away. The enemy to guard against is here in the city, at our very heart. He boasts of Junii and Antonii among his ancestors, and, claiming imperial blood, he shows off his friendliness and splendor to the soldiers. Everyone's thoughts are turned his way, while you, Vitellius, careless of friends and enemies, are coddling a rival who watches the emperor's troubles from his dinner table. For his ill-timed joy he must be paid back with a night of mourning and death, so that he knows and feels that Vitellius lives and rules, and has a son if anything should happen to him by fate."
3:39 Vitellius was torn between crime and fear. He was afraid that putting off Blaesus's death would bring his own downfall sooner, and that ordering it openly would bring terrible hatred, so he decided to attack with poison. He gave proof of the crime by the obvious joy with which he visited Blaesus. A savage remark of Vitellius's was even overheard, in which (I'll give the very words) he bragged that he had fed his eyes on the sight of his enemy's death. Besides his noble birth and refined manners, Blaesus was stubborn in his loyalty. At the height of the party's strength, when Caecina and the leading men, who were already looking down on Vitellius, courted him, he kept refusing. Upright, calm, wanting no sudden honor, and certainly not the throne, he still hadn't escaped being thought worthy of it.
## Fabius Valens hesitates and flees
3:40 Meanwhile Fabius Valens, marching more slowly than a man going to war should, with a long and soft train of concubines and eunuchs, got swift word that Lucilius Bassus had betrayed the Ravenna fleet. If he had hurried the march he'd begun, he could have got ahead of Caecina while he was still wavering, or reached the legions before the decisive fight. Some urged him to take his most loyal men, avoid Ravenna, and go by hidden paths to Hostilia or Cremona. Others wanted him to call the Praetorian cohorts from the city and break through with a strong force. He wasted the time for action in useless delay and discussion, and then rejected both plans. In a crisis the worst course is the one he took, following a middle path: he wasn't bold enough, and he wasn't careful enough.
3:41 He wrote to Vitellius asking for help. Three cohorts arrived with a British cavalry regiment, too many to slip by unnoticed and too few to force a way through. But even in such great danger Valens didn't lose his bad name: he was believed to take forbidden pleasures and to dishonor the households of his hosts with adultery and seductions. He had power, money, and the last lust of a collapsing fortune. When the infantry and cavalry finally arrived, the folly of his plan showed. With so small a force he couldn't pass through the enemy even if it had been completely loyal, and the loyalty they brought wasn't above suspicion. Shame and respect for their general's presence still held them back, but that is no lasting chain with men who fear danger and don't care about disgrace. Because of that fear he sent the cohorts ahead to Ariminum and ordered the cavalry to guard his rear. He himself, with a few companions whom bad luck hadn't changed, turned into Umbria and then Etruria. There, after learning how the battle of Cremona had ended, he made a plan that wasn't cowardly and, had it worked, would have been terrible: to seize ships, land in any part of the province of Narbonensis, and stir up the Gauls, the armies and the peoples of Germany, starting a new war.
3:42 After Valens left, the garrison holding Ariminum lost heart. Cornelius Fuscus brought up his army, sent Liburnian ships along the nearest shores, and surrounded the place by land and sea. The plains of Umbria and the stretch of Picenum washed by the Adriatic were taken, and all Italy was now divided between Vespasian and Vitellius along the ridges of the Apennines. From the bay of Pisa, Fabius Valens was driven by a dead calm at sea or a contrary wind into the harbor of Hercules Monoecus. Not far away was Marius Maturus, procurator of the Maritime Alps, loyal to Vitellius, who hadn't yet given up his oath though everyone around him was hostile. He welcomed Valens politely and frightened him, by his advice, out of rashly entering Gallia Narbonensis. At the same time the loyalty of the others was cracking from fear. For the procurator Valerius Paullinus, an enterprising officer who had been a friend of Vespasian before his rise, had made the surrounding communities swear allegiance to him.
3:43 Paullinus had stirred up everyone who, discharged by Vitellius, was taking up the war on their own, and he held the colony of Forum Julii, the key to the sea, with a garrison. He carried more weight because Forum Julii was his hometown and because he was respected by the Praetorians, among whom he had once been a tribune. The locals themselves, out of local pride and hope for his future power, worked hard to help the cause. When these preparations, solid in fact and swollen by rumor, spread among the wavering Vitellianists, Fabius Valens went back to his ships with four bodyguards, three friends and as many centurions. Maturus and the others who wished to stay and be sworn in to Vespasian were free to do so. Though the sea was safer for Valens than the coast or the towns, he was unsure of the future and more certain of what to avoid than whom to trust. Bad weather drove him onto the Stoechades, islands of the people of Massilia, where Liburnian ships sent by Paullinus caught him.
## The provinces choose Vespasian; trouble in Britain
3:44 Once Valens was captured, everything swung over to the winner's side. It started in Spain with the 1st legion, the Adjutrix, which hated Vitellius because it remembered Otho, and it pulled the 10th and the 6th along. Gaul didn't hold back. In Britain, goodwill toward Vespasian, who had commanded the 2nd legion there under Claudius and made a name in the war, brought the province over, though not without stirring among the other legions. Many centurions and soldiers in them had been promoted by Vitellius and were uneasy about swapping an emperor they'd already tested.
3:45 These quarrels and the frequent rumors of civil war lifted the spirits of the Britons. Their leader was Venutius, who, besides his natural fierceness and hatred of the Roman name, was driven by his own bitterness against Queen Cartismandua. Cartismandua ruled the Brigantes, strong in noble birth. Her power had grown since she captured King Caractacus by trickery and was seen as having adorned the triumph of Claudius Caesar. Then came wealth and the luxury of success. She spurned her husband Venutius and took his armor-bearer Vellocatus as her husband and as sharer of the kingdom. At once her house was shaken by the scandal: the people's loyalty was with the husband, and the adulterer had only the queen's lust and cruelty behind him. So Venutius brought in auxiliaries, and with the help of a revolt among the Brigantes themselves, put Cartismandua in extreme danger. Then she asked the Romans for help. Our infantry and cavalry units fought in battles of mixed success, but they did pull the queen out of danger. The kingdom was left to Venutius, and the war to us.
## Germany, the Dacians and Anicetus in Pontus
3:46 In those same days Germany was thrown into turmoil, and through the carelessness of the generals, the mutiny of the legions, attacks from outside and the treachery of allies, Roman power was almost brought down. I will soon tell of that war, its causes and its results, since it ran on longer. The Dacians, a people never to be trusted, were also stirred up, and with the army taken out of Moesia they were without fear. At first they only watched events quietly. But when they saw Italy blazing with war and everything turning hostile to everything else, they stormed the winter quarters of the cohorts and cavalry units and took both banks of the Danube. They were already preparing to wipe out the legions' camp, but Mucianus set the 6th legion against them, since he knew of the victory at Cremona and didn't want a foreign threat to come crashing in from both sides if Dacians and Germans broke in from different directions. As so often at other times, the good fortune of the Roman people was with us, bringing Mucianus and the forces of the East there, and bringing about what we settled at Cremona in the meantime. Fonteius Agrippa was moved from Asia, which he had governed for a year as proconsul, and put in charge of Moesia, with extra troops from the Vitellianist army. Scattering that army through the provinces and tying it down in foreign wars was part of the plan, and of the peace.
3:47 The other nations weren't quiet either. A barbarian slave who had once commanded the royal fleet suddenly set Pontus under arms. This was Anicetus, a freedman of Polemon, once very powerful, who couldn't stand the change after the kingdom was turned into a province. In Vitellius's name he raised the tribes that live along Pontus, bribed the neediest with hopes of plunder, and, leading a force not to be sneered at, struck without warning at Trapezus, a city of old fame, founded by Greeks on the far edge of the Pontic coast. A cohort there, once part of the royal forces, was cut down. They had since been given Roman citizenship, and while their standards and arms were Roman style, they kept the laziness and looseness of Greeks. Anicetus also set the fleet on fire and slipped away across an empty sea, because Mucianus had brought the pick of the Liburnian ships and all the soldiers to Byzantium. The barbarians even roamed the sea with contempt in ships they had built overnight. They call them camarae: narrow sides, a broad belly, joined without bronze or iron. When the sea is rough, they build up the sides of the ships with planks as the waves rise, until the boat is closed in like a house. So they roll among the waves with a prow at each end and oars that can be switched, so that landing from either direction is easy and safe.
3:48 This drew Vespasian's attention, and he picked detachments from the legions and a leader, Virdius Geminus, a soldier of proven record. Geminus attacked the disorderly enemy, scattered by greed for plunder, and drove them onto their ships. Liburnian ships were quickly fitted out, and he chased Anicetus to the mouth of the river Cohibus, where he was safe under the protection of the king of the Sedochezi, whom he had won over as an ally with money and gifts. At first the king protected his suppliant with threats and weapons. But when the price of betrayal or war was laid out before him, with the shaky faith typical of barbarians he agreed to Anicetus's death and handed over the refugees. So the slave war was ended. Vespasian was pleased with this victory, with everything flowing beyond his prayers, when the news of the battle of Cremona reached him in Egypt. So he hurried on to Alexandria all the faster, to squeeze the broken armies of Vitellius, and a city that needs outside help for its food, with hunger. He was also preparing to invade Africa, which lies on the same coast, by land and sea, planning to close off the grain supplies and cause shortage and quarreling among his enemies.
## Antonius after Cremona; the march to the Apennines
3:49 While the empire was changing hands in this shaking of the whole world, Primus Antonius after Cremona did not behave nearly as blamelessly. He may have thought the war had been done justice and the rest would come easily, or else success in such a nature revealed his greed, pride and other hidden vices. He swaggered through Italy as if it were a conquered country and courted the legions as if they were his own. In everything he said and did he was building himself a road to power. To fill the soldiers with a spirit of license, he offered the legions the posts of the centurions who had been killed, to be filled by their vote. By that vote the most unruly men were chosen. The soldiers were no longer under their generals' control; the generals were dragged along by the soldiers' violence. Antonius soon turned these mutinous practices, which wrecked discipline, into plunder for himself. He didn't fear Mucianus's approach at all, which was a worse mistake than having scorned Vespasian.
3:50 As winter drew near and the fields were soaked by the Padus, the army moved on lightly equipped. The standards and eagles of the victorious legions, the soldiers weighed down by wounds or age, and many who were unhurt were left behind at Verona. The cohorts, the cavalry units and men picked from the legions seemed enough for a war that was already beaten. The 11th legion had joined them. At first it had hesitated, but now that things were going well it was anxious about having stayed away. Six thousand Dalmatians, a fresh levy, went along. The commander was Pompeius Silvanus, a man of consular rank, but the real force behind the plans was the legion's legate, Annius Bassus. Under an appearance of deference he steered Silvanus, who was slow at war and wasted the days of action in talk, and he was there, quietly and energetically, for everything that had to be done. To these forces were added the best of the marines of the Ravenna fleet, who demanded service in the legions. Dalmatians filled up the fleet. The army and its generals halted at Fanum Fortunae, the Temple of Fortune, unsure of the overall plan, because they had heard that the Praetorian cohorts had marched out of Rome and thought garrisons held the Apennines. They were also frightened by the shortage in a region worn down by war, and by the mutinous shouts of soldiers demanding the clavarium (the word for the bonus). They had provided neither money nor grain, and haste and greed made it worse, since whatever could have been received was being seized.
## A soldier boasts of killing his brother
3:51 I have the best authority for this: among the victors there was such disregard for right and wrong that an ordinary cavalry trooper declared he had killed his brother in the last battle and asked the generals for a reward. Human law didn't allow them to honor that killing, and the logic of the war didn't allow them to punish it. They put him off, saying that what he had earned was too big to be paid at once. Nothing more is recorded. Still, the earlier civil wars had a similar horror. In the battle fought at the Janiculum against Cinna, a soldier on Pompey's side killed his brother, and when he learned what he'd done, killed himself, as Sisenna tells it. That is how much sharper our ancestors' remorse for crimes was, just as their glory for virtues was. I will mention these and other examples from old memory, not out of place, whenever the subject and the occasion call for examples of good or comfort in the face of evil.
## Mucianus and Antonius fall out
3:52 Antonius and the party's other generals decided to send cavalry ahead and scout all of Umbria for any place where the ridges of the Apennines could be climbed more gently, to call up the eagles, standards and whatever soldiers were at Verona, and to fill the Padus and the sea with supply convoys. Some of the generals were spinning out delays, since Antonius was already too much for them and they hoped for surer things from Mucianus. For Mucianus, uneasy at so quick a victory, and thinking that unless he took Rome in person he would have no share in the war's glory, kept writing to Primus and Varus in middle-of-the-road terms. He argued now that they must press on, now that delay had its uses, and he worded it so that, depending on how things turned out, he could deny a failure or claim a success. He gave plainer instructions to Plotius Griphus, whom Vespasian had recently put in the senatorial order and placed over a legion, and to the others he could count on. All of them wrote back with criticism of the haste of Primus and Varus, in terms that pleased Mucianus. By sending these letters on to Vespasian, he made sure that Antonius's plans and deeds would not be valued as Antonius had hoped.
3:53 Antonius took this badly and blamed Mucianus, whose accusations had cheapened his own dangers. He wasn't careful in his talk either, being unrestrained in tongue and not used to obeying. He wrote Vespasian a letter in a tone more arrogant than anyone should use to an emperor, and with a veiled attack on Mucianus. He wrote: "I drove the Pannonian legions into arms. My urging roused the Moesian commanders. My steadiness broke through the Alps, seized Italy, and cut off the German and Raetian auxiliaries. I scattered Vitellius's divided and dispersed legions, first with a storm of cavalry and then with the strength of the infantry through a day and a night, and that was the finest deed of all, and it was mine. The fall of Cremona belongs to the war. The civil strife of the old days cost the state more and brought down many cities. I serve my emperor not with messages and letters but with my own hand and arms. I won't stand in the way of the glory of those who meanwhile settled Dacia. They cared about the peace of Moesia, and I cared about the safety and security of Italy. By my urging, the Gauls and the Spains, the strongest part of the world, were turned to Vespasian. But my labors are wasted if only those who weren't present in the danger win the rewards of danger." Mucianus did not miss any of this. From it came bitter quarrels, which Antonius fed openly and Mucianus slyly, and so with less hope of ever being settled.
## Vitellius hides the news; Julius Agrestis
3:54 After his forces were broken at Cremona, Vitellius hid the news of the disaster, and by this foolish pretense he put off not the troubles but the remedies for them. Had he confessed and consulted, he would still have had hope and strength. But by making up a story that everything was fine, he made his situation worse with lies. There was an astonishing silence about the war in his presence. Talk was forbidden throughout the city, so people who would have told the truth if they could have, because it was forbidden, spread worse stories. The enemy's generals also helped inflate the report: they sent back captured spies of Vitellius after leading them around, so they would see the strength of the victorious army. Vitellius questioned all of them in private and then ordered them killed. A centurion, Julius Agrestis, showed remarkable steadfastness. After many conversations in which he tried in vain to fire Vitellius up to courage, he persuaded him to send him personally to look at the enemy's forces and see what had happened at Cremona. He didn't try to fool Antonius with secret scouting, but told him what the emperor had ordered and what he himself meant to do, and asked leave to see everything. People were sent to show him the battlefield, the remains of Cremona, and the captured legions. Agrestis went back to Vitellius, who denied that what he reported was true and even accused him of being bribed. "Since you need strong proof," Agrestis said, "and my life or death can't be of any other use to you now, I'll give you something you can believe." And with that he left, and confirmed his words by killing himself. Some say Vitellius had him killed, but they report the same of his loyalty and steadfastness.
## Vitellius at Mevania
3:55 Vitellius, as if woken from sleep, ordered Julius Priscus and Alfenus Varus, with fourteen Praetorian cohorts and all the cavalry units, to hold the Apennines. A legion drawn from the fleet followed. So many thousands of armed men, picked horses and men, would have been strong enough even to take the offensive under another leader. The remaining cohorts were given to his brother Lucius Vitellius to protect the city. Vitellius himself gave up none of his usual luxury, and in a hurry from lack of confidence, he rushed the elections, in which he named consuls for many years ahead. He handed out treaties to allies and Latin rights to foreigners. He relieved some of tribute and others of duties. In short, with no thought for the future, he tore the empire to pieces. The mob gaped at the size of his gifts, and the stupidest bought them with money, while the wise held that gifts that couldn't be given or received without ruining the state were worthless. At last, with the army that had settled in at Mevania demanding him, and with a large train of senators, many of whom he was dragging along by ambition and more by fear, Vitellius gave in and came to the camp, uncertain in mind and at the mercy of untrustworthy advice.
3:56 While he was speaking to the troops (strange to say), so many ill-omened birds flew over him that they hid the daylight under a dark cloud. Another dreadful sign followed: a bull ran away from the altar, scattered the equipment for the sacrifice, and was stabbed far off, not in the way victims are struck. But the greatest omen of all was Vitellius himself. He knew nothing of soldiering and had no foresight in planning. He kept asking others what order the column should march in, how scouting should be done, and how far to push the war or drag it out, and at every message he was shaking in face and walk, and then drunk. Sick of camp life, and having heard that the Misenum fleet had gone over, he went back to Rome, fearing each newest wound and careless of the greatest danger. For though he could have crossed the Apennines with his army's strength intact and attacked in the open an enemy worn down by winter and shortage, he scattered his forces and handed his keenest soldiers, stubborn to the last, over to be butchered or captured. The most experienced centurions disagreed, and if consulted would have told him the truth. The closest of his friends kept them away, since the emperor's ears had been trained so that anything useful sounded harsh, and he accepted nothing but what was pleasant and would hurt him.
## Misenum and Campania rebel
3:57 The fleet at Misenum (this shows how much even one man's boldness counts in civil strife) was drawn into revolt by Claudius Faventinus, a centurion dismissed in disgrace by Galba, who forged letters of Vespasian offering a reward for treason. The fleet was under Claudius Apollinaris, a man neither steady in loyalty nor energetic in treachery. Apinius Tiro, a former praetor who happened to be at Minturnae, offered himself as leader of the rebels. They pushed the towns and colonies into the movement. Puteoli was especially keen for Vespasian, while Capua stayed loyal to Vitellius, so they mixed local rivalry into the civil war. Vitellius picked Claudius Julianus, who had recently ruled the Misenum fleet with an easy hand, to calm the soldiers' spirits. A city cohort and gladiators, whom Julianus commanded, were given to him as help. When the two camps were set up facing each other, Julianus went over to Vespasian's side with little hesitation, and they took Tarracina, safe because of its walls and position rather than their own abilities.
3:58 When Vitellius learned this, he left part of his forces at Narnia with the prefects of the Praetorian Guard and set his brother Lucius, with six cohorts and 500 cavalry, against the war advancing through Campania. Sick at heart, he was cheered by the soldiers' enthusiasm and the people's shouts demanding arms, while he called a cowardly crowd, who would dare nothing beyond words, by the false name of an army and legions. At the urging of his freedmen (the more distinguished his friends, the less faithful), he ordered the tribes to be called together and swore in those who gave their names. With too many coming, he split the work of enrolling between the consuls. He ordered the senators to supply a number of slaves and a weight of silver. The Roman Knights offered their service and money, and even freedmen asked for the same duty without being asked. That show of duty, which began in fear, turned into goodwill, and most of them pitied not so much Vitellius as the fate and position of the emperorship. He himself did his best to wring pity out of them with his face, his voice and his tears, generous with promises and, as frightened people tend to be, extravagant. He even asked to be called Caesar, a title he had refused before, but now out of superstition about the name, and because in fear the advice of the wise and the rumor of the crowd are heard alike. But as all undertakings begun on thoughtless impulse, strong at the start, grow weak with time, the senators and Knights drifted away little by little, at first hesitantly and when he wasn't there, then scornfully and without distinction, until Vitellius, ashamed of the failure of his effort, gave up asking for what wasn't being given.
## The Flavianist army crosses the Apennines
3:59 Vitellius's seizure of Mevania, and the war seeming to be reborn with fresh strength, had terrified Italy. His frightened departure now added a clear boost of favor for the Flavianist side. The Samnites, Peligni and Marsi woke up, jealous that Campania had got ahead of them, and, like people in a new allegiance, were keen in every task of war. But the army, battered by foul winter weather crossing the Apennines, could hardly struggle through the snow even with no one opposing it, and saw how much danger they would have faced if luck hadn't turned Vitellius back, luck that helped the Flavianist generals as often as sound planning did. There they met Petilius Cerialis, who had slipped past Vitellius's guards in country dress and with his knowledge of the ground. Cerialis was a close relative of Vespasian and a man not without reputation in war, and so he was taken in among the generals. Many have said that escape was open to Flavius Sabinus and Domitian too, and messengers sent by Antonius got through in various disguises, showing them a place to go and a force to protect them. Sabinus pleaded poor health, unfit for hardship and daring. Domitian had the courage, but the guards Vitellius had put on him, though they promised to join his flight, were feared as plotting against him. And Vitellius himself, out of regard for his own family ties, was planning nothing cruel against Domitian.
3:60 When the Flavianist leaders reached Carsulae, they took a few days to rest while the eagles and standards of the legions caught up. The site of the camp itself was pleasing, with a wide view, a safe supply line, and very prosperous towns at its back. They also hoped for talks with the Vitellianists, who were ten miles away, and for defections. The soldiers took this badly and preferred victory to peace. They wouldn't even wait for their own legions, whom they saw as sharers in the plunder rather than in the dangers. Antonius called them to an assembly and told them that Vitellius still had forces, uncertain ones if they had time to think, but fierce if they gave up hope. "The beginning of a civil war has to be left to luck," he said. "Victory is finished by plans and reason. Already the Misenum fleet and the fairest coast of Campania have revolted from Vitellius, and out of the whole world he has nothing left except what lies between Tarracina and Narnia. We won enough glory in the battle at Cremona, and too much ill will from the destruction of Cremona. Don't crave to capture Rome rather than save it. Your rewards will be greater and your honor far greater if you win safety for the Senate and the Roman people without bloodshed." Words like these calmed their spirits.
3:61 Not long after, the legions arrived. With the army swollen in fear and rumor, the Vitellianist cohorts wavered. No one urged them to fight, and many were competing to go over, to hand their companies and squadrons to the victor as a gift and to win favor for themselves later. From them it was learned that Interamna, on the nearby plains, was held by a garrison of 400 cavalry. Varus was sent at once with a light force and killed a few who resisted. More threw down their arms and asked for pardon. Some fled back to the camp and filled everything with fear, magnifying the courage and numbers of the enemy with rumors, to soften the disgrace of having lost their post. Among the Vitellianists there was no punishment for disgrace. The rewards of deserters overturned loyalty, and all that was left was a contest in treachery. There were frequent desertions of tribunes and centurions. The rank-and-file soldiers had hardened in their loyalty to Vitellius, until Priscus and Alfenus deserted the camp and went back to Vitellius, and in their shame at treason freed everyone else of theirs.
## The death of Fabius Valens
3:62 In those same days Fabius Valens was put to death in custody at Urbinum. His head was shown to the Vitellianist cohorts so they wouldn't cherish any further hope, since they believed Valens had made his way into the German provinces and was raising old and new armies there. The sight pushed them into despair. And the Flavianist army, to an amazing degree, took the death of Valens with raised spirits as the end of the war. Valens was born at Anagnia, into a Roman Knight family. He was a man of loose habits but not without talent, and he sought a name for refinement through wild living. At the Juvenalia games under Nero he acted in farces, at first as if forced and later by choice, with more skill than decency. As legate of a legion he first supported Verginius and then slandered him. He murdered Fonteius Capito, either after he had corrupted him or because he couldn't. A traitor to Galba, he was loyal to Vitellius, and the treachery of others made him look good.
## The Vitellianist army gives up at Narnia
3:63 With hope cut off on every side, the Vitellianist soldiers meant to go over to the other side, but to do it with some dignity too. They came down under their standards and banners to the plains below Narnia. The Flavianist army stood in dense ranks along the road, alert and armed as if for battle. The Vitellianists were taken in between them, and as they stood surrounded, Primus Antonius spoke kindly to them. Part were ordered to stay at Narnia and part at Interamna. Some of the victorious legions were left with them, not oppressive to men who stayed quiet but strong against any rebelliousness. In those days Primus and Varus didn't stop sending frequent messages offering Vitellius safety, money and a quiet retreat in Campania, if he would lay down his arms and put himself and his children in Vespasian's hands. Mucianus wrote letters in the same terms. Vitellius mostly trusted them, and talked about the number of slaves he'd take and the choice of a home on the coast. Such a numbness had come over his mind that if the others hadn't remembered he had been emperor, he would have forgotten it himself.
## Sabinus is urged to act and talks with Vitellius
3:64 The leading men of the state, in private talks, urged Flavius Sabinus, prefect of the city, to claim a share of the victory and the fame. They told him: "You have your own soldiers in the city cohorts. The watch cohorts won't fail you. There are our own slaves, the good fortune of the party, and the fact that everything comes easy to winners. Don't give up the glory to Antonius and Varus. Vitellius has only a few cohorts, and they're shaken by gloomy news from every side. The people's mood shifts easily, and if you offer yourself as leader, the same flatteries will soon be ready for Vespasian. Vitellius wasn't equal to his position even in good times, and he is all the weaker in his fall. The credit for ending the war will go to whoever takes the city. It would be right for you to keep the empire for your brother, and right for Vespasian to count everyone else below you."
3:65 Sabinus, old and frail, took these words without enthusiasm. But some people attacked him with hidden suspicions, saying that out of envy and rivalry he was holding back his brother's rise. For Flavius Sabinus, the elder, had outranked Vespasian in influence and money while both were private citizens. He was believed to have propped up his brother's failing credit, but only sparingly, taking Vespasian's house and land as security. So, though harmony remained on the surface, hidden grudges were feared. A kinder reading is that the gentle man shrank from blood and killing, and that was why he kept holding talks with Vitellius about peace and laying down arms on terms. They met often at home, and finally, as report had it, made a deal in the temple of Apollo. Their words had two witnesses, Cluvius Rufus and Silius Italicus. Their faces were noted by those watching from a distance: Vitellius's was downcast and degraded, and Sabinus's was not gloating but more like that of a man feeling pity.
## Vitellius's friends urge him to fight
3:66 If Vitellius had been able to sway the minds of his supporters as easily as he had given way himself, Vespasian's army would have entered the city without bloodshed. But the more loyal each man was to Vitellius, the more he rejected peace and terms, pointing to the danger and disgrace of it, and to a trust that rested on the victor's whim. They told Vitellius: "Vespasian isn't so arrogant as to let Vitellius live as a private man, and even the defeated won't stand for it, so pity itself is dangerous. You are an old man, and you've had your fill of good fortune and bad, but what name and what standing will your son Germanicus have? Now they promise you wealth, a household and a comfortable retreat in Campania. But once Vespasian has seized the throne, neither he nor his friends nor even his armies will feel safe until every rival is gone. Fabius Valens, a prisoner kept in reserve against reversals, was too dangerous for them even so. And Primus and Fuscus, and Mucianus, who is the very picture of that party, will have no power over Vitellius except the license to kill him. Caesar did not leave Pompey alive, and Augustus did not leave Antony, unless Vespasian has a loftier spirit, Vespasian who was your father's dependent when your father was Claudius's colleague. In fact, to live up to your father's censorship, his three consulships, and all the honors of your distinguished house, at least let despair arm you for daring. The soldiers are standing firm, and the people are still zealous. Finally, nothing worse can happen than what we are rushing into on our own. If we are beaten, we must die, and if we surrender, we must die. All that matters is this: do we breathe our last in mockery and insult, or in courage?"
## Vitellius gives up the throne in the Forum
3:67 Vitellius's ears were deaf to brave counsel. His mind was swamped with pity and worry that by stubborn fighting he'd leave the winner less merciful toward his wife and children. He also had an aged, worn-out mother. She had died a few days earlier, at a fortunate moment, which spared her the ruin of her house, and she had gained nothing from her son's reign except grief and a good name. On the 18th of December, after hearing that the legion and cohorts that had surrendered at Narnia had defected, he left the Palace in dark mourning dress with his household weeping around him. His little son was carried in a litter, as if in a funeral procession. The people's voices were flattering but badly timed. The soldiers kept an ominous silence.
3:68 Hardly anyone was so forgetful of human affairs as not to be moved by that sight: a Roman emperor, lord of the human race only a short time before, leaving the seat of his fortune and going out through the people and the city to give up his power. They had never seen or heard of anything like it. Sudden violence had brought down Caesar the dictator, and a hidden plot had brought down Caligula. Night and an unknown countryside had hidden Nero's flight. Piso and Galba had fallen, one might say, in battle. Now Vitellius, in his own assembly, among his own soldiers, with even the women looking on, said a few words suited to the sorrow of the moment: that he was stepping down for the sake of peace and the state, and that they should only keep his memory and pity his brother, his wife, and his young, innocent children. At the same time he held out his son, recommending him first to individuals and then to the whole crowd. Finally, with tears choking him, he unfastened the dagger from his side and held it out to the consul standing next to him (it was Caecilius Simplex), as a sign of giving back the power of death and life over citizens. When the consul refused it, and those standing in the assembly shouted against it, he left, as if to place the symbols of power in the temple of Concord and go to his brother's house. Here the shouting grew louder, from people who blocked him from a private house and called him back to the Palatine. The other route was cut off, and only the one leading to the Sacred Way was open. Then, at a loss, he went back to the Palatine. The rumor that he was giving up the throne had gotten there first, and Flavius Sabinus had sent written orders to the tribunes of the cohorts to keep their soldiers under control.
## Sabinus takes the Capitol
3:69 Then, as if the whole state had fallen into Vespasian's lap, the leading senators, most of the Roman Knights, and all the city soldiers and the watch filled Flavius Sabinus's house. Word came there of the feeling among the common people and the threats of the German cohorts. He had gone too far to turn back. Each man, afraid for himself in case the Vitellianists hunted them down while they were scattered and therefore weaker, pushed him, hesitating, into arms. But as happens in such matters, everyone gave advice and few shared the risk. As Sabinus's armed escort came down by Lake Fundanus, the most eager of the Vitellianists met them. A small fight followed there, in an unexpected tumult, but it went well for the Vitellianists. In the alarm, Sabinus took the safest choice available and occupied the citadel of the Capitol with a mixed body of soldiers and some senators and Knights. It isn't easy to give their names, since after Vespasian's victory many claimed that service to the party. Some women even went through the siege with them, the most notable being Verulana Gratilla, who followed no children or relatives, only the war. The Vitellianist soldiers surrounded the besieged with a careless guard, and so at nightfall Sabinus brought into the Capitol his own children and his brother's son Domitian. He sent a messenger by an unwatched route to the Flavianist generals to tell them that they were surrounded and in trouble unless help came. He passed the night so quietly that he could have left without harm. Vitellius's soldiers were fierce against danger but not very attentive to the labor of watching, and a sudden winter downpour made it hard to see or hear.
3:70 At first light, before either side began hostilities, Sabinus sent Cornelius Martialis, a chief centurion, to Vitellius with instructions and a complaint that the agreement was being broken. His message was: "It was clearly a show, a pretense of giving up power, to fool so many distinguished men. If not, why did you go from the Rostra to your brother's house, which looks over the Forum and draws the eyes of the crowd, instead of to the Aventine and your wife's family home? That would have suited a private man avoiding every look of power. Instead you went back to the Palatine, the very stronghold of power. From there an armed column was sent out, the busiest part of the city was strewn with the bodies of innocent people, and the Capitol itself was not spared. I am only a civilian, one senator among many. While the quarrel between Vespasian and Vitellius was being decided by battles between legions, by captured cities and surrendered cohorts, and while Spain, Germany and Britain were deserting, Vespasian's brother held to his allegiance, until he was invited to talk terms. Peace and harmony are useful to the beaten and only a credit to the winners. If you regret the agreement, don't draw your sword against me, whom you deceived by treachery, or against Vespasian's son, who is barely grown. What is gained by killing one old man and one young man? Go meet the legions and fight there for supreme power. Everything else will follow the outcome of the battle." Flustered, Vitellius answered with a few words in his own defense, putting the blame on the soldiers, whose excessive fire his moderation couldn't match. He advised Martialis to slip away secretly through a hidden part of the Palace, so that the soldiers wouldn't kill him as the go-between of a hated peace. Vitellius could neither command nor forbid. He was no longer an emperor, only the cause of the war.
## The Capitol burns
3:71 Martialis had barely got back to the Capitol when the furious soldiers arrived, with no leader, each man acting on his own. At a quick march they passed the Forum and the temples overhanging it and drove their line up the opposite hill as far as the first gates of the Capitol's citadel. In ancient times there were porticoes on the right side of the slope as you went up. The defenders climbed onto their roof and buried the Vitellianists under stones and tiles. The attackers had no weapons but swords, and sending for catapults and missiles seemed to take too long. They threw torches onto a projecting portico, and following the fire they would have broken through the scorched gates of the Capitol, if Sabinus hadn't torn down statues on all sides, the glories of their forebears, and thrown them up in the very entrance like a wall. Then the attackers rushed other approaches to the Capitol, by the grove of the Asylum and where the Tarpeian rock is climbed by a hundred steps. Both attacks took the defenders by surprise, and the closer and fiercer one came through the Asylum. The climbers couldn't be stopped as they scaled the joined buildings, which, built up high in long peace, were level with the ground of the Capitol. Here there's doubt whether the attackers threw fire onto the roofs, or whether the besieged did, which is the more common story, as they pushed back the climbers who had made progress. From there the fire slipped to the porticoes next to the temples. Then the eagles holding up the pediment, made of old wood, caught flame and fed it. So the Capitol burned to the ground with its gates shut, and no one defended it and no one plundered it.
3:72 This was the saddest and most shameful thing to happen to the Roman republic since the city was founded. With no foreign enemy, and with the gods kind to us if our ways had allowed it, the seat of Jupiter Best and Greatest, founded by our ancestors with proper auspices as a pledge of empire, which neither Porsenna when the city surrendered nor the Gauls when it was captured had been able to violate, was destroyed by the madness of emperors. The Capitol had burned once before, in civil war, but then by one person's crime. Now it was openly besieged and openly burned. What were the reasons for taking up arms? What price was paid for so great a disaster? Were we fighting for our country? King Tarquinius Priscus had vowed it in the Sabine war and laid the foundations, more in hope of future greatness than to fit the still modest means of the Roman people. Later Servius Tullius, with the allies' eager help, and then Tarquinius Superbus, with the enemy spoils from the capture of Suessa Pometia, built it up. But the glory of finishing it was saved for liberty. After the kings were driven out, Horatius Pulvillus dedicated it in his second consulship, with a magnificence that the immense wealth later won by the Roman people adorned rather than increased. It was built again on the same footprint after it had burned, following an interval of 415 years, in the consulship of Lucius Scipio and Gaius Norbanus. Sulla as victor took on the work but did not dedicate it, the one thing denied to his good fortune. The name of Lutatius Catulus stayed on it, among all the great works of the Caesars, down to Vitellius. That was the temple that was then burning.
## Sabinus and Atticus taken; Domitian escapes
3:73 But the disaster caused more panic among the besieged than among the besiegers. Vitellius's soldiers lacked neither cunning nor steadiness in uncertainty. On the other side were frightened soldiers and a sluggish commander, dazed as if his wits had been taken, who could not use his tongue or his ears, who couldn't be steered by others' advice and couldn't carry out his own. He was swung this way and that by the enemy's shouts, forbidding what he had ordered and ordering what he had forbidden. Then, as happens in lost causes, everyone gave orders and no one carried them out. Finally they threw away their arms and looked around for ways to escape and hide. The Vitellianists burst in and mixed everything with blood, steel and flames. A few military men, most notably Cornelius Martialis, Aemilius Pacensis, Casperius Niger and Didius Sceva, dared to fight and were cut down. Flavius Sabinus, unarmed and not trying to flee, was surrounded, and with him the consul Quintius Atticus, picked out for the shadow of his office and for his own vanity, because he had posted edicts among the people praising Vespasian and slandering Vitellius. The rest escaped by various chances, some in slave dress, others hidden by the loyalty of their clients and tucked away among baggage. Some caught the Vitellianists' watchword by which they knew each other, and by asking it and answering it themselves, used boldness as a hiding place.
3:74 At the first break-in, Domitian hid with the temple-keeper. Through a freedman's cleverness he put on a linen robe, mixed in with a crowd of worshippers, and went unrecognized, and he hid at the house of Cornelius Primus, one of his father's clients, near the Velabrum. When his father held power, he knocked down the temple-keeper's lodging and put up a modest chapel to Jupiter the Preserver, with an altar bearing the story of his adventure carved in marble. Later, when he took the empire himself, he dedicated a huge temple to Jupiter the Guardian, with himself in the god's lap. Sabinus and Atticus were loaded with chains and brought to Vitellius, who received them with no hostile word or look, while those who demanded the right to kill them, and payment for their work, roared. A shout started among those nearby, and the dirtiest part of the crowd demanded Sabinus's execution, mixing threats with flattery. Vitellius was standing before the steps of the Palatine, preparing to plead for him, and they prevailed on him to give up. Then Sabinus was stabbed and mangled, his head was cut off, and his body was dragged to the Gemoniae.
3:75 That was the end of a man not at all to be despised. He had served the state for thirty-five years and was distinguished at home and in the field. No one could question his blamelessness and fairness. He talked too much, and that was the only fault rumor charged him with, in the seven years he governed Moesia and the twelve he was prefect of the city. At the end of his life some thought him sluggish, and many thought him moderate and sparing of citizens' blood. All agree that before Vespasian became emperor, the glory of the family rested with Sabinus. I have heard that his killing pleased Mucianus. Many even said that peace was served by ending the rivalry between the two, one of whom thought of himself as the emperor's brother, the other as his partner in power. But Vitellius resisted the people's demand for the consul's execution. He had been soothed, and was, as it were, paying back a favor, because when people asked who had set the Capitol on fire, Atticus had offered himself as the guilty one. By that confession, whether or not it was a lie that suited the moment, he seemed to have taken the blame and the crime on himself and cleared the Vitellianist side.
## Tarracina falls to Lucius Vitellius
3:76 In those same days Lucius Vitellius, camped near Feronia, was threatening Tarracina with destruction. The gladiators and seamen shut up there didn't dare leave the walls or risk danger in the open. As I mentioned above, Julianus commanded the gladiators and Apollinaris the seamen, wanton and lazy men, more like gladiators than commanders. They kept no watches and didn't strengthen the weak spots in the walls. Loose day and night, they made the pleasant spots along the shore ring with their noise, sent their soldiers off here and there to serve their luxury, and talked about the war only at their dinners. A few days earlier Apinius Tiro had left, and by harshly collecting gifts and money from the towns, he added more ill will than strength to his side.
3:77 Meanwhile a slave of Vergilius Capito deserted to Lucius Vitellius. He promised that if he were given a force, he would hand over the unguarded citadel. Late at night he placed light cohorts on the highest ridges of the mountains right above the enemy. From there the soldiers ran down more to slaughter than to fight. They cut down men who were unarmed or reaching for their arms, and some roused from sleep, all confused by the darkness, the panic, the blare of trumpets and the enemy's shouting. A few of the gladiators resisted and did not die unavenged. The rest rushed for the ships, where everything was tangled in the same terror, with country people mixed in, whom the Vitellianists butchered without distinction. Six Liburnian ships got away in the first uproar, among them Apollinaris, the fleet's prefect. The rest were captured on the beach, or sank under the weight of those rushing aboard. Julianus was brought to Lucius Vitellius, disgraced by a whipping, and had his throat cut in front of him. Some blamed Triaria, Lucius Vitellius's wife, for putting on a soldier's sword and acting with arrogance and cruelty amid the grief and ruin of Tarracina's fall. Lucius himself sent his brother a laurel-wreathed message about the success, asking whether he should return at once or press on to subdue Campania completely. That was good not only for Vespasian's side but for the state. For if the soldiers, fresh from victory and, on top of their natural stubbornness, fierce with success, had rushed on Rome, there would have been a fight of no small scale and the city would not have escaped destruction. For Lucius Vitellius, though infamous, had energy, and he was strong not through virtues, as good men are, but through vices, like every worst man.
## The Flavianist army delays; Antonius at Saxa Rubra
3:78 While this was going on on Vitellius's side, Vespasian's army left Narnia and spent the holiday days of Saturn at Ocriculum, in idleness. The reason given for so harmful a delay was waiting for Mucianus. Some accused Antonius with suspicions that he was delaying treacherously after secret letters from Vitellius offering a consulship, a marriageable daughter and a rich dowry as the price of betrayal. Others said this was made up to please Mucianus. Some said that the plan of all the generals was to show the war to the city rather than bring it, since the strongest cohorts had deserted Vitellius, and with all his support cut away he seemed likely to give up the throne. But everything was ruined by Sabinus's haste and then his cowardice. After rashly taking up arms, he had been unable to defend against three cohorts the Capitol's citadel, which was strongest and could not be stormed even by great armies. It isn't easy to pin on one man a fault that belonged to all. Mucianus held the victors back with ambiguous letters. Antonius, through misplaced deference, or while trying to throw the blame back on someone else, earned blame himself. The other generals, thinking the war was finished, left their mark on its ending. Not even Petilius Cerialis, sent ahead with a thousand cavalry to enter the city by the Via Salaria through cross-country roads in Sabine territory, had hurried enough, until the news of the Capitol's siege roused everyone at once.
3:79 Antonius came by the Via Flaminia to Saxa Rubra with much of the night already gone, bringing help that was too late. There he learned that Sabinus had been killed, the Capitol had burned, the city was trembling, and everything was grim. It was also reported that the common people and even slaves were arming for Vitellius. And Petilius Cerialis had lost a cavalry fight. He had rushed on carelessly, as if after a beaten enemy, and the Vitellianists, with foot soldiers mixed among their cavalry, met him. The fight took place not far from the city, among buildings, gardens and twisting lanes, which the Vitellianists knew well and which unsettled the strangers. The cavalry wasn't all of one mind either, since some had joined them who had recently surrendered at Narnia and were watching to see which way fortune would go. The cavalry prefect Julius Flavianus was captured, and the rest were scattered in shameful flight, with the victors not pursuing beyond Fidenae.
## The Senate's envoys
3:80 With this success the people's enthusiasm grew. The city crowd took up arms. A few had military shields, and more seized whatever weapons came to hand and demanded the signal for battle. Vitellius thanked them and told them to rush out to defend the city. Then the Senate was called, and envoys were chosen to go to the armies and urge agreement and peace in the name of the state. The envoys' fortunes varied. Those who met Petilius Cerialis came into extreme danger, since the soldiers rejected peace terms. The praetor Arulenus Rusticus was wounded, and the outrage was greater because, besides the violation of the title of envoy and praetor, there was the man's own personal dignity. His companions were beaten, and the lictor next to him, who tried to push back the crowd, was killed. If they had not been defended by an escort provided by the general, the sacred right of envoys, respected even among foreign peoples, would have been profaned with deadly violence by the madness of Roman citizens right under their country's walls. Those who came to Antonius were received in a better spirit, not because his soldiers were more restrained, but because the general had more authority.
3:81 Musonius Rufus, of Roman Knight rank, who modeled himself on philosophy and the teachings of the Stoics, had mixed in with the envoys. Moving among the companies, he began to lecture the armed men on the blessings of peace and the differences between peace and war. Most found it funny, and more found it tiresome. Some were ready to knock him down and trample him, if he hadn't given in to the warnings of the most level-headed and the threats of others and dropped his ill-timed wisdom. The Vestal Virgins also met them, with a letter from Vitellius to Antonius. He asked for one day to be taken out of the final struggle, and said that if they allowed a little delay, everything might be easier to arrange. The Virgins were sent away with honor, but the answer written back to Vitellius was that the murder of Sabinus and the burning of the Capitol had ended all dealings of war.
## The battle for Rome
3:82 Antonius did try to calm the legions, calling them to an assembly, so that they would camp near the Mulvian Bridge and enter the city the next day. His reason for delay was that the soldiers, worked up by the fighting, would show no regard for the people, the Senate, or even the temples and shrines of the gods. But they saw any postponement as hostile to victory. At the same time the banners glittering across the hills, though an unarmed crowd followed them, made it look like a hostile army. They advanced in three columns: one, just as it had been standing, along the Via Flaminia, one beside the bank of the Tiber, and the third approached the Colline Gate along the Via Salaria. The common people were scattered by a cavalry charge. The Vitellianist soldiers met them, themselves in three groups. Many varied battles took place before the city, but they mostly went well for the Flavianists, who were better in the planning of their generals. Only those who had turned to the left side of the city toward the gardens of Sallust, through narrow, slippery streets, were caught in a hard fight. The Vitellianists, standing on the garden walls, kept the climbers off with stones and javelins until late in the day, when the cavalry who had broken in through the Colline Gate surrounded them. The hostile armies also met in the Campus Martius. For the Flavianists there was fortune and a victory won so many times, while the Vitellianists charged on despair alone, and though beaten, gathered together again in the city.
3:83 The people stood by and watched the fighters, and as at a game, cheered first one side and then the other with shouts and applause. Whenever one side gave way, they demanded that the men hidden in shops, or those who had taken refuge in some house, be dragged out and killed, and so they got the larger share of the loot. For with the soldiers turned to blood and killing, the spoils went to the crowd. The whole city presented a savage and ugly picture: battles and wounds in one place, baths and taverns in another. There were pools of blood and heaps of bodies, and right next to them prostitutes and men just as shameless. There was every vice of idle luxury and every crime of the bitterest conquest, so that you'd think the same city was both raving mad and carousing. Armed armies had fought in the city before, twice when Lucius Sulla won and once when Cinna did. The cruelty then was no less, but now there was an inhuman unconcern, and not even for a moment did the pleasures stop. As if it were an extra joy added to their holidays, they exulted and enjoyed it, with no concern for either side, glad at the public disasters.
## The camp falls; Vitellius is found and killed
3:84 The hardest part was the storming of the camp, which the fiercest of the Vitellianists held as their last hope. So the victors pressed it all the harder, especially the veteran cohorts, bringing up at once everything ever devised for destroying the strongest cities: the tortoise, catapults, earthworks and torches. They kept shouting that all the toil and danger they had taken on in so many battles would be completed by this job. "We've given the city back to the Senate and the Roman people, and the temples to the gods," they shouted, "but a soldier's honor is in his camp. That is his country and his home. If we don't take it back right away, we'll have to spend the night under arms." On the other side, the Vitellianists, though unequal in numbers and in fate, still disturbed the victory, delayed the peace, and fouled homes and altars with blood, clinging to the last comforts of the beaten. Many, half-dead, breathed their last on the towers and battlements. When the gates were wrenched off, the remaining crowd threw itself on the victors, and they all fell with their wounds in front, turned toward the enemy. Even in dying, their concern was to have an honorable end. When the city fell, Vitellius had himself carried in a small chair through the back of the Palatine to his wife's house on the Aventine, so that if he could dodge the day by hiding, he could escape to his brother's cohorts at Tarracina. Then, from his changeable nature and from the way fear works, since everything in front of a man afraid of everything displeases him most, he went back to the Palatine, vast and deserted, where even the lowest slaves had slipped away or avoided meeting him. The emptiness and the silence of the rooms terrified him. He tried closed doors and shuddered at the empty chambers. Worn out by his miserable wandering and hiding himself in a shameful hiding place, he was dragged out by Julius Placidus, a tribune of a cohort. His hands were tied behind his back. With his clothes torn, a disgusting sight, he was led along, with many jeering and no one weeping, since the ugliness of his end had taken away pity. A German soldier met them and struck out with a hostile blow. Whether he was aiming at Vitellius, in anger or to free him sooner from the mockery, or at the tribune, was never settled. He cut off the tribune's ear and was killed on the spot.
3:85 Vitellius was forced by hostile sword points sometimes to lift his face and offer it to insults, sometimes to watch his own statues falling, and mostly to look at the Rostra or at the spot where Galba was killed. At last they drove him to the Gemoniae, where the body of Flavius Sabinus had lain. One remark showing a spirit not unworthy of him was heard, when he answered a tribune who was insulting him, "Yet I was your emperor." Then he fell under the wounds heaped on him. And the crowd abused him dead with the same wickedness with which it had favored him alive.
## What kind of man Vitellius was; Domitian hailed as Caesar
3:86 Luceria was his native place. He was completing his 57th year. His consulship, priesthoods, name and place among the leading men he got by no effort of his own, but entirely through his father's fame. The throne was offered to him by men who didn't know him. The army's affection has rarely come to anyone through good qualities as it did to him through his laziness. Still, he had frankness and generosity, which turn to ruin unless there is moderation. He believed friendships are held by the size of gifts, not by steady character, and he deserved friendship more than he had it. It certainly served the state that Vitellius be beaten, but those who betrayed Vitellius to Vespasian can't count their treachery as a credit, since they had deserted Galba. With the day sinking toward evening, the Senate couldn't be called, because of the fear of the magistrates and senators, who had slipped out of the city or were hiding in the houses of their clients. When nothing hostile was feared any more, Domitian came forward to the leaders of the party and was hailed as Caesar, and the soldiers in large numbers, armed as they were, escorted him to his father's house.
