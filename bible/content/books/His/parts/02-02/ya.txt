2:1 Far away, fortune was already lining up a new line of emperors. Its twists would bring the state joy or disaster, and the emperors themselves success or ruin. Galba was still alive and well when Titus Vespasian left Judea. His father had sent him. Titus gave two reasons for the trip: his duty to the emperor, and the fact that he was old enough now to go after public jobs. But people love making things up. Word spread that he'd been summoned to Rome to be adopted. Why believe it? Galba was old and had no kids, and that was plenty to gossip about. So was the custom of naming a pile of possible heirs until one man finally gets picked. Titus himself was like fresh wood on that fire. He had talent for any destiny, and a handsome face with real dignity. Then came his father's rising luck. The prophecies pointing his way. Even random events that believers read as signs. In Corinth, the main city of Achaia, he got solid news that Galba was dead. Men there swore that Vitellius had taken up arms and war had started. Titus got anxious. He called in a few friends and turned his situation over from both sides. "If I go on to Rome," he said, "nobody will thank me for a favor that honors somebody else. I'd be a hostage for Vitellius or Otho. If I turn back, the winner will definitely hold it against me. But nothing is settled yet. My father is joining one side, so a son gets a pass. And if Vespasian takes charge of the state, men with a war on their minds will have to forget old grudges." Neat reasoning. Now he had to bet on it.

2:2 Thoughts like these tossed him between hope and fear. Hope won. Some believed he turned back because he longed for Queen Berenice. He wasn't cold toward her. But it never got in the way of business. He spent his youth having a good time. Once he was emperor, though, he showed more self-control than he had during his father's reign. Now to the voyage. He hugged the coast of Achaia and Asia, with land on his left the whole way. He passed the islands of Rhodes and Cyprus. Then he struck out across open water for Syria. Along the way, a strong craving hit him to see the temple of Venus at Paphos. Locals and outsiders alike knew it. Time for a quick history lesson, and I won't drag it out. Here's how the worship began, how the rites go, and what the goddess looks like. Nowhere else is she shown that way. Wait until you see her.

2:3 First, the temple's history. Old tradition says King Aerias founded it, though some say that's the name of the goddess herself. A newer story says Cinyras made the temple holy. The goddess, who began life in the sea, drifted ashore at this very spot. But the skill of reading entrails (the guts of sacrificed animals, read for signs) came from abroad, they say. Tamiras of Cilicia brought it. The two families agreed that their descendants would run the rites together. Later, the royal family didn't want to be left without some edge over the foreign line. So the newcomers gave up the very skill they had brought. Now only a priest from Cinyras's line is consulted. Worshippers bring whatever animals they've vowed. But they choose males, and the surest signs come from the entrails of young goats. Pouring blood on the altar is forbidden. It gets only prayers and clean fire. It stands out in the open air, and rain never wets it. Picture that. The goddess isn't shown as a human. Her image is a rounded mass, broad at the bottom and narrow at the top, like a cone. Why? Nobody knows.

2:4 Titus took in the wealth first, and the gifts of kings. Then came everything else the Greeks, who love antiques, pin on some uncertain past. He asked the goddess about his voyage. Word came back: the road was open and the sea was friendly. So he sacrificed a number of animals and asked about his own future, in roundabout words. The priest, Sostratus, studied the entrails. The signs were good. They matched what Titus hoped, and the goddess was nodding yes to some great undertaking. For the moment, Sostratus kept his answer short and ordinary. Then he asked for a private meeting and spelled out the future. Titus walked out with his spirits lifted and headed for his father. He arrived like a huge promise of success for provinces and armies that were still wobbling. Vespasian, meanwhile, had nearly won the Jewish war. Only the siege of Jerusalem was left. Why so hard? Blame the mountain stronghold and the Jews' stubborn religion. It was steep, grinding work, and not because the people inside had strength left to hold out through hardship. As I said earlier, Vespasian had three legions (the Roman army's big units) that had seen real war. Mucianus had four, in peacetime. Still, rivalry and the glory of the neighboring army had driven out laziness. Danger and hard work had toughened Vespasian's men. Mucianus's army got the same energy from long calm and no hardships of war. Two roads, one result. Each army had support troops, cavalry, fleets and allied kings. Both had a famous name. The reasons differed.

2:5 Vespasian was a soldier through and through. He led the column from the front. He picked the campsite. Day and night he fought the enemy with clever plans, or with his own hands if it came to that. He ate whatever was lying around. His clothes and looks barely set him apart from a regular soldier. Honestly? Ignore his greed, and he matched the great generals of the old days. Mucianus was the opposite. He stood out like a spotlight, with his splendor, his wealth and a lifestyle far beyond what a private citizen could afford. But he was smoother with words, and a pro at planning how the government ran. Put these two together and you'd have a killer team for running things. That is, if you could strip away each man's faults and keep only the good. Sadly, Mucianus ran Syria and Vespasian ran Judea. Jealousy over their neighboring provinces kept them at odds. Then Nero fell. Only then did they drop their grudges and start planning together. At first they worked through friends. Then Titus stepped in as the big promise that held their deal together. He ended their ugly fighting by showing them what they both stood to gain. By nature and training, he was just the man for the job, even with Mucianus. (Yes, even Mucianus.) The tribunes (officers), the centurions and the regular soldiers were won over too. Each man got whatever fit him: an appeal to his drive or his love of doing as he pleased, to his virtues or his vices. Whatever worked.

2:6 The oath was already sworn. Both armies had pledged loyalty to Otho before Titus got there. News travels fast, but a civil war is a huge, slow beast, and it dragged things out. The East had been calm for ages. Now, for the first time, it was gearing up for war with itself. Here's the odd part. The nastiest civil wars between citizens had always started in Italy or Gaul, where the West had its power. Pompey, Cassius, Brutus and Antony each had civil war chase them across the sea. It went badly for every one of them. Out in Syria and Judea, the emperors were more like rumors than real people. People heard about them far more than they saw them. The legions had never mutinied. They had only made threats against the Parthians, with mixed results. In the last civil war, other provinces were rocked, but peace held here, and loyalty went to Galba. Then word got around that Otho and Vitellius were racing to grab the Roman world with wicked weapons. The soldiers started grumbling and sizing up their own strength. Why should others walk off with the prizes of power while they got nothing but orders? Fair question. Seven legions sat close by in Syria and Judea, with huge numbers of auxiliaries (support troops who were not citizen soldiers). Egypt was right next door, with two legions. On the other side lay Cappadocia, Pontus, and every fort along the Armenian frontier. Asia and the other provinces were full of men and loaded with money. Add every island of the Mediterranean. And the sea itself was handy and safe for getting ready. The war had everything it needed.

2:7 So who held back? The generals. They knew how itchy their troops were for a fight, but they waited anyway and let others fight it out. Their reasoning was simple: in a civil war, winners and losers never really trust each other. And it hardly mattered whether luck left Otho or Vitellius standing. Even the best generals get cocky after a win. The soldiers' messy discipline, laziness and love of luxury, plus the flaws of the two men themselves, would wreck one through war and the other through winning. Cheerful thought. So the generals stalled until the right moment. Vespasian and Mucianus had decided only recently, while the rest had decided long before. The best men wanted to save the state, plenty of others wanted loot, and some were in money trouble back home. Good men and bad, for different reasons, all wanted the same thing. War.

2:8 Then came a scare in Achaia and Asia. A report said Nero was on his way, and it was false. Stories about how he died kept changing, so more people claimed he was still alive, and more people believed it. I'll cover the other pretenders in their place in this work. More on them later. This one, the story goes, was a slave from Pontus. Others said he was a freedman from Italy, meaning a man who had been a slave and was later set free. He was good at the harp and at singing. Add a face that looked a lot like Nero's, and the claim got easier to swallow. He gathered deserters and drifters, men desperate because they were broke, and bribed them with huge promises. Then he set sail. Storms blew him onto the island of Cythnus. There he signed up some soldiers heading home from the East. The ones who refused? He had them killed. He also robbed merchants and armed their strongest slaves. The centurion Sisenna was carrying clasped right hands, the sign of friendship, from the Syrian army to the Praetorian Guard, the emperor's own bodyguard. The pretender tried every trick on him. Sisenna, afraid of violence, slipped off the island in secret. From there the alarm spread everywhere. The famous name stirred up people who were hungry for change and fed up with how things stood. The rumor grew every day. Then luck stepped in.

2:9 Now the hunt. Galba had put Calpurnius Asprenas in charge of Galatia and Pamphylia. He got two warships from the fleet at Misenum to track down the pretender. With them he reached Cythnus. Some people there even turned up to summon the ship captains in Nero's name. Bold. The pretender put on a sad face. He appealed to his old soldiers' loyalty and begged the captains to drop him off in Syria or Egypt. The captains hesitated, or maybe they were pulling a trick. They said they had to talk to their men and would come back once everyone was ready. Asprenas, though, got a faithful report of every word. At his urging, the ship was boarded and taken. The man, whoever he really was, was killed. His body, with its striking eyes and hair and its fierce face, went to Asia and then on to Rome. Case closed.

2:10 Even small things could start huge fights now. The state was in chaos. After so many changes of ruler, it swung between freedom and anything-goes license. Vibius Crispus had money, clout and a sharp mind. That made him famous, not good. He wanted Annius Faustus hauled in front of the Senate. Faustus was a Roman Knight, a rich man just below the senators, and he had worked as an informer under Nero. Under Galba, the senators had recently voted that accusers' cases should be looked into. The decree was applied unevenly. It was weak against a powerful defendant and strong against a helpless one, but it still carried some fear. Crispus, meanwhile, was throwing all his influence into destroying the man who had informed on his brother. He had talked a big chunk of the Senate into demanding that Faustus be handed over to be destroyed. No defense. No hearing. Others pushed the other way. In their view, nothing helped the accused more than the accuser's enormous power. Give him time, they said. Tell him the charges. Hear him out, however hated and guilty he was, because precedent demanded it. At first they won, and the hearing was put off for a few days. Before long, though, Faustus was convicted. Even so, the people gave him far less approval than his rotten character should have earned. They remembered that Crispus had made the same kind of accusations for a reward. What they hated was not the punishment. It was the man getting revenge.

2:11 Otho's war started well. He had ordered the armies in Dalmatia and Pannonia to march, and four legions were on the move. Each one sent 2,000 men on ahead. The main bodies trailed behind at a moderate distance. Galba had raised the 7th. The 11th and 13th were veterans. The 14th was famous above all for crushing the revolt in Britain. Nero had made it even more famous by naming it among his best troops. That gave these men a long loyalty to Nero. Now they were eager to back Otho. But the more they trusted their own strength, the slower they moved. Auxiliaries (troops who were not legions), cavalry and infantry both, ran out in front of the column. Rome sent a force too. It was nothing to laugh at: five Praetorian cohorts (the emperor's own guard units), some cavalry squads, and the 1st legion. Then came a shameful kind of help. Gladiators. Two thousand of them. Even strict commanders had used gladiators in civil wars, but that doesn't make it better. Annius Gallus took command. He went ahead with Vestricius Spurinna to seize the banks of the Padus. The first plans had failed, you see. Caecina had already crossed the Alps, and they had hoped to keep him inside Gaul. Otho himself marched with the army. With him came picked guards, the rest of the Praetorian cohorts, veterans from the Praetorian camp, and a huge crowd of men pulled from the fleet. Was his march lazy? Was it soft? No. He wore an iron breastplate and walked on foot in front of the standards. Rough, messy, nothing like his reputation.

2:12 Luck seemed to be on Otho's side. His fleets ruled the sea, so he held most of Italy, all the way to the start of the Maritime Alps. Three generals were sent to attack those mountains and then push on into the province of Gallia Narbonensis: Suedius Clemens, Antonius Novellus, and Aemilius Pacensis. Not a promising crew. Pacensis was in chains, put there by his own mutinous soldiers. Antonius Novellus had no real authority. Suedius Clemens chased popularity instead of commanding. He was as quick to wreck good discipline as he was to crave a fight. Marching through their own country, past its fields and homes? You'd never know it. They burned, wrecked, and looted as if the whole land were enemy territory. Nobody had prepared for the danger, which made it crueler. The fields were full of wealth. The houses stood wide open. Owners came out to greet the soldiers with their wives and children. Safe in peacetime one moment, surrounded by war the next. Marius Maturus was then running the Maritime Alps as a procurator (an official who ran money and property for the emperor). He called up the tribes, who never run out of young men, and set out to keep Otho's men away from his province's borders. It went nowhere. At the first charge, the mountain people were cut down and scattered. They had been gathered in a rush, and they knew neither camp nor commander. No honor in winning. No disgrace in running.

2:13 That battle left Otho's soldiers angry, and the town of Albintemilium paid for it. What had they won in the field? No loot. Their opponents were poor country people with cheap weapons. They couldn't take prisoners either, because those people were fast and knew the land. So the soldiers fed their greed on innocent people instead. Then a Ligurian woman showed amazing courage, and the soldiers' crime looked even uglier. She had hidden her son. The soldiers figured money was hidden with him, so they tortured her to find out where he was. She pointed to her womb. He was hiding there, she said. Threats came next. Then even death. Nothing could shake that brave answer. She never budged.

2:14 Fabius Valens got the news from frightened messengers. Otho's fleet was threatening Gallia Narbonensis, a province sworn to Vitellius. Envoys from the colonies were already there, begging for help. He sent it: two cohorts of Tungri, four squadrons of cavalry, and the whole cavalry wing of the Treveri, led by the prefect Julius Classicus. Some of these troops stayed behind in the colony of Forum Julii. The reason was simple. If everything went by land, the enemy fleet could rush up to an empty sea. Twelve squadrons of cavalry and the best men from the cohorts went after the enemy. A Ligurian cohort, an old local auxiliary force, joined them, along with 500 Pannonians who had not yet been signed up under standards. The fight came fast. Here's how the enemy had lined up. Some sailors, mixed in with local country people, stood on the hills near the sea. Praetorian soldiers filled the flat ground between the hills and the shore. Out on the water lay the fleet, side by side, turned and spread out in a threatening front. Vitellius's men were weak on foot but strong on horseback. So they put the Alpine tribesmen on the nearest ridges and their infantry in tight rows behind the cavalry. Then the Treveri squadrons charged. Carelessly. Veteran soldiers met them head on. At the same time, country people who were great throwers pelted the Treveri from the side with stones. Those people were mixed in among the soldiers. Some were brave, some were cowards, but winning made them all bold. Then the fleet swung around behind the fighters. Fresh terror hit the beaten men. They were trapped on every side. Their whole force would have been wiped out if the dark of night had not stopped the winners and hidden the runaways. Night saved them.

2:15 Beaten, but not finished. Vitellius's men called up reinforcements and went after an enemy who felt safe and was careless after winning. The guards on watch were cut down. They broke into the camp, and panic spread to the ships. Then, as the fear slowly settled, the Vitellians held a nearby hill they had already taken, and soon broke out again. The slaughter was savage. The prefects of the Tungri cohorts held their line for a long time. Then a storm of missiles overwhelmed them. Did Otho's men walk away clean? No. The enemy cavalry wheeled around and cut off some of the men who had chased them recklessly. Then, as if a truce had been agreed, both sides pulled back. Neither wanted the cavalry on one side or the fleet on the other to cause a sudden panic. Vitellius's men went to Antipolis, a town in Gallia Narbonensis. Otho's men went to Albingaunum in inland Liguria. A truce, more or less.

2:16 The fame of Otho's winning fleet kept Corsica, Sardinia, and the other nearby islands on his side. Corsica nearly got ruined anyway. The reckless procurator Decumus Pacarius (the official who ran money and property for the emperor) almost wrecked the island. In a war this huge, his plan could do nothing for the whole side. It would only ruin him. Hating Otho, he decided to back Vitellius with the Corsicans' strength. Would that help have mattered, even if it worked? No. He gathered the island's top men and explained his plan. Two men spoke against it: Claudius Pyrrhicus, captain of the Liburnian ships stationed there, and Quintius Certus, a Roman Knight. Pacarius ordered them killed. Their deaths terrified everyone present. The ignorant crowd caught the fear too, the way crowds always take on other people's fears. They all swore loyalty to Vitellius. Then Pacarius started drafting men and wearing out the untrained islanders with military duties. They hated the unfamiliar work, and they began to think about how weak they were. "We live on an island," they said. "Germany and its legions are far away. Even places guarded by infantry and cavalry have been raided and wrecked by a fleet." Their feelings flipped fast. They didn't use open force. They waited for a good moment to set an ambush. The men who usually surrounded Pacarius had left. He stood in the bath, naked and helpless, with nobody to help him. They killed him there. His companions were butchered too. The killers carried the heads to Otho, as the heads of public enemies. But the killers got lost in the general chaos, among so many bigger crimes. Otho didn't reward the killers. Vitellius didn't punish them.

2:17 Silius's cavalry had already cracked the border open and, as I said earlier, carried the war into Italy. Nobody loved Otho. But that didn't mean anyone wanted Vitellius. Years of peace had worn the people down until they would accept any master at all, and they would bow to whoever showed up. Which side was better? They didn't care. Meanwhile the Vitellianists (Vitellius's men) held the richest part of Italy: every plain and city between the Padus and the Alps. Caecina's advance cohorts had arrived too, and near Cremona a Pannonian cohort was captured. Between Placentia and Ticinum, a hundred cavalry and a thousand fleet men were cut off. Trapped. After those wins, nothing could hold the Vitellianists back. Not the river. Not its banks. Even the sight of the Padus riled up the Batavians and the men from across the Rhine. They crossed it all at once, opposite Placentia, and grabbed some scouts. The rest of the scouts panicked. Caecina's whole army was right there, they reported. It wasn't.

2:18 Spurinna held Placentia, and he was sure Caecina hadn't shown up yet. If Caecina did come, the plan was simple: stay behind the walls. Why throw three Praetorian cohorts, a thousand men from the detachments and a few cavalry at a veteran army? He wouldn't. But his soldiers were wild, and they knew nothing about war. They grabbed their standards and flags and charged out. Their commander tried to hold them back, and they aimed their weapons at him. They shoved aside the centurions and tribunes. They even yelled that Otho had been betrayed and that Caecina had been let in. Picture it. Spurinna went along with the soldiers' recklessness. At first he had no choice. Then he acted as if he wanted it too, so his advice would count for more once the uproar died down. Give in now, get heard later.

2:19 Night was closing in when the Padus came into view. So they decided to build a fortified camp. City soldiers weren't used to that kind of work, and it crushed their spirits. Then the oldest men started scolding themselves for being so gullible. Think of the danger, they said. What if Caecina and his army surrounded such a small force out on open plains? Big trouble. Calmer talk soon ran through the whole camp. The centurions and tribunes joined in. They praised the general's foresight: he had picked a colony with plenty of people and supplies as his base for the war. Finally Spurinna himself led everyone back to Placentia. Only the scouts stayed behind. He wasn't blaming the men for the mistake so much as showing them why it was one. Now the soldiers were less rowdy and took orders better. The walls got stronger, battlements went up, and the towers rose higher. They were preparing for more than weapons. They needed discipline and a love of obeying orders. That was the one thing that side lacked. Courage was never the problem.

2:20 Caecina marched through Italy with his army in good order. It was as if he had left his cruelty and wild living behind the Alps. The towns and colonies still read his clothes as arrogance. He spoke to men in togas (the Roman robe) while wearing a many-colored cloak and trousers, a foreign outfit. They took his wife Salonina's behavior as a personal insult too. She hurt nobody. She rode through on horseback in a purple outfit that made her stand out. They were offended anyway, as if she had hurt them. Why? Human nature. People stare hard at someone else's sudden good luck. And they expect the most moderation from those they once saw as equals. Caecina crossed the Padus. He tested the Othonianists' loyalty with talks and promises, and they tested his the same way. Peace and harmony: big, fine-sounding words, tossed around and worth nothing. So he turned all his planning to attacking Placentia, with a great show of terror. A good start, he knew, and his reputation would carry the rest.

2:21 The first day was all rush and no skill. It looked nothing like a veteran army. Men stuffed with food and wine came up to the walls. Out in the open. Careless. In that fight, the amphitheater burned down, a gorgeous building just outside the walls. Who lit it? Maybe the attackers, as they flung torches, sling bullets and flaming missiles at the defenders. Or maybe the defenders, flinging them back. The townspeople were the suspicious type, and they had a third idea. Outsiders from neighboring colonies had sneaked in fuel, they thought, out of jealousy and rivalry, since no building in Italy was that huge. Whatever the cause, they had been afraid of something far worse. So at first they shrugged it off. Once they felt safe again, they mourned as if nothing worse could have happened to them. Caecina was driven back, losing plenty of his men, and the night went to getting the siege works ready. The Vitellianists readied screens, hurdles and sheds to protect the men digging under the walls. The Othonianists readied stakes and massive loads of stone, lead and bronze, meant to smash and bury the enemy. Shame over failing and hope of glory drove both sides. Their pep talks were different, though. One side bragged about the legions and the German army. The other bragged about the city soldiers and the Praetorian cohorts. The first side sneered at their enemies as lazy, spoiled slackers raised on the circus (the big arena for races and games) and the theaters. The others called them foreigners and outsiders. All the while, they cheered or cursed Otho and Vitellius. Insults outnumbered praise.

2:22 Dawn. The walls were jammed with defenders, and the fields sparkled with armed men. The legions came on in a thick column, while auxiliaries, in small scattered bands, pelted the high parts of the walls with arrows and rocks. They went in close on the weak spots, where the walls were old or crumbling. From above, the Othonianists threw javelins with steadier, surer aim at the German cohorts. The Germans were charging in without thinking. Shields shaking over their shoulders, bare-chested, they yelled a savage chant in their native way. The legionaries, hidden under screens and hurdles, dug under the walls, built a ramp and went after the gates. The Praetorians had huge millstones ready for exactly this moment. They rolled them down with a tremendous crash. Some of the men climbing up were buried under them. Others were speared through and left lifeless. Others were torn apart. Panic made the slaughter worse, and men were hit even harder from the walls, so they fell back, their reputation in pieces. Caecina was ashamed of an attack he had started so rashly. Camp right there as a laughingstock with nothing to show? He refused. He crossed the Padus again and headed for Cremona. As he left, Turullius Cerialis surrendered to him with a number of fleet men. So did Julius Briganticus, with a few cavalrymen. Briganticus was a cavalry prefect, born among the Batavians. Turullius was a top-ranking centurion, friendly to Caecina. He had commanded a company in Germany.

2:23 Spurinna worked out where the enemy was heading. He wrote to Annius Gallus: Placentia had held, here is what happened, and here is what Caecina is planning. Gallus was marching the 1st legion to help Placentia, but could a few cohorts (groups of soldiers) outlast a long siege by the German army? He doubted it. Then word came that Caecina had been beaten and was running for Cremona. Gallus stopped at Bedriacum. His men were itching to fight. He could barely hold them back. Their hunger for battle had nearly turned into mutiny. Bedriacum is a village between Verona and Cremona, and two great disasters for Rome would later make it infamous. Around then, Martius Macer won a fight near Cremona. Martius was bold. He loaded his gladiators into boats, ferried them over the Padus (the river) and dropped them on the far bank without warning. The Vitellianist auxiliaries there fell into chaos. Those who stood firm were cut down, and the rest ran for Cremona. But the winners' charge was called off, for fear fresh enemy troops would turn the whole battle around. The Othonianists did not buy that. They read the worst into everything their generals did, and the more cowardly the man, the louder his mouth. They raced to pile charges on Annius Gallus, Suetonius Paullinus and Marius Celsus (Otho had put those two in command as well). The worst troublemakers were the men who had murdered Galba. Guilt and fear had driven them wild, and they threw everything into chaos. Some shouted angry, mutinous talk out in the open. Others wrote secret letters to Otho. Otho trusted the lowest men around him and feared the good ones, so he was always on edge. When things went well, he lost his nerve. When they went badly, he held up better. Backwards, right? In the end he sent for his brother Titianus and handed him the war.

2:24 Meanwhile, Paullinus and Celsus were on a roll. Caecina was miserable. Nothing he tried worked, and his army's fame was fading fast. Placentia had driven him off. His auxiliaries had just been cut down. Even in the scout skirmishes, which were frequent but not worth telling, he kept coming off worse. Then Valens drew closer. Caecina feared all the glory of the war would go to him. So he rushed to repair his name, more eagerly than wisely. Twelve miles from Cremona, at a place called the Castors, he hid his fiercest auxiliaries in the woods that hang over the road. The cavalry got their orders: ride far ahead, start a fight, then retreat on purpose. The enemy would chase them in a hurry, and then the hidden men would strike all at once. A clever trap. The Othonianist generals heard about it. Paullinus took command of the infantry, and Celsus took the cavalry. On the left stood a detachment of the 13th legion, four auxiliary cohorts and 500 horsemen. Three Praetorian cohorts (the emperor's elite guard troops) held the raised road in deep ranks. On the right front marched the 1st legion, with two auxiliary cohorts and 500 cavalry. Behind them came a thousand more horsemen, Praetorian and auxiliary. They would add weight to a win or back up anyone in trouble.

2:25 The Vitellianists turned their backs before the lines even clashed, as if they were running away. Celsus knew a trick when he saw one. He held his men in place. The Vitellianists broke ranks and charged, careless. Celsus gave ground slowly, and they chased him far too long. Straight into the trap. Auxiliary cohorts hit them on the flanks, the legions met them head on, and the cavalry swept around and shut the circle behind them. Suetonius Paullinus did not signal the infantry to attack right away. He was slow by nature. He liked careful, reasoned plans better than lucky breaks, so he had the ditches filled in, the field cleared and the line stretched out. He figured there was time enough to start winning once he knew they could not lose. That delay gave the Vitellianists a way out. They slipped into the vineyards, where the tied-together vines made it hard to move. A small wood lay next to them. From there they found their nerve again. They came back out. They killed the boldest riders of Otho's Praetorian cavalry. King Epiphanes, who was loyally pushing the troops on for Otho, was wounded. Not a good day for the slow plan.

2:26 Then the Othonianist infantry charged. The enemy line was crushed. Even the men rushing up to help were sent running. Caecina had not sent his cohorts in together but one at a time, and that made the panic worse. Scattered men were weak everywhere, and the fear of the runners swept them away. Back in Caecina's camp, a mutiny broke out, because the whole army had not been led out. The camp prefect (the officer in charge of the camp), Julius Gratus, was put in chains. The charge: he was plotting treason for his brother, who served with Otho. The Othonianists had already chained that brother, the tribune Julius Fronto, on the same charge. Fear was everywhere. It gripped the fugitives and the men rushing to help, the battle line and the rampart. Both sides said the same thing afterward. Caecina and his whole army could have been wiped out. Only one thing stopped it. Suetonius Paullinus sounded the recall. His reason, he said, was fear of so much more work and a longer march. Fresh Vitellianist soldiers might come out of their camp and hit his tired men. His men would be in confusion. No reserve would stand behind them. A few people agreed with the general. Most of the army took it badly.

2:27 A beating can do odd things to an army. This one scared the Vitellianists, Vitellius's side, less than it made them obedient. Caecina's troops were like that, though Caecina blamed his own soldiers and said they were better at mutiny than at fighting. Fabius Valens's men had reached Ticinum by now, and they were the same. The contempt for the enemy was gone. They wanted their honor back. They also took orders from their general more steadily. But they'd had a serious mutiny. It doesn't fit into Caecina's campaign, and I shouldn't have broken in on it, so I'll rewind. Back in Nero's war, the Batavian cohorts (groups of soldiers) had split off from the 14th legion. They were on their way to Britain. Then they heard about Vitellius's rising and teamed up with Fabius Valens in the country of the Lingones. And they swaggered. Talk about rude. Whenever they walked into a legion's tents, they bragged. They had kept the men of the 14th in line. They had taken Italy from Nero. The whole fortune of the war, they said, was in their hands. The other soldiers felt insulted, and their general felt bitter. Arguments and fistfights wrecked discipline. In the end, Valens began to fear that all this rudeness would turn into betrayal.

2:28 Then bad news hit Valens. Otho's fleet had beaten the Treveri and Tungri cavalry, and Gallia Narbonensis was being surrounded. He saw a way to do two things at once. He could protect an allied people. And he could use a soldier's cunning to break up the troublesome cohorts, which were too strong when they stayed together. So he sent part of the Batavians to the rescue. Word got around fast. Of course it did. The allies were upset, and the legions grumbled. Their complaint was blunt: "They're robbing us of our bravest men. These veterans have won so many wars. Now they're pulled off the battle line, just as the enemy comes into view! If a province matters more than the city and the empire's safety, fine, we should all follow them there. But what if victory's health, its support and pillar, rests in Italy? Then you can't rip the strongest limbs off the body."

2:29 The shouting didn't stop. Valens sent in his lictors to put down the mutiny, and the mob went after him personally. They threw stones and chased him as he ran. They yelled that he was hiding the loot from the Gauls and the gold of the people of Vienna, pay for their own hard work. Then they tore his baggage apart. They jabbed javelins and lances into the general's tent and the very ground. Valens was hiding in a cavalry officer's quarters, dressed as a slave. Picture it. That's when the camp prefect, Alfenus Varus, saw the mutiny dying down bit by bit and added a plan. He forbade the centurions to make their rounds of the sentries. He stopped the trumpet calls that summon soldiers to their duties. Everyone froze. They stared at each other, scared by one fact: nobody was in command. Nobody. First came silence, then patience, and finally prayers and tears. They begged for pardon. And when Valens came out, wretched, weeping, and saved when nobody expected it, the mood flipped. Joy, pity and even affection swept through them. Crowds go to extremes in either direction, and this one swung from rage to delight. They cheered him and congratulated him. They carried him to the platform with the eagles and standards around him. Valens showed useful self-control and demanded punishment for no one. But pretending nothing had happened would look more suspicious, so he rebuked a few men. He knew that in civil wars soldiers get more freedom than generals.

2:30 A second scare was coming. Valens's men were fortifying a camp at Ticinum when news arrived of Caecina's setback. The mutiny nearly started again. The soldiers thought Valens's trickery and delays had made them miss the battle. They wanted no rest and wouldn't wait for their general. They marched out ahead of the standards and shoved the standard-bearers along. At a rapid march, they joined Caecina. Valens had a bad name with Caecina's army. Those men complained that they'd been left facing the enemy's full strength while so much fewer. An excuse, sure. It was also flattery: they played up the newcomers' strength so those troops wouldn't look down on them as beaten cowards. Valens had more men, nearly twice the number of legions and auxiliaries. Did that win the soldiers over? No. Their sympathies still leaned toward Caecina. People thought he was quicker to be generous. He was younger and tall, and they liked him in a rather empty way. So the two commanders were rivals. Caecina mocked Valens as filthy and disgraceful. Valens mocked Caecina as puffed up and vain. Still, they buried their hatred and chased the same advantage. Letter after letter piled insults on Otho, with no thought of forgiveness. Otho's generals had plenty of dirt on Vitellius, but they held back.

2:31 Here's how the two men looked before they died. People feared Vitellius's lazy comforts less than Otho's burning passions. (Otho won great honor by his death. Vitellius won the foulest disgrace by his.) Galba's murder had made Otho feared and hated. Nobody held it against Vitellius that he'd begun the war. Vitellius shamed himself with his belly and his gluttony. Otho's extravagance, cruelty and recklessness made people think he was more dangerous to the state. Pick your poison. Once Caecina's and Valens's forces joined, the Vitellianists had no reason left to put off a battle with their whole force. Otho held a discussion: drag the war out, or try his luck? Then Suetonius Paullinus spoke up. No one at that time was considered a more skilled soldier, and he thought his reputation called for him to give an opinion on the whole way to run the war. His argument was simple. Haste helped the enemy. Delay helped Otho's side.

2:32 "Vitellius's whole army is here," he said. "Why rush it? Not much strength stands behind it. Gaul is ready to rise, and Vitellius can't walk away from the Rhine bank while such hostile tribes wait to pour in. Britain's army is stuck behind an unfriendly people and the sea. Spain has no troops to spare. Gallia Narbonensis got shaken by our fleet's attack and by a defeat. As for Italy north of the Padus, the Alps wall it in and no ship can supply it. The marching army has stripped it bare. Nobody can find grain for that army anywhere, and an army can't hold together without supplies. Hungry armies fall apart. Then there are the Germans, the fiercest part of the enemy's force. Drag the war into summer, and the new land and climate will weaken them until they break apart. Plenty of wars that were terrifying at first have faded away through weariness and delay. Our side is in a different spot. We have everything in abundance, and our people are loyal. Pannonia, Moesia, Dalmatia and the East still hold armies that haven't been touched. We hold Italy and Rome, the head of the world. We hold the Senate and the people, names that never go dim, even if they're sometimes overshadowed. We hold the wealth of the state and of private citizens, plus a huge supply of money. In civil strife, money is stronger than iron. Our soldiers are used to Italy, or to even greater heat. The river Padus lies in front of us. Behind us are cities with strong garrisons and walls, and none of them will yield to the enemy. Placentia's defense has already proved it. So let Otho drag the war out. In a few days the 14th legion, already famous, will arrive with the Moesian troops. Then he can think it over again. If he picks battle, we'll fight with more strength."

2:33 Marius Celsus sided with Paullinus. So did Annius Gallus. He had taken a bad fall from his horse a few days earlier, and the men sent to ask his opinion came back and said he agreed. Otho wanted a fight to the finish. His brother Titianus and the Praetorian prefect Proculus were green and impatient. They swore that luck, the gods and Otho's own spirit were backing their plans, and always would. They wanted nobody daring to argue, so they flattered him. Smooth. Once the fight was settled, a new question came up. Should the emperor be at the battle, or sit it out? Paullinus and Celsus stopped arguing. They didn't want to look like they were shoving him into danger. Then the same two men who had pushed the bad plan, Titianus and Proculus, talked him into pulling back to Brixellum. Out of harm's way. Saved for the big decisions and for ruling. Sounds sensible, right? That day was the first blow that crushed Otho's cause. A strong force went with him: Praetorian cohorts, his bodyguard and his cavalry. The men left behind lost heart, because they didn't trust their generals. Otho was the one man the soldiers trusted, and he trusted only them. Then he left. The generals were standing on shaky ground.

2:34 The Vitellianists knew everything. Civil wars are full of deserters, and the enemy's scouts asked about the other side's plans without hiding their own. Caecina and Valens sat tight and watched. The enemy was walking blindly into ruin. So why not wait for the mistakes? Other people's blunders were their idea of wisdom. Meanwhile they started a bridge across the Padus, acting as if they meant to cross right there. A line of gladiators was drawn up against them. The bridge also kept their own soldiers busy, so they wouldn't sit around idle. Here's how it worked. Boats were spaced evenly, tied together at both ends with strong beams, and pointed upstream. Anchors went down above them to hold the bridge still. The cables stayed slack, not tight, so the boats could rise with the river without wrecking the setup. A tower stood at the end, built out on the last boat. Engines and machines could fire from it and push the enemy back. The Othonianists built their own tower on the bank. They hurled stones and burning torches from it. Tower faced tower.

2:35 An island sat in the middle of the river. The gladiators struggled toward it in boats, and the Germans swam ahead of them. By chance, quite a few Germans had already crossed. Then Macer packed some light galleys with his boldest gladiators and sent them in. Bad idea. Gladiators don't have a soldier's steadiness in battle. Picture it. On rocking boats, they couldn't land their blows like men planted firmly on a riverbank. They swayed this way and that, scared, and the rowers and fighters got tangled up. Chaos. Then the Germans jumped into the shallows by themselves. They grabbed the sterns and climbed the sides, or they sank the boats with their hands. Both armies watched every second of it. The Vitellianists were delighted. The Othonianists cursed the disaster, and the man who caused it, harder with every cheer.

2:36 The survivors fled in whatever boats were left, and the fight was over. Then the soldiers demanded that Macer be executed. A spear thrown from a distance had already wounded him. Now they rushed him with drawn swords. Tribunes and centurions jumped in and saved him. Soon after, Vestricius Spurinna arrived with his cohorts, on Otho's orders. He left only a small garrison behind at Placentia. Then Otho sent Flavius Sabinus to take over the troops Macer had led. Sabinus was the consul-elect, a man already picked to be consul next. The soldiers were glad of the switch. And the leaders? They were disgusted. Sick of the constant mutinies, they wanted no part of such a hated command. Nobody wanted that job.

2:37 I read in some writers that the armies hesitated. Either they feared war, or they were sick of both emperors, whose shame and disgrace came out more openly every day. The armies wondered whether to drop the fight. They could talk it over among themselves. Or they could let the Senate pick an emperor. That's why, they say, Otho's generals urged waiting. Paullinus pushed hardest. He had hopes for himself. He was the oldest of the ex-consuls and famous as a soldier, and he had won glory and a name in his campaigns in Britain. Fair enough, I'll grant this much: a few people, praying in silence, wanted peace instead of strife. They wanted a good, decent emperor instead of the worst and most shameful ones. But would a crowd in such a rotten age really change? I don't think Paullinus, sensible as he was, expected it. People who had wrecked peace for love of war were not about to give up war for love of peace. I don't think armies this different in language and customs could ever agree on it. And the commanders and generals? Most of them knew their own extravagance, poverty and crimes. I doubt they would have put up with an emperor who wasn't stained like them. He had to be tied to them by favors they'd done. They wanted an emperor who owed them.

2:38 The craving for power is old and deep in people. It grew along with the empire, and then it burst out. When things were small, keeping everyone equal was easy. Then the whole world was conquered, and rival cities and kings were wiped out. Suddenly there was time to lust after wealth that was safe to enjoy. That's when the first fights broke out between senators and the people. Sometimes the tribunes stirred up trouble, and sometimes the consuls were too powerful. The very first tries at civil war happened inside the city, right in the Forum. Before long, Gaius Marius climbed up from the lowest of the people, and Lucius Sulla climbed up as the cruelest of the nobles. Their armies crushed freedom, and the two men turned it into a dictatorship. Then came Gnaeus Pompeius. He hid his character better. He was no better. After that, every fight was about one thing: supreme power. Citizen armies didn't put down their weapons even at Pharsalia and Philippi. So were the armies of Otho and Vitellius likely to end this war on their own? Hardly. The same anger of the gods drove them, the same madness of men, the same causes of crime. These wars were settled, so to speak, by single blows. The leaders' laziness was to blame. But thinking about the old ways and the new has carried me too far. Back to events, in order.

2:39 After Otho left for Brixellum, his brother Titianus got the title of commander. But the real force and power belonged to the prefect Proculus. Celsus and Paullinus were generals in name only. Nobody used their good judgment. They were empty names, there to cover for other people's mistakes. The tribunes and centurions were unsure. Why wouldn't they be? The better men had been pushed aside, and the worst ones had the influence. The soldiers were eager, but they'd rather second-guess their officers' orders than carry them out. They decided to move the camp up to the fourth milestone from Bedriacum, and they botched it. It was spring and rivers were everywhere, yet the men still ran short of water. Then they argued about whether to fight. Otho kept pressing them by letter to hurry, and the soldiers insisted the emperor be at the battle. Many wanted the troops across the Padus called up. Which choice would have been best? Not easy to say. What they did was the worst. That much is certain.

2:40 Off they went, as if for a whole campaign instead of one battle. The goal was the spot where the Padus and the Arda meet, sixteen miles away. Celsus and Paullinus objected. Why send men tired from the march and weighed down with baggage into the arms of an enemy who was sure to attack? Bad plan, they said. The enemy would be traveling light, with barely four miles behind him. He would catch the army strung out on the march, or scattered and busy digging a rampart. Titianus and Proculus lost the argument, so they fell back on their right to command. Rank won. And a Numidian really had galloped in with an angry message. Otho scolded the generals for being slow and ordered them to settle the matter now. He was sick of delay. He couldn't stand the wait.

2:41 Caecina was busy with his bridge that day. Then two tribunes from the Praetorian cohorts turned up and asked for a meeting. He was about to hear their terms and give his own when scouts burst in with word that the enemy was close. The talk ended right there. Was it a trick, a betrayal or an honest deal? Nobody ever found out. Caecina sent the tribunes away and rode back to camp. Fabius Valens had already given the signal for battle, and the soldiers stood armed. The legions were still drawing lots for their places in the march when the cavalry charged. Strange but true. A smaller Othonianist force nearly drove those riders back onto the rampart. Only the Italian legion's courage stopped it. The legion's men drew their swords and forced the beaten riders to turn and fight again. The Vitellianist legions lined up calmly, because thick scrub hid every weapon from view even though the enemy was near. The Othonianists had no such luck. Their generals were scared, and their soldiers hated their officers. Baggage wagons and camp servants were jammed into the ranks, like a crowd squeezing through one door. Steep ditches ran along both sides, and the road was too narrow even for a calm march. Some men huddled around their standards, and others hunted for theirs. People ran up shouting for each other, and the noise was pure chaos. Each man went where his nerve or his fear sent him. Some shoved to the front. Others slipped to the back.

2:42 Terror had stunned them. Then false joy made them go slack. Some men turned up with a lie: Vitellius's whole army had switched sides. Where did the rumor start? Maybe Vitellius's scouts spread it, or maybe it began on Otho's own side, by a trick or by pure luck. Nobody ever learned which. The Othonianists let their fighting spirit drop and greeted the enemy. The answer was a hostile roar. Many of Otho's men had no idea why anyone had called out a greeting, so now they feared betrayal. Then the enemy line crashed down on them, ranks solid, stronger and bigger. The Othonianists were scattered, outnumbered and worn out. They fought back hard anyway. Not a fair fight. The ground was a tangle of trees and vines, so the battle kept changing shape. Some men fought up close and some from far off, in bands and in wedges. On the raised road they stood foot to foot and shoved with their bodies and shield bosses. Javelins were no use now, so swords and axes hacked through helmets and breastplates. They knew each other, and everyone could see them. The whole war hung on this fight.

2:43 Out in an open field between the Padus and the road, two legions ran into each other. Vitellius had the 21st, called the Rapax, famous for its old glory. Otho had the 1st, called the Adjutrix, which had never fought a battle but was fierce and hungry for its first honor. Quite a meeting. The 1st cut down the front ranks of the 21st and grabbed their eagle. The 21st burned with grief. It hit back, drove the 1st off, killed the legate Orfidius Benignus and also seized a pile of flags and standards from the enemy. Elsewhere, the 13th fell back before a charge by the 5th. The 14th was surrounded by bigger numbers. Otho's generals had run off long before. Caecina and Valens kept feeding in reserves. Then fresh help arrived. Alfenus Varus came with his Batavians, who had scattered a band of gladiators who had been ferried across the river. The cohorts opposite had already cut those gladiators down right in the water. Flush with that win, the Batavians hit the enemy's flank.

2:44 The center broke. The Othonianists scattered in every direction toward Bedriacum. It was a huge distance, and heaps of bodies blocked the road. Why was the killing worse? Because prisoners taken in a civil war bring no profit. Suetonius Paullinus and Licinius Proculus took different roads and steered clear of the camp. Vedius Aquila, legate of the 13th legion, panicked and ran blindly into the angry soldiers. He reached the rampart while it was still daylight. Mutineers and runaways roared around him. Insults flew, and so did blows. They called him a deserter and a traitor. They had no real charge. Classic mob behavior: every man dumps his own guilt on someone else. Night gave Titianus and Celsus a break. By then the sentries were posted and the soldiers had calmed down. Annius Gallus had won them over with advice, pleading and the weight of his authority. Don't pile more disaster on top of defeat, he told them, by killing each other. War over or war on, the beaten had one comfort left: stand together. Most of the army had lost heart. The Praetorian soldiers growled that treachery had beaten them, not courage. "The Vitellianists didn't win for free," they said. "Their cavalry got beaten, and a legion lost its eagle. We still have the troops across the Padus, and Otho himself. The Moesian legions are coming. A large part of the army stayed behind at Bedriacum, and those men haven't been beaten. And if it has to happen, we'd die more honorably in battle." That was despair talking. It drove the grim and the scared forward. Most often it drove them to anger, not fear.

2:45 The Vitellianists camped at the fifth milestone from Bedriacum. Their generals didn't dare attack the enemy camp that day, hoping for a surrender instead. The army had no baggage and had come out only to fight, but weapons and victory were protection enough. By the next day, it was clear what Otho's army wanted. Even the angriest men were starting to regret the fight. Envoys went over, and Vitellius's generals agreed to peace at once. The envoys were held back for a short while, and everyone got nervous. Had they won or not? Nobody knew yet. Soon the envoys came back. The camp was thrown open. Winners and losers both wept. They cursed civil war with a miserable kind of joy. A victory that felt like a funeral. In the same tents, some men nursed wounded brothers and others nursed wounded relatives. Hopes and rewards hung in the air. Death and grief were certain. Nobody had come through so untouched that he had no one to mourn. Men searched for the body of the legate Orfidius and cremated it with the usual honors. A few others were buried by their own relatives. Everyone else was left lying on the ground.

2:46 Otho waited for news from the battle. He wasn't afraid, and he knew exactly what he wanted. Gloomy rumors came first, and then runaways from the fighting stumbled in and made it plain: everything was lost. The soldiers didn't wait for their emperor to speak. Keep your chin up, they told him. Fresh fighters were still left, and they swore to suffer the worst and dare anything. Was this flattery? No. They burned with a wild drive to charge back in and turn their side's luck around. Men far off stretched out their hands, and men close by clutched his knees. Picture it. Plotius Firmus pushed hardest of all. He commanded the Praetorian Guard, the emperor's own bodyguard. Again and again he begged Otho not to abandon such a loyal army, soldiers who had served him so well. "It takes more courage," he said, "to put up with bad luck than to run from it. Brave, determined people keep hoping even when fortune turns on them. Cowards and slackers are scared, so they rush into despair." Whenever Otho's face softened or hardened, the crowd cheered or groaned along with it. And it wasn't only the Praetorians, Otho's own soldiers. The men sent ahead from Moesia reported the same stubborn mood in the army on its way. The legions had entered Aquileia. So nobody can doubt it: the war could have flared up again. Brutal. Grim. Uncertain for winners and losers alike.

2:47 Otho himself wanted no more war. Not what they hoped to hear. "Ask you to risk your courage and spirit again?" he said. "I think that would cost me too much. My life isn't worth the price. Say I chose to live. The more hope you show me, the more beautiful my death will be. Fortune and I know each other by now, and don't bother counting how long. Good luck is hard to handle when you don't expect it to last. This civil war started with Vitellius. He was the first to make us fight over the throne with weapons. I'll fight only once, and I'll be the example. Let later generations judge Otho by that. As for Vitellius, he can enjoy his brother, his wife and his children. I need no payback and no comfort. Others have held power longer, but nobody has walked away from it so bravely. Am I really going to let so many young Romans and so many fine armies be cut down again and ripped from the state? I don't want to stand between you and your safety, and you shouldn't stand between me and my resolve. Saying too much about the end is partly cowardice. Want proof of my resolve? I blame nobody. Only someone who wants to live blames gods or men."

2:48 Then he went from man to man, speaking gently to each by age or rank. Get out quickly, he urged, and don't make the winner angry by hanging around. With the young he used his authority. With the old he pleaded. His face stayed calm and his words fearless, even as he fought back his friends' tears, which came at the worst time. Hard to watch. Boats and wagons? He ordered them for everyone leaving. The notes and letters that praised his loyalty or insulted Vitellius? He burned them. He handed out cash, but carefully, not like a man about to die. Soon he was comforting Salvius Cocceianus, his brother's son, a very young man who was scared and grieving. Otho praised the boy's loyalty and scolded his fear. "Do you really think Vitellius is so cold-hearted that he won't return this favor, now that his whole household is safe? By rushing my end, I earn the winner's mercy. I didn't give up the last throw of the dice for the state out of total despair. My army was demanding battle. I've won plenty of fame for myself and plenty of noble standing for my descendants. After the Julii, the Claudii and the Servii, I was the first to bring the throne into a new family. So face life with a brave heart. Never forget that Otho was your uncle. But don't dwell on it too much."

2:49 Then he sent everyone away and rested a little. He was going over his last worries when a sudden uproar broke in. The soldiers were panicking and out of control. They threatened death to anyone who tried to leave. Their worst violence was aimed at Verginius, whose house they had locked up and surrounded. Otho scolded the ringleaders. Then he went back and kept talking with the people leaving, until every one of them got out unharmed. Evening fell, and he drank cold water to quench his thirst. Two daggers were brought to him. He tested the edge of each and tucked one under his head. Once he knew his friends had set out, he spent a quiet night. It is even said he slept. Think about that. At first light he fell on the blade with his chest. At the dying man's groan, his freedmen (slaves he had freed) and his slaves rushed in. So did Plotius Firmus, prefect of the Praetorian Guard. They found one wound. The funeral was rushed. He had begged for that with earnest prayers, so his head wouldn't be cut off and made a mockery. The Praetorian cohorts carried his body with praise and tears. They kissed his wound and his hands, and some soldiers killed themselves beside the funeral pyre. Guilt? Fear? No. It was rivalry in honoring him, and love for their emperor. After that, this kind of death became common at Bedriacum, Placentia and the other camps, whatever the rank. A plain tomb was built for Otho. Plain, and likely to last.

2:50 So Otho's life ended in his 37th year. He came from the town of Ferentium. His father had been a consul (one of Rome's two top officials), and his grandfather a praetor. His mother's family was less distinguished, though not without honor. I have already shown what his childhood and youth were like. Two bold deeds shaped his name: one monstrous, one remarkably noble. Later generations gave him about as much good name as bad. Collecting marvels and entertaining readers with made-up tales? That would be beneath this work. Still, I wouldn't dare to wipe out what common report and tradition pass down. Here is what the locals tell. On the day of the fighting at Bedriacum, a bird of an unfamiliar kind landed in a busy grove near Regium Lepidum. Crowds of people gathered, and flocks of birds flew around it. Nothing scared it off or drove it away until Otho killed himself. Then it vanished from sight. Those who counted the times found that the start and the end of the wonder matched the end of Otho's life. Strange.

2:51 Otho's funeral set the soldiers off again. Grief and pain stirred their mutiny back up. Who could hold them back? Nobody. They turned to Verginius and begged him to take the throne, or else to go as their envoy to Caecina and Valens. They threatened him while they begged. Some request. Verginius slipped out through the back of his house, and by the time the soldiers burst in, he had given them the slip. Rubrius Gallus carried in the petition from the cohorts that had been at Brixellum, and the pardon came right away. The troops Flavius Sabinus had commanded gave in to the winner, with Sabinus as the go-between. The winner got them.

2:52 The war was over everywhere. For a big part of the Senate, the danger was the worst yet. They had left Rome with Otho, then been left behind at Mutina. The news of the lost battle reached them there. The soldiers wouldn't buy it. A fake rumor, they said. They figured the Senate was against Otho, so they hung on every word the senators said and read the worst into their faces. At last, with insults and abuse, they hunted for an excuse to start a massacre. The senators feared something else too. Vitellius's side was so strong now that they might look slow to welcome his victory. So they were scared both ways. They met together, but none of them had a clear plan. Each man felt safer hiding in everyone's shared guilt. Then the council of Mutina offered them weapons and money and called them Conscript Fathers. Great compliment. Terrible timing.

2:53 Then came a famous quarrel. Licinius Caecina went after Eprius Marcellus for speaking in a slippery way. The others kept their opinions to themselves. Why pick Marcellus? His name was hated, because people remembered his informing on others. That made him an easy target for ill will, and it riled Caecina up. Caecina was new, only just let into the Senate, and he wanted to make his name with big feuds. Calm heads among the better men broke up the fight. Everyone went back to Bononia to talk it over again, hoping more messengers would turn up by then. At Bononia, men were posted along the roads to question each newcomer. One of Otho's freedmen (a man Otho had freed from slavery) was asked why he had left. He said he carried his master's last orders. He said Otho was still alive when he left him. But all Otho cared about, he said, was how future generations would remember him. Otho had torn himself away from the pleasures of life. The senators were filled with admiration, and too ashamed to ask more. All eyes turned to Vitellius.

2:54 Lucius Vitellius, the brother of Vitellius, sat in on the meetings, letting the flatterers butter him up. Then panic. Caenus, a freedman of Nero, suddenly threw everyone into terror with a vicious lie. The 14th legion had shown up, he said. It had joined the forces from Brixellum and cut the winners down. The luck of the two sides had flipped. Why make it up? Otho's passports, which people had been ignoring, would count again with happier news. Some lie. Caenus was rushed off to Rome, and a few days later he paid the price on Vitellius's order. Meanwhile the senators were in more danger, because Otho's soldiers believed the story. Leaving Mutina made it scarier, since it looked like an official decision to give up the cause. They stopped meeting as a group. Every man looked out for himself, until letters from Fabius Valens arrived and took their fear away. The more people admired Otho's death, the faster the news flew.

2:55 Rome, though, had no alarm at all. The games of Ceres ran on as usual. Then reliable messengers came into the theater with the news that Otho was dead. Every soldier in the city had sworn loyalty to Vitellius, under Flavius Sabinus, the city prefect. The crowd cheered for Vitellius. They hauled Galba's images around the temples, dressed up in laurel and flowers. They heaped wreaths into a mound by the Lake of Curtius, right where Galba had bled out. In the Senate, every honor piled up under other emperors' long reigns got voted in at once. Praise and thanks went to the German army, and a delegation was sent to congratulate them. A letter from Fabius Valens to the consuls was read. It was not over the top. Still, the senators liked Caecina better. He was modest, so he never wrote at all. The best letter was no letter.

2:56 Italy, meanwhile, was suffering something worse than war. Vitellius's soldiers had spread out into the towns and colonies. They looted, grabbed, and wrecked every place with violence and lust. Greedy? You bet. They would sell out for anything, right or wrong. Nothing was off limits, sacred or not. There were even men who killed their own enemies in a soldier's disguise. The soldiers knew the region, so they picked out rich farms and wealthy owners to loot. Anyone who fought back was marked for death. Their commanders were in their hands and didn't dare to stop them. Caecina was less greedy, but hungrier for popularity. Valens had a bad name for his profits and gains, so he looked the other way when others committed crimes. Italy's wealth had been drained long ago. On top of that came a force of infantry and cavalry that big, with all its violence, losses and wrongs. Barely bearable.

2:57 Vitellius didn't know yet that he had won. He was still scraping together what was left of the German army, as if a big war lay ahead. A few old soldiers stayed behind in winter quarters, and new men were rushed through Gaul to fill the names of the legions left there. Hordeonius Flaccus got the job of guarding the Rhine bank. Vitellius added 8,000 picked men from the army of Britain to his own force. A few days into the march, the news caught up with him. His side had won at Bedriacum. Otho was dead. The war was over. Vitellius called an assembly and piled on praise for the soldiers' courage. Then the army made a demand: they wanted his freedman Asiaticus (a former slave he had set free) made a Roman Knight (a rank marked by gold rings). Vitellius shut down the shameful flattery in public. Private was another matter. At a dinner party, true to his fickle ways, he handed over what he had refused. He gave Asiaticus the gold rings. So much for his principles. Asiaticus was a foul slave, hungry for power by crooked means.

2:58 More news reached Vitellius around then. Both Mauretanias had come over to his side, and the procurator Albinus (an official who governed a province for the emperor) was dead. Here's how it happened. Nero had put Lucceius Albinus in charge of Mauretania Caesariensis, and Galba had added the province of Tingitana. Albinus had a real force: nineteen cohorts and five cavalry wings. Add a huge crowd of Moors, who were used to war by robbery and raids. When Galba was killed, Albinus leaned toward Otho. But Africa wasn't big enough for him. Big dreams. He threatened Spain, which sits just across a narrow strait, and that scared Cluvius Rufus. Rufus ordered the 10th legion to the shore, as if he were about to cross. Then Rufus sent centurions ahead to win the Moors over to Vitellius. That was easy, since the German army's fame was huge in the provinces. On top of that, a story spread about Albinus. He looked down on the title of procurator, people said, and was taking a king's badges and the name Juba. That was the rumor.

2:59 Once the mood turned, the killing started. Asinius Pollio, a cavalry prefect and one of Albinus's most loyal men, was killed. So were Festus and Scipio, prefects of two cohorts. Albinus himself was sailing from Tingitana to Mauretania Caesariensis when his ship touched the shore, and the killers butchered him there. His wife threw herself in front of them. They killed her right alongside him. Did Vitellius look into any of it? No. Big matters, he got through with a short hearing, because serious cares were not his thing. He sent the army by land and went down the river Arar himself. His trip had no imperial show to it. It carried the look of his old poverty, until Junius Blaesus turned up. Blaesus was governor of Gallia Lugdunensis, from a famous family, generous, and just as rich as he was generous. He surrounded the emperor with attendants and gave him a generous escort. That very help made Blaesus unwelcome to Vitellius. Vitellius hid his hatred behind slavish flattery. At Lugdunum, the leaders of the winning and losing sides were waiting. Vitellius praised Valens and Caecina before an assembly, then seated them on either side of his chair of office. After that he ordered the whole army out to meet his infant son. The boy was carried out in a general's cloak. Vitellius held him on his lap, called him Germanicus, and draped him in every sign of imperial fortune. Too much honor for good times. For bad times? A comfort.

2:60 Executions came next. The boldest Othonian centurions were put to death. That did more than anything to turn the Illyrian armies against Vitellius. The other legions caught the same mood, and they envied the German soldiers too. So they started thinking about war. Meanwhile Vitellius kept Suetonius Paullinus and Licinius Proculus waiting in a gloomy, filthy delay. When they finally got a hearing, their defense was necessary, not honorable. They took credit for treason. Their own double-dealing, they said, explained the long march before the battle, Otho's exhausted troops, and the line tangled up with wagons. Plus a lot of things that were really accidents. Vitellius believed their treachery and found them not guilty of being loyal to Otho. Wonderful. Salvius Titianus, Otho's brother, never faced any danger, since his brotherly devotion and his laziness were taken as excuses. Marius Celsus kept his consulship. But many people believed that Caecilius Simplex had tried to buy that office, paying with money and with Celsus's death. Later it was thrown in Simplex's face in the Senate. Vitellius refused to allow it. He later gave Simplex a consulship that did not have to be bought with crime or money. Trachalus had accusers. He also had Galeria, Vitellius's wife, to protect him.

2:61 All these great men had their crises. Here is an embarrassing one. Who was Mariccus? Nobody special: a man from the common people of the Boii. Still, he leaped into the game of fortune, like a stranger sitting down at a high-stakes card table. His move: take on Rome's arms by faking divinity. He called himself the champion of Gaul and a god, a title he handed himself. By then he had gathered 8,000 men and was dragging in the nearest villages of the Aedui. Then the Aedui's own state, a most serious one, came down on him with a picked force of its young men, plus some cohorts Vitellius added. The crowd of fanatics scattered. Mariccus was caught in that fight. Soon he was thrown to wild beasts. They didn't tear him up. So the foolish crowd decided he couldn't be hurt. That lasted until he was killed. Vitellius watched.

2:62 After that, the cruelty stopped. Nobody went after the other side or their property. Soldiers who fell fighting for Otho had their wills honored, and the law handled the rest. Here's the thing: if Vitellius could have kept his love of luxury in check, nobody would have feared his greed. But feasts were his weakness, a shameful, bottomless one. Fancy food poured in from the city and from Italy until the roads from both seas rang with traffic. The leading men of the states were drained by the cost of his banquets. The states themselves were ruined. His soldiers got used to pleasure and lost all respect for their leader. Their drive and courage went with it. Not a great combination for an army. He sent an edict ahead to Rome. In it he gave up the title Augustus and turned down the name Caesar. His real power? He kept every bit. The astrologers were thrown out of Italy. Roman Knights, the wealthy class just below the senators, were forbidden to disgrace themselves in gladiator schools and the arena, on pain of harsh penalties. Earlier emperors had pushed them into it with money, and more often with force. Towns and colonies had even competed to lure the most corrupt young men with pay.

2:63 Then Vitellius's brother arrived, and the teachers of tyranny crept in. Vitellius grew prouder and crueler. He ordered Dolabella killed. Remember him? Otho had sent him off to the colony of Aquinum. When Otho died, Dolabella came back into the city. Plancius Varus, a former praetor and a close friend of Dolabella, accused him before Flavius Sabinus, the officer who kept order in Rome. Varus said Dolabella had broken out of custody and offered himself as leader of the losing side. He added that the cohort at Ostia had been tampered with. Huge charges. Proof? None. Then Varus changed his mind and begged for a pardon after the crime, but it came too late. Flavius Sabinus hesitated, because the matter was so serious. Then Triaria, the wife of Lucius Vitellius, stepped in. She was fierce, far beyond what you'd expect of a woman. She warned Sabinus not to chase a name for mercy at the emperor's risk. Sabinus was gentle by nature, but fear made him easy to sway. He was afraid for himself in another man's danger, and he did not want to look like Dolabella's helper. So he gave the falling man a shove.

2:64 Why did Vitellius want Dolabella dead? Fear and hatred. Dolabella had married his former wife, Petronia. Vitellius wrote to him and ordered him to skip the crowded Flaminian Road, turn off toward Interamna, and be killed there. The executioner thought that was too slow. At an inn along the road, he threw Dolabella to the ground and cut his throat. A wave of hatred crashed down on the new reign. People saw it as a first taste of what was coming. Triaria's wildness was bad enough. Next to the decent people in the house, it looked even worse. Galeria, the emperor's wife, took no part in the cruelty. Neither did Sextilia, the mother of the two Vitellii, a woman of the same decency and the old-fashioned kind. Reading her son's first letters, Sextilia reportedly said she had given birth to Vitellius, not to a Germanicus. Ouch. No later stroke of luck and no flattery from the state ever made her happy. She felt only her family's misfortunes.

2:65 Marcus Cluvius Rufus had a problem. He left Spain and caught up with Vitellius after Vitellius had left Lugdunum. His face showed joy and congratulations. Inside, he was anxious, because he knew people had piled accusations on him. The accuser was Hilarus, a freedman of the emperor (a former slave the emperor had set free). Hilarus said that when Cluvius heard Vitellius and Otho both claimed the throne, he tried to win power for himself and hold Spain. That, Hilarus claimed, was why Cluvius put no emperor's name at the top of his travel papers. Hilarus also pointed to lines in Cluvius's speeches. Insults to Vitellius, he called them, meant to win Cluvius popularity. So did Cluvius go down? No. His influence was strong enough that Vitellius ordered his own freedman punished. Cluvius joined the emperor's retinue (his group of attendants) and kept Spain. He would govern it from far away, like Lucius Arruntius. Tiberius Caesar had kept Arruntius at home out of fear. Vitellius kept Cluvius with no fear at all. Trebellius Maximus got no such honor. The soldiers in Britain were so angry at him that he fled. Vettius Bolanus, who was with the emperor at the time, was sent to replace him.

2:66 The beaten legions gave Vitellius a new headache. Their spirit was far from broken. They were scattered across Italy, mixed in with the winners, and they talked like enemies. The 14th legion was especially fierce. Its soldiers flatly denied they had been beaten. At Bedriacum, they said, only the detachments had lost. The legion's real strength was not even there. Vitellius decided to send them back to Britain, where Nero had called them from. Meanwhile one Batavian cohort would camp with them, because the Batavians and the 14th had hated each other for years. Armed men, old hatred, one camp. Peace was never going to last. At Augusta of the Taurini, a Batavian chased a craftsman, calling him a cheat. A legionary stuck up for the craftsman, who was his host. Their comrades rushed in on both sides. Shouting turned into killing. It would have become a savage battle if two Praetorian cohorts (units of the emperor's guard) had not taken the side of the 14th. That gave the legion confidence and the Batavians a fright. Vitellius then attached the Batavians to his own force, as a reward for their loyalty. The legion he ordered over the Graian Alps, on a route that avoided Vienna. The people of Vienna were under suspicion too. The night the legion left, fires were left burning all over, and part of the colony of Taurina burned. Like most war damage, it was forgotten next to the bigger disasters of other cities. Down from the Alps came the 14th, and its most mutinous soldiers marched their standards toward Vienna. The better men agreed to hold them back. The legion was shipped across to Britain.

2:67 Next, Vitellius feared the Praetorian cohorts. So he broke them up. Then he sweetened the deal: an honorable discharge, if they handed their weapons to their tribunes, the mid-ranking officers. Sure. That backfired, because Vespasian's war kept growing and those same Praetorians signed up again. Before long they were the backbone of the Flavianist cause. The 1st legion of marines? Off to Spain, where the peace and quiet would cool it down. The 11th and 7th returned to their winter quarters. The 13th got orders to build amphitheaters, since Caecina at Cremona and Valens at Bononia were preparing gladiator shows. Priorities, right? The cares of ruling never made Vitellius forget his pleasures.

2:68 Now the winners had a mutiny of their own. Vitellius had quietly broken up the beaten side, but then his own men started in on each other. It began as a game. The dead piled up, though, and people hated Vitellius even more for it. He was sitting at a banquet in Ticinum, with Verginius as his invited guest. Legates and tribunes (senior and mid-ranking officers) copy their emperors. Some copy his strictness, others his taste for early banquets, and the soldiers follow along, alert or lazy. Around Vitellius? Drunk, loose, a mess. It looked more like an all-night party than a camp with discipline. Then two soldiers were egged on, as a joke, into a wrestling match. One came from the 5th legion, a big unit of Roman citizen soldiers. The other was a Gallic auxiliary, a non-citizen soldier from a support unit. The legionary hit the ground and the Gaul jeered at him. The watching crowd split into two sides, and the legionaries charged out and killed two auxiliary cohorts. Ouch. The cure for the riot was another riot. Then dust rose in the distance, with weapons glinting in it. Someone yelled that the 14th legion had turned back to attack. It hadn't. It was the rear guard of the column. Once people saw who it was, the fear drained away. Meanwhile a slave of Verginius happened to run into the soldiers, and they accused him of being Vitellius's would-be assassin. The mob stormed the banquet, howling for Verginius's death. Vitellius was scared of every suspicion, yet even he never doubted Verginius was innocent. Still, he could barely hold back men who wanted a former consul dead, a man who had once been their own general. Nobody faced more outbursts than Verginius. He was still admired and still famous. But the soldiers hated him like people who feel looked down on.

2:69 The next day Vitellius met the Senate's delegation. He had ordered them to wait for him there. Then he marched into the camp and praised the soldiers' loyalty to the skies. The auxiliaries grumbled that the legionaries were getting off free of punishment and swaggering about it. Vitellius sent the Batavian cohorts back to Germany, so they wouldn't try anything worse. Fate was setting up a civil war and a war abroad at the same moment. The Gallic auxiliaries were also sent home to their states. There were huge numbers of them, taken on in the first days of the revolt just for show. Then came the money problem. Handouts had already drained the empire's funds, and he wanted what was left to last. So he cut the legions and auxiliaries down and banned recruiting. Anyone could get a discharge. Brilliant. It ruined the state, and the soldiers hated it. The same duties now fell on fewer men, so hard work and danger came around more often. Luxury was eating away at their strength too. That went against the old discipline and the customs of their ancestors. Under those ancestors, Rome stood firmer on courage than on money. Courage first. Cash second.

2:70 Next stop, Cremona. Vitellius watched a gladiator show Caecina put on. Then a craving hit him. He wanted to stand on the plains of Bedriacum and see the traces of his recent victory with his own eyes. It was a foul, horrifying sight. Less than forty days had passed since the battle. Bodies lay mangled and limbs severed, with men and horses rotting and the ground soaked with decay. Trees and crops had been trampled into a dreadful wasteland. It was like a nightmare you can't wake up from. The road was just as ghastly. The people of Cremona had covered part of it with laurel and roses. They had built altars and sacrificed animals the way a king would. The party was pleasant for the moment, and it soon ruined them. Valens and Caecina went along and pointed out where the fighting had been. Here the column of legions had burst in, there the cavalry had risen up, and over there bands of auxiliaries had swung around. The tribunes and prefects each praised their own deeds, mixing lies, truth and exaggeration together. Sure they did. The common soldiers left the road too, shouting and cheering. They wanted to find the stretches of fighting and gape at the heaps of weapons and piles of bodies. A few were moved to tears and pity by the twists of fortune. But Vitellius didn't look away. Thousands of unburied citizens lay there, and he didn't shudder. He was delighted. He had no idea how close his own fate was, and he held a sacrifice to the gods of the place.

2:71 Fabius Valens put on a gladiator show at Bononia, with all the gear shipped in from Rome. The nearer Vitellius got to Rome, the looser his march became. Actors and crowds of eunuchs mixed in, along with every other habit of Nero's court. Vitellius openly admired Nero himself. He used to trail after Nero whenever Nero sang, and nobody had forced him. The most honorable men were the ones who had to. Luxury and gluttony had bought him and owned him. Then came the consulships. Valens and Caecina needed open months to hold the office, one of the year's two top jobs, so other men's terms were cut short. Martius Macer was passed over, supposedly because he had been a general on Otho's side. Valerius Marinus, whom Galba had named consul, was put off. He had done nothing wrong. He was only mild, the sort who'd take a wrong meekly. Pedanius Costa was left out too. The emperor disliked him. Costa had dared to act against Nero, and he had pushed Verginius into action. Other reasons were given, of course. In the end, out of slavish habit, thanks were voted to Vitellius. Thanks for what, exactly?

2:72 However sharp its start, the lie lasted only a few days. A man turned up claiming to be Scribonianus Camerinus. He said he had hidden in Histria, scared of Nero's times. Why Histria? The old Crassi family still had estates there, people who depended on them, and a popular name. He let all the worst of his followers in on the story. A gullible crowd and some soldiers rushed to join him, maybe because they didn't know the truth, maybe because they just loved chaos. Then he was dragged before Vitellius and asked who on earth he was. Nobody believed his answers. Then his owner recognized him: a runaway slave named Geta. So much for the Crassi name. He was punished the way slaves were.

2:73 It's hard to believe how arrogant and lazy Vitellius became. Scouts from Syria and Judea had come back with news: the East had sworn loyalty to him. The reports were vague and uncertain. Still, Vespasian was on people's lips and in every rumor, and the name often got Vitellius riled up. But no rival was in sight. So Vitellius and his army went wild with cruelty, lust, plundering and foreign ways. Nobody was stopping them.

2:74 Meanwhile Vespasian was weighing the war, counting weapons and forces, near and far. His soldiers were so ready for it that they only listened in silence as he led the oath and prayed for every blessing on Vitellius. That said plenty. Mucianus had no quarrel with Vespasian. If anything, his sympathy ran even more toward Titus. The prefect of Egypt, Tiberius Alexander, had already joined the plans. He counted the 3rd legion as his own, because it had moved from Syria to Moesia, and the other Illyrian legions were expected to follow. Why were they all so keen? Vitellius's soldiers had set the whole army aflame with their arrogance. Those men were fierce in body, rough in speech, and sure that everyone else was beneath them. Still, a war this huge usually means delay. One moment Vespasian soared with hope. The next, he was running through the bad outcomes. What a day that would be, he thought: sixty years old, two young sons, and handing himself over to war! In private ventures, he thought, a man can push ahead and take more or less from fortune, whatever he likes. But for anyone who wants the throne? Nothing lies between the heights and the cliff.

2:75 The German army's strength was always before Vespasian's eyes. Any military man knew how strong it was. His own legions had no experience of civil war, he thought. Vitellius's legions were the winners. Among the beaten, there were more complaints than strength. Loyalty was shaky because of the soldiers' quarrels. And any single man could be a danger. So what good were cohorts and cavalry? Not much, if one man or another could commit a crime right now and collect his prize from the other side. That's how Scribonianus was killed under Claudius. His killer, Volaginius, rose from the ranks to the highest military post. Whole armies are easy to sway. One assassin is hard to dodge.

2:76 Vespasian was still wobbling. His friends and the other legates (army commanders) steadied him, and so did Mucianus. Mucianus had held many private talks with him. Now he spoke in front of everyone. Bold move. "Anyone with big plans has to ask some questions," he said. "Will it help the state? Will it bring glory? Is it easy to pull off, or at least not very hard? Then look at the man giving the advice. Is he risking his own neck? And if luck is on your side, who gets the biggest honor?" He pushed on. "I'm calling you to the throne, Vespasian. Think how good that is for the state and how amazing for you. Next to the gods, it's in your own hands. Don't worry that this sounds like flattery. Being picked after Vitellius is closer to an insult. We aren't taking on the sharpest mind of the divine Augustus, and we aren't fighting the careful old age of Tiberius. We aren't even facing the families of Gaius, Claudius or Nero, whose family had ruled for a long time. You gave way even to the family portraits of Galba. Sleep any longer, let the state be fouled and destroyed, and it looks like laziness and cowardice. That's true even if being a slave were as safe for you as it is shameful. The time to look like you never wanted the throne is gone. The throne is your only refuge now. Only one. Remember Corbulo's murder? I admit his family was grander than ours. But Nero's birth was nobler than Vitellius's. A man who is feared is famous enough to the one who fears him. And Vitellius proves an army can make an emperor. He had no years of service and no military fame. He rose only because people hated Galba. Vitellius has even made Otho look like a great emperor, now that Otho is missed. Think about that. Otho wasn't beaten by a general's skill or an enemy's strength. He was beaten by his own rush to despair. Meanwhile Vitellius scatters his legions, disarms his cohorts, and hands out fresh seeds of war every day. The soldiers' fire and fierceness is wearing away in taverns and drinking parties, and in copying their emperor. You have nine whole legions from Judea, Syria and Egypt. No battle has drained them, and no mutiny has ruined them. They are tough, experienced soldiers, and they are masters of a foreign war. Add strong fleets, cavalry wings and cohorts, plus the most loyal kings. Add more experience than anyone else has."

2:77 "I only ask not to be ranked behind Valens and Caecina. But don't brush off Mucianus as a partner just because you see no rival in him. I put myself above Vitellius, and you above me. Your family has the name of a triumph (a victory parade in Rome). You also have two young sons, and one is already fit for the throne. He's famous for his first years in the army, even among the German armies. If I were emperor, I'd look ridiculous not stepping aside for the man whose son I'd adopt. But between us, winning and losing won't be split the same way. If we win, I take whatever honor you give me. The danger and the risk we share equally. Better still, you command your armies. Leave me the war and all its uncertainty. Odd but true: the losers keep stricter discipline than the winners today. Anger, hatred and a hunger for revenge make the losers brave, while winners go dull from contempt and stubbornness. Strange, but true. War itself will tear open the hidden, swelling wounds of the winning side. Ugly. Do I trust your watchfulness, thrift and wisdom? I do. Exactly as much as I count on Vitellius's laziness, ignorance and cruelty. Still, in war our cause is better than it ever could be in peace. Why? Those who are still debating a revolt have already revolted."

2:78 Mucianus finished, and the others crowded around Vespasian, bolder now. They urged him on, quoting what the prophets had said and what the stars were doing. Vespasian wasn't immune to superstition himself. Later, as master of the world, he openly kept an astrologer named Seleucus as his adviser and forecaster. Old omens came back to his mind. Funny how that works. A cypress tree on his land, famous for its height, had suddenly fallen. The next day it rose again from the same spot, taller and wider, and green. The diviners all agreed it was a big, lucky sign. The highest fame was promised to Vespasian when he was still a young man. At first it seemed fulfilled. He had won a triumph, the consulship (one of the year's two top offices) and glory from the Jewish victories. Once he had all of those, he started to think it meant the throne. Now to Carmel, between Judea and Syria. It's the name of both the mountain and the god. Their ancestors said the god has no statue and no temple. There was just an altar and deep respect. Vespasian was sacrificing there, turning his secret hopes over in his head. The priest Basilides checked the entrails again and again. Then he spoke. Mysterious stuff. "Vespasian, whatever you're planning, a house, bigger fields or more slaves, here is the answer. You're being given a great home, huge boundaries, and lots of men." Puzzling words. Rumor grabbed them at once. Then rumor started spelling them out, and nothing was more often on the crowd's lips. Everyone repeated it. Around Vespasian himself the talk was even louder. Hopeful people always get an earful.

2:79 No doubts were left, so they parted. Mucianus went to Antioch, the capital of Syria, and Vespasian went to Caesarea, the capital of Judea. Plans set. The plan to hand Vespasian the empire began in Alexandria, when Tiberius Alexander rushed things along. He made the legions swear loyalty to Vespasian on July 1st. People later celebrated that day as the first day of his rule. But the army of Judea swore in front of Vespasian himself on July 3rd. They were so fired up they wouldn't even wait for his son Titus. Titus was on his way back from Syria, carrying messages between Mucianus and his father. The soldiers acted on pure impulse. No formal assembly. No legions brought together. Just impulse.

2:80 They were still hunting for the right time and place. The hardest part of a job like this? Finding someone to speak first. Hope, fear, success and failure all swirled through their heads. Then one day Vespasian walked out of his room. Here it came. A few soldiers stood there in their usual spot, ready to greet him as their legate. They hailed him as emperor instead. Then the others came running. They piled on the names Caesar and Augustus and every other title of the throne. Fear turned to confidence in their luck, just like that. Vespasian himself did not get puffed up or arrogant. Not a bit. Nothing about him changed with his new situation. He shook off the fog that such a great height had spread over his eyes. Then he spoke to them like a soldier and took in the good news pouring in from every side. Mucianus had been waiting for exactly this. He moved fast. He quickly had the eager soldiers swear loyalty to Vespasian. Next stop: the theater at Antioch, where the people usually hold their discussions. The crowd poured in, gushing with flattery. Mucianus spoke to them. He was pretty polished, even in Greek, and he knew how to show off in everything he said and did. But what really riled up the province and the army was Mucianus's claim about Vitellius. Vitellius, he said, had decided to move the German legions to Syria, where the duty would be easy and rich. The Syrian legions would go to the German winter camps in exchange, where the weather and the work were harsh. Picture that swap. The people of the province had grown used to living beside the soldiers. Many were tied to them by friendship or family. The soldiers, after many years of service, loved their familiar camps like home. Home was home.

2:81 All of Syria swore the same oath before July 15th. Sohemus came in with his whole kingdom, and that was no small force. Antiochus came too, with his huge family fortune, and he was the richest of the kings who answered to Rome. Agrippa got secret messages from his people while Vitellius still knew nothing, so he left Rome and raced across the sea. Queen Berenice was just as fired up for the cause. She was young and gorgeous, and even old Vespasian liked her, since her gifts were so lavish. Every province along the coast, from Asia to Achaia, took the oath. So did the inland country toward Pontus and Armenia. But there was a problem: the legates, the generals running those provinces, had no troops. Cappadocia had no legions yet. Not ideal. The plan for the whole war came together at a council in Berytus. Who showed up? Mucianus, with his legates, his tribunes (the mid-ranking officers), and the most distinguished centurions and soldiers. Picked men of high standing came from the army of Judea too. Picture it. Horsemen, foot soldiers, and kings trying to outdo each other with flashy displays. It looked like an emperor's luck.

2:82 First came the troops. Vespasian called back the veterans, the old soldiers who had left the army, and raised new recruits. Strong cities got orders to set up weapon workshops. Antioch minted gold and silver coins. The right people drove each job forward in its own place. Vespasian went around himself. He pushed everyone on. He praised the good ones and spurred the lazy ones, more by example than by force. And he forgave his friends' faults more readily than he noticed their strengths. Many got army posts or governing jobs from him, and a good number became senators. All were outstanding men, and they soon rose to the top. For a few, luck did more than merit. Mucianus, in his first speech, had promised the soldiers only a modest cash bonus. Vespasian paid no more in a civil war than others had in peacetime. He refused to buy the soldiers off. That gave him a better army. Funny how that works. Envoys went to Parthia and Armenia. The goal was to keep the legions, busy with civil war, from leaving their backs open. Titus would press on in Judea, and Vespasian would guard the gates of Egypt. Against Vitellius, they figured part of the forces would do, with Mucianus in command and Vespasian's name behind them. And nothing is hard for fate. Letters went out to every army and legate. The order was to invite the Praetorians, the emperor's guard, who were hostile to Vitellius. The offer: a way back into service.

2:83 Mucianus set out light. His force was small. He acted like the emperor's partner, not his servant. He wouldn't go slowly, since that looked like hesitating. He didn't rush either. So the rumors grew with every mile. He knew his force was small, but he also knew that people believe bigger things about those they can't see. Behind him, though, a huge column was on the march. It held the 6th legion and 13,000 men from the detachments (small groups of soldiers pulled out for special duty). He had ordered the fleet to sail from the Pontus to Byzantium. And his own plan? He hadn't decided. Should he skip Moesia and go for Dyrrachium with his infantry and cavalry, while his warships shut off the sea to Italy? That would leave Asia and Achaia safe behind him. But they had no troops. Unless he garrisoned them, they would be at Vitellius's mercy. And Vitellius himself would be stuck, with enemy fleets threatening Brundisium, Tarentum and the coasts of Calabria and Lucania. Which part of Italy should he defend? He would have no idea. Good luck with that.

2:84 Ships, soldiers, weapons: the provinces rang with it. But nothing wore people down like the hunt for money. Mucianus liked to say money was the sinews of civil war, what holds a body together. His hearings had nothing to do with right or truth. He looked at one thing only, how big a man's fortune was. Informers were everywhere, and the richest men were picked out and plundered. These measures were heavy and unbearable. But the war excused them, so they carried on into peacetime. Vespasian himself, early in his reign, was not so stubborn about keeping the unfairness going. Then too much good luck and some bad teachers got to him. And he dared to keep them up. Mucianus also paid for the war out of his own pocket. He was generous with his private funds because he grabbed from the state with greedier hands. The others followed his lead and gave. Getting it back? Hardly anyone could.

2:85 Meanwhile the Illyrian armies were eager, and that sped Vespasian along. They had already come over to his side. The 3rd legion set the example for the other legions of Moesia, the 8th and the 7th, Claudius's legion. Both were soaked in support for Otho, though they had missed the battle. They marched to Aquileia and acted like enemies. They shoved aside the men bringing news about Otho. They tore up the flags with Vitellius's name on them. In the end they seized the money and split it among themselves. Then fear set in, and out of the fear came a plan. Whatever they had to explain to Vitellius could be pinned on Vespasian. So the three Moesian legions tried to win over the Pannonian army by letter. If it refused, they would use force. Right in the middle of this mess, Aponius Saturninus, governor of Moesia, dared a terrible crime. He sent a centurion to kill Tettius Julianus, legate of the 7th legion. The cause? A personal feud, dressed up as loyalty to his side. When Julianus learned of the danger, he took guides who knew the land and fled through the roadless parts of Moesia, past Mount Haemus. After that he mostly stayed out of the war. He had started a trip to Vespasian, and he dragged it out with delays. Fast or slow, it all depended on the news.

2:86 Pannonia went the same way. The 13th legion and the 7th, Galba's legion, still burned with pain and anger over Bedriacum. They joined Vespasian without a second thought. Primus Antonius was mostly why. Back under Nero, he had broken the law and been found guilty of fraud. Then, amid the war's other evils, he won back his rank as senator. Galba put him in charge of the 7th legion. People believed he wrote to Otho again and again, offering himself as a commander. Otho snubbed him, so he sat out the Othonian war. Then Vitellius's cause began to wobble. Antonius followed Vespasian and added real weight to his cause. He was vigorous in action and quick with words. He had an artist's gift for stirring up hatred against others. He was powerful in fights and revolts. A grabber. A spender. In peace he was the worst. In war, you didn't dare look down on him. Moesia and Pannonia joined up, and they dragged the soldiers of Dalmatia along. The legates there, former consuls (top officials), did nothing to stir things up. Tampius Flavianus governed Pannonia and Pompeius Silvanus ran Dalmatia. Both were rich old men. But a procurator (the emperor's manager there) was on hand too: Cornelius Fuscus. He was in the prime of life and came from a famous family. As a young man, he loved quiet so much that he gave up his rank as senator. Later he led a colony of his own for Galba, and that job got him the procuratorship. When he switched to Vespasian's side, he carried the fiercest torch for the war. He cared less for the prizes of danger than for danger itself. He picked the new, shaky and risky over the safe and long-held. So he and Antonius went to work. They shook and stirred every weak spot anywhere. Letters went to the 14th legion in Britain and the 1st in Spain. Why those two? Both had backed Otho against Vitellius. More letters were scattered across the Gauls. Within moments, a huge war was blazing. The Illyrian armies were in open revolt. The rest were ready to follow their luck.

2:87 Vespasian and his leaders were busy in the provinces. Vitellius? He got more pathetic and lazier by the day. He stopped to enjoy every town and villa along the road. Then he crawled toward the city with a huge, slow column. Sixty thousand armed men trailed behind him, spoiled rotten, with zero discipline. The camp servants outnumbered even them, and their nerve was the worst you could find among slaves. All those legates and friends would have been a headache for any commander, even a strict one. And the crowd kept growing. Senators and Roman Knights (the wealthy class just below the senators) came out from the city to meet him. Some were afraid. Many wanted to flatter him. The rest drifted out little by little, until it was all of them, because nobody wanted to be left behind while others set out. Then came the worthless lower classes: buffoons, actors, charioteers. Vitellius knew them from their shameful services. He took a strange delight in such disgraceful friends. Colonies and towns were drained to feed the whole mob. Even the farmers and their fields were plundered like enemy ground. And the harvest was ripening. Ouch.

2:88 The soldiers killed each other, and in savage numbers. After the mutiny at Ticinum, the legions and the auxiliaries were still at odds. Still, they teamed up whenever they had to fight country people. The worst slaughter came at the seventh milestone from the city. Vitellius was handing out prepared food to the soldiers one by one, the way gladiators were fed. The common people had poured out and mixed into the whole camp. Some of them had a slave's homegrown city wit. They robbed the careless soldiers. They cut belts in secret, then asked whether the men were armed. Bad joke. The soldiers weren't used to insults and couldn't take it. Swords came out. They attacked the unarmed crowd. One of the dead was a soldier's father, who had been standing with his son. Later he was recognized. Once word of the killing got out, the soldiers held back from the innocent. Inside the city, though, panic spread like fire through dry grass. Soldiers ran ahead in every direction. Most of them headed for the Forum, eager to see the spot where Galba's body had lain. They looked terrifying, bristling with animal skins and huge spears. They didn't know the place, so they kept crashing into the crowd. A slippery street, a bump, a fall, and the arguing turned to fists and swords. Even the tribunes (mid-ranking officers) and prefects zoomed around with scary gangs of armed men. Gangs, everywhere.

2:89 Vitellius came from the Mulvian bridge on a flashy horse, dressed like a general in cloak and sword. He was driving the Senate and the people along in front of him. Then his friends stepped in. They talked him out of marching in as if he had captured the city. So he changed into a civilian toga and brought in his army in order. Four legions' eagles led the way. Around them stood the same number of flags from other legions. Next came the standards of twelve cavalry wings, and the horsemen rode behind the foot soldiers. After that, thirty-four cohorts, grouped by nation name or by weapon. In front of the eagles walked the camp prefects, the tribunes and the chief centurions, all in white. The other centurions each stood beside his own company, glittering with arms and decorations. Medals and neck chains shone. Handsome army, right? Better than an emperor like Vitellius deserved. Then he climbed to the Capitol. There he hugged his mother and named her Augusta.

2:90 Next day came the big speech. Vitellius spoke about himself as if his audience were the Senate and people of some other country. He praised his own energy and self-control. Self-control? Everyone listening knew about his shameful acts. So did all of Italy, which he had crossed in a haze of sleep and luxury. The crowd didn't care. It had no worries and couldn't tell truth from lies. It had learned the usual flattery, so it applauded with shouts and cries. Vitellius held back from the name Augustus, and they pushed him to take it. Just as empty as his earlier refusal.

2:91 Everyone in the city read meaning into everything. So when Vitellius, now chief priest, announced public ceremonies for July 18th, people took it as a gloomy sign. That day had been unlucky since ancient times, marked by the disasters at the Cremera and the Allia. He knew as little about law, human and divine, as a man possibly could. His freedmen (men who had once been slaves) and friends were just as careless. Living with them was like living among drunks. Still, he ran the elections for consul in a civil way. He stood beside the candidates like any ordinary citizen. He also chased every scrap of the lowest crowd's talk, like a spectator at the theater or a fan at the races. Good qualities would have made all of that pleasant and popular. But people remembered his earlier life. So it looked tasteless and cheap. He kept coming to Senate meetings, even for small debates. Once, Helvidius Priscus, elected praetor but not yet in office, spoke against what he wanted. Vitellius was upset at first. He only called on the tribunes of the people, officials whose job was to protect ordinary citizens, to back his insulted authority. His friends feared his anger ran deeper, so they tried to calm him. Vitellius answered that two senators disagreeing was nothing new in a free republic. He himself, he said, used to oppose Thrasea. Most people laughed at the nerve of that comparison. Some were pleased, though. Vitellius had picked Thrasea as his model of true glory, not one of the most powerful men of the day. Fair enough.

2:92 Who commanded the emperor's guard? Two men, each with a patron. Vitellius had put Publilius Sabinus, a cohort prefect, and Julius Priscus, then a centurion (an army officer), in charge of the Praetorian Guard. Valens had lifted Priscus, and Caecina had lifted Sabinus. Between two men at odds, Vitellius had no authority. Caecina and Valens did the emperor's work instead. Their hatred was old, and they hid it badly in the war and in the camp. Corrupt friends made it worse, and so did a city that breeds quarrels. Now they scrambled for popularity, with crowds of followers and huge lines of callers. Everyone compared them, while Vitellius's favor swung from one to the other. Sounds like a great workplace, right? Power is never very safe when it gets too big. Vitellius himself lurched between sudden anger and ill-timed affection. Caecina and Valens scorned him and feared him at once. Did that slow them down? Not at all. They still seized houses, gardens and the wealth of the empire. Meanwhile a sorry, needy crowd of nobles got no help from the emperor's pity. Galba had brought them back to their country with their children. Then came an edict that the leading men liked and the people approved. It gave the returned exiles back their rights over their freedmen (former slaves who had been set free). The freedmen tried every trick to undo the favor, with a slave's cleverness. They hid money in secret or powerful pockets. Some moved into Caesar's household. They grew more powerful than their own masters.

2:93 The soldiers had nothing to do. The camp was packed and overflowing, so they wandered through the porticoes, the temples and the whole city. They didn't know where their headquarters was. Talk about a bad job. They stood no watches and never toughened up with work. City pleasures, and things too shameful to mention, wore down their bodies with laziness and their minds with lust. Finally they stopped caring even about their health. A large part of them camped in the unhealthy parts of the Vatican, and many troops died. The Tiber ran right beside them, and the Germans and Gauls guzzled its water. They couldn't stand the heat either. Their bodies were already prone to disease, and that wrecked them. On top of that, crooked deals and favoritism threw the army's chain of command into chaos. Sixteen Praetorian cohorts and four city cohorts were being signed up, a thousand men each. In that draft, Valens dared more than Caecina. He claimed he had rescued Caecina himself from danger. And it was true that his arrival had revived the cause, and his winning battle had turned around the bad talk about his slow march. The whole army of Lower Germany also followed Valens. Caecina's loyalty is believed to have started wobbling right there.

2:94 Vitellius spoiled his generals. The soldiers got even more freedom. Each man picked his own service. Unfit? He could still join the city force if he liked. Good soldiers could stay with their legions or in the cavalry, and many did, worn out by sickness or put off by the bad climate. But the best men of the legions and the cavalry were drained into the capital's force. Twenty thousand men from the whole army, mixed together and not picked, crushed the old glory of the Praetorian camp. Ouch. While Vitellius spoke to the troops, they shouted for the execution of Asiaticus, Flavus and Rufinus. Those men were leaders of the Gauls, and they had fought for Vindex. Vitellius didn't stop the shouting. He was a coward at heart. He also knew a donative (a cash bonus) was coming, and he had no money. So he gave the soldiers everything else. The freedmen of earlier emperors were ordered to pay up like a tax, based on how many slaves they had. And Vitellius himself, with no thought except to throw money away, was building a stable for his charioteers. He filled the circus (the big arena for chariot races and shows) with gladiator fights and wild-beast hunts. He spent like a man with money to burn. He had none.

2:95 Caecina and Valens threw Vitellius a birthday bash. Gladiator shows ran in every neighborhood of the city. Nobody had ever seen shows that big before that day. The worst people in Rome loved it, and the decent ones were disgusted. Altars were a sore point too: Vitellius built them on the Campus Martius, and he held funeral rites for Nero. The decent ones felt the same about those. Animals were sacrificed and burned in the state's name. The Augustales lit the fire. That is a priesthood Tiberius Caesar dedicated to the Julian family, and Romulus had set up a similar one for King Tatius. Vitellius's freedman Asiaticus, less than four months after the victory, was keeping up with the Polycleti, the Patrobii and all the other old hated names. Honesty and hard work meant nothing at that court. Power had one road: feed Vitellius's endless appetites. Lavish dinners. Wild spending. Gluttony. The emperor figured it was enough to enjoy the moment, and he never planned past it. In just a few months, he is believed to have blown nine hundred million sesterces (a huge sum of Roman money). Rome, great and wretched, had put up with an Otho and a Vitellius in one year. Then it lived through a string of shameful twists under the Vinii, the Fabii, the Iceli and the Asiatici, until Mucianus and Marcellus arrived. Different men. Same morals.

2:96 Vitellius heard of the first revolt by letter, and it was the 3rd legion's. Aponius Saturninus wrote the letters, before he too joined Vespasian's side. But Aponius was rattled by the sudden event, so he hadn't written the whole story. Vitellius's flattering friends softened it by saying one legion only had mutinied and the other armies were loyal for sure. Vitellius told the soldiers the same thing. He lashed out at the recently discharged Praetorians, saying they were spreading false rumors. Civil war? No danger, he insisted. He kept Vespasian's name quiet, and soldiers were sent to roam the city and shut up people's talk. Good plan, right? Nothing fed the rumors more.

2:97 Vitellius called for help. He summoned auxiliary troops from Germany, Britain and Spain. He did it slowly, trying to hide how badly he needed them. The legates (army commanders) were just as slow. Hordeonius Flaccus already suspected the Batavians and was worried about a war of his own. Vettius Bolanus had Britain, a province that was never quite quiet. Both men were wobbling. Spain was in no hurry either, since at that time it had no governor who had been a consul (one of Rome's two top officials for a year). Its three legions had legates of equal rank. While Vitellius was doing well, they would have competed to obey him. Now his luck was failing, and they all held back equally. Of course they did. In Africa, the legion and cohorts raised by Clodius Macer went back into service on Vitellius's order. Galba had disbanded them soon after. The rest of the young men eagerly signed up. Why? Vitellius had governed that province as proconsul (a governor of a province) honestly, and people liked him for it. Vespasian's term had been notorious and hated. So the allies guessed how each man would rule. It turned out the opposite.

2:98 Valerius Festus, the legate, started out loyal and backed the eager people of his province. Then he began to wobble. In letters and edicts he openly supported Vitellius, but in secret messages he supported Vespasian. He was ready to defend whichever one grew stronger. Basically, he was playing both sides. Some soldiers and centurions came through Raetia and Gaul carrying Vespasian's letters and edicts. They were caught, sent to Vitellius and put to death. More got through, hidden by friends' loyalty or by their own cleverness. So Vitellius's preparations were known, while most of Vespasian's plans stayed unknown. Why? First, Vitellius's laziness. Then the Pannonian Alps, taken over by garrisons, held the messengers back. The sea was a problem too. The Etesian winds (winds that helped ships sail east and blocked the trip back) favored anyone sailing east. They blocked anyone coming back.

2:99 Vitellius finally got scared. With the enemy invading and bad news pouring in from every side, he ordered Caecina and Valens to take the field. Caecina went ahead, while Valens, only just up from a serious illness, was kept back by weakness. Not a great start. The German army looked very different as it marched out of the city. Weak bodies. Dead spirits. The column crawled along, long and thin, weapons hanging loose in their hands and the horses listless. The sun, the dust and the weather wore the soldiers down. And the less they could take hard work, the more they wanted to mutiny. Bad combination. Then add Caecina, with his old showing off and his new laziness. Ouch. Fortune had spoiled him, and luxury had made him soft. Was he already plotting to betray Vitellius? Perhaps. Wearing down the army's spirit may have been part of the plan. Many believed Flavius Sabinus had shaken his mind, with Rubrius Gallus carrying the messages. The promise was simple: Vespasian would honor the terms for changing sides. Caecina was also reminded of his hatred and envy of Fabius Valens. Caecina was the weaker of the two in Vitellius's favor, but with the new emperor he could win favor and strength. Betrayal started to look like a bargain.

2:100 Caecina walked out of Vitellius's embrace loaded with honor. He sent part of his cavalry ahead to seize Cremona. Behind them marched detachments of the 1st, 4th, 15th and 16th legions, then the 5th and 22nd. The rear held the 21st Rapax and the 1st Italian legion, with detachments of three British legions and picked auxiliaries. Once Caecina had left, Fabius Valens wrote to the army he had once led himself. He told it to wait for him on the road, just as he and Caecina had agreed. But Caecina was on the spot, which made him stronger, so he pretended the plan had changed. The whole mass of men, he claimed, should meet the growing war. The legions were ordered to hurry to Cremona, and some toward Hostilia. Caecina himself veered off to Ravenna, claiming he had to address the fleet. Sure, the fleet. Soon he was hunting for privacy in Patavium, where he could plan his treason. And Lucilius Bassus? Vitellius had put him in charge of the fleets at Ravenna and Misenum, after he commanded a cavalry wing. But Bassus was furious that he hadn't been made Praetorian prefect (head of the emperor's guard) at once. The anger was unjust. His revenge was worse: a shameful betrayal. Ugly. Did he pull Caecina in? Or did the same wickedness drive them both? Nobody can know. Bad men are alike, after all.

2:101 Who wrote the story of this war? Writers of the time, while the Flavian family held power. They gave reasons like concern for peace and love of the state. Those reasons had been twisted into flattery. Here is what I think. These men were fickle by nature, and their loyalty had gone cheap after Galba was betrayed. Cheap indeed. On top of that, they ruined Vitellius out of rivalry and envy, so that others wouldn't get ahead of them in his favor. Sure, they called it love of the state. Meanwhile, Caecina caught up with the legions. He tried every trick he had to undermine the stubborn loyalty of the centurions and soldiers to Vitellius. Bassus had less trouble with the same attempt, since the fleet had served Otho only recently and was easy to turn. Its last loyalty was barely cold.
