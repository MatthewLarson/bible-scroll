## Titus turns back from the road to Rome
2:1 Far away, fortune was already getting ready the start and the causes of a new dynasty. Its ups and downs would bring joy or disaster to the state, and success or ruin to the emperors themselves. Titus Vespasian had been sent from Judea by his father while Galba was still alive and well. The reasons he gave for the trip were his duty to the emperor and the fact that he was now old enough to run for office. But the public, always eager to make things up, had spread the story that he'd been called to Rome to be adopted. The emperor's old age and lack of children gave people something to talk about, and so did the country's habit, until one man is chosen, of naming lots of possible heirs. The rumor got stronger because of Titus himself. His talent was big enough for any destiny, and he had a handsome face with a certain dignity to it. Add to that his father's rising fortunes, the prophecies that pointed his way, and even chance events, which people in a believing mood took as omens. At Corinth, the main city of Achaia, he got solid word that Galba was dead, and he met men who swore that Vitellius was in arms and that war had begun. He was worried. He called in a few friends and went over his situation from both sides. "If I go on to Rome," he said, "nobody will thank me for a courtesy meant to honor someone else, and I'll be a hostage in the hands of Vitellius or Otho. If I turn back, the winner will certainly be offended. But the outcome is still unsettled, and with my father joining one side, a son will be excused. And if Vespasian takes charge of the state, men who have a war on their minds will have to forget old offenses."
## Titus at Paphos: the temple of Venus
2:2 Thoughts like these tossed him between hope and fear, and hope won. Some believed he turned back because he longed for Queen Berenice. His young heart wasn't cold toward Berenice, but it was no obstacle to business. He spent his youth enjoying himself, but he showed more self-control in his own reign than in his father's. So he sailed along the coast of Achaia and Asia, keeping the land on his left, to the islands of Rhodes and Cyprus, and from there struck out across open water for Syria. On the way he felt a strong urge to visit and see the temple of Venus at Paphos, famous among locals and foreigners alike. It won't take long to tell briefly how the worship began, how the temple's rites go, and what the goddess looks like, since she is shown that way nowhere else.
2:3 According to old tradition, the temple's founder was King Aerias, though some say that's the name of the goddess herself. A more recent story says the temple was consecrated by Cinyras, and that the goddess herself, conceived in the sea, drifted ashore at this spot. But the skill and craft of reading entrails was brought in from abroad, they say, by Tamiras of Cilicia, and it was agreed that the descendants of both families would preside over the rites. Later, so the royal family wouldn't be left without some advantage over the foreign line, the newcomers gave up the very craft they had brought. Now only a priest from Cinyras's line is consulted. Worshippers offer whatever victims they've vowed, but males are chosen, and the surest signs are in the entrails of young goats. Pouring blood on the altar is forbidden. It's served only with prayers and clean fire, and though it stands in the open air, rain never wets it. The goddess's image isn't in human form. It's a rounded mass that rises from a broad base to a narrow top, like a cone, and the reason for that is unclear.
## The oracle, and the armies of the East
2:4 Titus looked over the wealth, the gifts of kings, and everything else that the Greeks, who love antiquities, attach to some uncertain past. First he asked the oracle about his voyage. When he was told the way was open and the sea favorable, he sacrificed a number of victims and then asked, in roundabout words, about himself. The priest was named Sostratus. He saw that the entrails were favorable and matched what Titus hoped, and that the goddess was nodding approval to some great enterprise. For the moment he gave a short, ordinary answer, but then he asked for a private meeting and laid out the future. Titus, his spirits lifted, went on to his father. He arrived like a great pledge of success to provinces and armies that were still wavering. Vespasian had nearly finished the Jewish war. Only the siege of Jerusalem remained, which was hard, steep work because of the mountain stronghold and the Jews' stubborn religion, not because the people inside had enough strength left to hold out through hardship. As I said earlier, Vespasian himself had three legions with war experience. Mucianus commanded four in peacetime. But rivalry and the glory of the neighboring army had driven out laziness. The dangers and hard work had given Vespasian's men their toughness, and in Mucianus's army, long untroubled peace and freedom from the hardships of war had given the same amount of vigor. Both had auxiliary cohorts and cavalry, fleets and allied kings, and both had a famous name, though for different reasons.
## Vespasian, Mucianus, and an East that waits
2:5 Vespasian was a sharp soldier. He marched at the head of the column, picked the campsite, and stood up to the enemy night and day with his planning, or with his own hand if the moment called for it. He ate whatever came to hand, and his dress and appearance hardly set him apart from a common soldier. In short, but for his greed, he was the equal of the generals of old. Mucianus, by contrast, stood out for his splendor, his wealth, and a style of living that went far beyond a private citizen's scale. He was better with words and skilled at arranging and foreseeing the business of government. They'd have made a remarkable blend for ruling, if their faults could be stripped away and only their virtues mixed together. But Mucianus governed Syria and Vespasian Judea, and jealousy over their neighboring provinces kept them at odds. Only when Nero fell did they lay their grudges aside and put their plans together. At first they worked through friends. Then Titus became the great guarantee of their agreement, and he ended their harmful quarrel by pointing to their shared interest. By nature and training he was just the man to win over even someone like Mucianus. The tribunes, the centurions and the rank-and-file soldiers were won over by appeals to their drive or their love of doing as they pleased, to their virtues or their vices, as each man's character suggested.
2:6 Before Titus arrived, both armies had sworn their oath to Otho. The news had come fast, as it usually does, but the huge, slow machinery of a civil war held things up. The East, quiet after a long peace, was preparing for one for the first time. In earlier days the most powerful civil wars between citizens had started in Italy or Gaul, with the strength of the West. As for Pompey, Cassius, Brutus and Antony, civil war followed all of them across the sea, and it ended badly for them. In Syria and Judea the emperors were heard of more than seen. There had been no mutiny among the legions, only threats against the Parthians, with mixed results. In the last civil war, while other provinces were shaken, peace here was never disturbed, and after that came loyalty to Galba. But once the word spread that Otho and Vitellius were rushing to carry off the Roman world with wicked weapons, the soldiers began to grumble and to size up their own strength. They didn't want the rewards of power to belong to others while they were left with nothing but the necessity of obeying. Close at hand were seven legions, in Syria and Judea, with huge numbers of auxiliaries. Next came Egypt with its two legions, and on the other side Cappadocia and Pontus and every garrison along the Armenian frontier. There was Asia, and the other provinces, not short of men and rich in money. There were all the islands of the Mediterranean. And there was the sea itself, which would be both convenient and safe while the war was being prepared.
2:7 The generals knew how eager the troops were, but they decided to wait while others fought it out. In a civil war, they said, winners and losers never truly grow together in good faith, and it made no difference whether fortune left Otho or Vitellius standing. Even outstanding generals turn arrogant in success. As for these two, the soldiers' indiscipline, laziness and luxury, along with their own faults, would ruin one of them by war and the other by victory. So they put off the fighting until the right moment. Vespasian and Mucianus had decided only recently, the others long before. The best men were moved by love of the state, many by the lure of plunder, and some by shaky finances at home. So good men and bad, for different reasons but with the same eagerness, all wanted war.
## A false Nero
2:8 Around this time Achaia and Asia were thrown into a false panic by a report that Nero was on his way. Rumors about how he died varied, so more people were pretending he was alive, and more believed it. I'll tell the adventures and attempts of the other pretenders in their place in this work. This one was a slave from Pontus or, according to some, a freedman from Italy, skilled at the harp and at singing. Those skills, along with a resemblance in the face, made his claim more believable. He picked up some deserters, vagrants made desperate by poverty, whom he'd bribed with huge promises, and put to sea. Storms drove him to the island of Cythnus. There he recruited some soldiers who were traveling home from the East, and ordered the ones who refused to be killed. He also robbed the merchants and armed the strongest of their slaves. The centurion Sisenna was carrying clasped right hands, the sign of friendship, from the armies of Syria to the Praetorians. The pretender worked on him with every kind of trick until Sisenna slipped off the island in secret, afraid of violence. From there the alarm spread far and wide. Many were stirred up by the famous name, hungry for change and sick of how things stood. The rumor was growing daily when chance put an end to it.
2:9 Galba had given Calpurnius Asprenas the government of Galatia and Pamphylia. Two warships from the fleet at Misenum were assigned to him to go after the pretender, and with them he reached the island of Cythnus. Some people were even found to summon the ship captains in Nero's name. The pretender, dressed up in a look of sorrow and appealing to the loyalty of his old soldiers, begged them to set him down in Syria or Egypt. The captains, wavering or perhaps playing a trick, said they had to speak to their men and would come back when everyone's mind was ready. But everything was faithfully reported to Asprenas. At his urging the ship was boarded and taken, and the man, whoever he really was, was killed. The body, with its striking eyes and hair and fierce face, was taken to Asia and from there to Rome.
## The informer Annius Faustus on trial
2:10 In a state torn by strife, wavering between freedom and license after so many changes of ruler, even small matters stirred up great upheavals. Vibius Crispus, whose money, power and talent put him among the distinguished rather than the good, called for Annius Faustus, a Roman Knight who had worked as an informer under Nero, to be brought before the Senate. Under Galba the senators had recently voted that the cases of accusers should be looked into. That decree had been applied unevenly. It was weak against a powerful defendant and strong against a helpless one, but it still carried some terror. Crispus was also using his own influence to the fullest to destroy the man who had informed against his brother. He had persuaded a large part of the Senate to demand that Faustus be handed over to destruction with no defense and no hearing. But with others, nothing helped the accused as much as the accuser's excessive power. They said the accused should be given time, told the charges, and heard out, however hated and guilty he was, as precedent required. At first they won, and the hearing was put off for a few days. Before long Faustus was condemned, though not with the full approval of the people that his rotten character should have earned. They remembered that Crispus had carried on the same kind of accusations for profit, and what they disliked was not the penalty but the man who was getting revenge.
## Otho's armies march north
2:11 Meanwhile the war opened well for Otho, whose orders had set the armies of Dalmatia and Pannonia moving. There were four legions, and from each of them 2,000 men were sent on ahead. The main bodies followed at moderate intervals. The 7th had been raised by Galba. The 11th and 13th were veteran legions, and the 14th was especially famous for putting down the revolt in Britain. Nero had added to their glory by choosing them as his best troops. That gave them a long loyalty to Nero and made them eager for Otho. But the more they trusted their strength, the slower they were. The auxiliary cavalry and infantry went ahead of the legions' column. Rome itself supplied a force not to be sneered at: five Praetorian cohorts, detachments of cavalry, and the 1st legion. With them came a disreputable kind of auxiliary, 2,000 gladiators, though even strict commanders had used them in civil wars. Annius Gallus was put in charge of this force. He was sent ahead with Vestricius Spurinna to occupy the banks of the Padus, since the first plans had fallen through now that Caecina, whom they'd hoped to keep inside Gaul, had crossed the Alps. Otho himself was accompanied by picked men of the guard, with the rest of the Praetorian cohorts, veterans from the Praetorian camp, and a huge number of men levied from the fleet. His march was neither lazy nor spoiled by luxury. He wore an iron breastplate and walked on foot in front of the standards, rough, unkempt, and nothing like his reputation.
2:12 Fortune seemed to smile on his efforts. His fleets controlled the sea, and so he held most of Italy, all the way to where the Maritime Alps begin. He had given three generals, Suedius Clemens, Antonius Novellus and Aemilius Pacensis, the job of attacking those mountains and moving on the province of Gallia Narbonensis. But Pacensis was put in chains by his own mutinous soldiers, Antonius Novellus had no authority at all, and Suedius Clemens led by courting popularity. He was as ready to break the good order of discipline as he was eager for battle. You wouldn't have thought they were walking through Italy, or through the fields and homes of their own country. They burned, wrecked and plundered as if on foreign shores and in enemy cities, and all the more cruelly because nobody anywhere had made any preparation against the danger. The fields were full of wealth and the houses stood open. The owners came out to meet them with their wives and children, and in the middle of the safety of peace they were surrounded by the horrors of war. Marius Maturus was then governing the Maritime Alps as procurator. He called up the tribes, which don't lack young men, and set out to keep the Othonianists away from his province's borders. But at the first charge the mountain people were cut down and scattered. They had been gathered in a rush, they knew neither camp nor commander, and so there was no honor for them in winning and no disgrace in running away.
2:13 Angered by that battle, Otho's soldiers turned their rage on the town of Albintemilium. In the field they had won no plunder, since their opponents were poor country people with cheap weapons. They couldn't even take prisoners, because those people were fast on their feet and knew the ground. So their greed was satisfied by the suffering of innocent people. The horror was made worse by a splendid example of courage from a Ligurian woman. She had hidden her son. The soldiers thought money was hidden along with him, and tortured her to find out where he was. She pointed to her womb and answered that he was hiding there. No threats afterward, and not even death itself, could shake that magnificent answer.
## The battle on the coast of Gallia Narbonensis
2:14 Frightened messengers brought Fabius Valens word that Otho's fleet was threatening the province of Gallia Narbonensis, which had sworn loyalty to Vitellius. Envoys from the colonies were already there begging for help. He sent two cohorts of Tungri, four squadrons of cavalry, and the whole cavalry wing of the Treveri under the prefect Julius Classicus. Part of these troops were held back in the colony of Forum Julii, so that if everything were sent overland, the enemy fleet wouldn't rush up to an empty sea. Twelve squadrons of cavalry and picked men from the cohorts went against the enemy. They were joined by a cohort of Ligurians, an old local auxiliary force, and 500 Pannonians who hadn't yet been enrolled under standards. The fight came without delay. The enemy had drawn up like this: part of the men levied from the fleet, mixed in with local country people, stood on the hills near the sea; the Praetorian soldiers filled the level ground between the hills and the shore; and out on the water the fleet lay alongside, ready to fight, turned and spread out in a threatening front. The Vitellianists, weaker in infantry but strong in cavalry, placed the Alpine tribesmen on the nearest ridges and their infantry cohorts in close ranks behind the cavalry. The squadrons of the Treveri charged the enemy carelessly. Veteran soldiers met them head on, and at the same time they were pelted from the side with stones by the country people, who were good throwers. Those people were mixed in among the soldiers, brave and cowardly alike, and when they were winning they were all equally bold. The beaten men were hit with a fresh terror when the fleet swung around into the rear of the fighters. So they were shut in on every side, and the whole force would have been wiped out if the dark of night hadn't held back the winning army and covered the fugitives.
2:15 The Vitellianists, though beaten, didn't stay quiet. They called up reinforcements and attacked an enemy who felt safe and was acting carelessly after his success. The sentries were cut down, the camp broken into, and panic reached the ships. Then, as the fear slowly died down, they held out on a hill nearby that they had taken, and soon broke out again. A savage slaughter followed, and the prefects of the Tungri cohorts, after long holding their line, went down under a shower of missiles. The Othonianists didn't win without bloodshed either, since the enemy cavalry wheeled around and cut off some who had chased them recklessly. And then, as if a truce had been agreed so that neither the cavalry of one side nor the fleet of the other could cause a sudden panic, the Vitellianists pulled back to Antipolis, a town of Gallia Narbonensis, and the Othonianists to Albingaunum in inland Liguria.
## Corsica
2:16 Corsica, Sardinia and the other islands in the nearby sea were held for Otho by the fame of his victorious fleet. But Corsica was nearly ruined by the recklessness of the procurator Decumus Pacarius. In a war this huge it could do nothing for the whole, and it only brought ruin on himself. Out of hatred for Otho, he decided to help Vitellius with the strength of the Corsicans, which would have been worthless help even if it had worked. He called together the leading men of the island and laid out his plan. Claudius Pyrrhicus, captain of the Liburnian ships stationed there, and Quintius Certus, a Roman Knight, spoke against it, and he ordered them killed. The men present were terrified by their deaths, and so was the ignorant crowd, which always shares other people's fears. They all swore loyalty to Vitellius. But when Pacarius started drafting men and wearing out a disorganized population with military duties, they hated the unaccustomed work and began to think about their weakness. They said, "We live on an island, and Germany and the strength of its legions are far away. Even places protected by infantry and cavalry have been raided and wrecked by a fleet." Their feelings turned around suddenly. They didn't use open force, though, but chose a moment for an ambush. When the men who usually surrounded Pacarius had gone, they killed him as he stood helpless and undressed in the bath, with nobody to help him. His companions were butchered along with him. The killers carried the heads to Otho as the heads of public enemies. But lost among so many greater crimes in the general confusion, they were neither rewarded by Otho nor punished by Vitellius.
## Caecina in Italy, and the siege of Placentia
2:17 As I said earlier, Silius's cavalry had already opened the way into Italy and carried the war across the border. Nobody had any love for Otho, but it wasn't that they preferred Vitellius either. A long peace had worn them down to accept any kind of slavery, ready to submit to whoever came and careless about the better cause. The richest side of Italy, all the plains and cities between the Padus and the Alps, was held by Vitellius's forces, since the cohorts Caecina had sent ahead had arrived too. A cohort of Pannonians had been captured near Cremona, and a hundred cavalry and a thousand men levied from the fleet were cut off between Placentia and Ticinum. After these successes the Vitellianist soldiers were no longer held back by the river or its banks. The sight of the Padus itself stirred up the Batavians and the men from across the Rhine. They crossed it suddenly opposite Placentia and seized some scouts, and so frightened the rest that, in their panic, they falsely reported that Caecina's whole army was there.
2:18 Spurinna, who held Placentia, was sure that Caecina hadn't arrived yet. Even if he was approaching, Spurinna meant to keep his men inside the fortifications, and not throw three Praetorian cohorts, a thousand men from the detachments, and a few cavalry against a veteran army. But the unruly soldiers, who knew nothing of war, snatched up their standards and flags and rushed out. They pointed their weapons at their commander when he tried to hold them back, brushing aside the centurions and tribunes. They even shouted that Otho was being betrayed and that Caecina had been invited in. Spurinna went along with a recklessness that was someone else's. At first he was forced to, then he pretended to want it, so that his advice would carry more weight once the mutiny cooled down.
2:19 When the Padus came into sight and night was coming on, they decided to build a fortified camp. That kind of work was new to city soldiers, and it broke their spirits. Then the oldest men began to scold themselves for their gullibility and to point out the danger they'd be in if Caecina and his army surrounded such a small force on open plains. Soon calmer talk was heard all through the camp. The centurions and tribunes joined in and praised their general's foresight in choosing a colony strong in manpower and resources as his base of operations. Finally Spurinna himself, not so much blaming them for the error as showing them the reasoning, led everyone back to Placentia, except for the scouts he left behind. They were now less rowdy and took orders better. The walls were strengthened, battlements added, and the towers built higher. Preparations were made not only for weapons but for discipline and a love of obedience, the one thing that side lacked, since they had no reason to be unhappy with their courage.
2:20 Caecina, as if he'd left his cruelty and wild living on the other side of the Alps, moved through Italy with his army in good order. The towns and colonies took his clothing as a sign of arrogance, because he spoke to men in togas while wearing a many-colored cloak and trousers, a foreign garment. They also took his wife Salonina's behavior as a personal insult, though it hurt no one that she rode through on horseback in a purple outfit that made her stand out. That's human nature: people look hard at someone else's new good fortune and demand moderation from nobody more than from those they once saw as equals. Caecina crossed the Padus. He tried to test the loyalty of the Othonianists in a conference and with promises, and was tested the same way himself. Once the fine-sounding but empty words peace and harmony had been tossed around, he turned all his planning to attacking Placentia, with a great show of terror. He knew that if his first moves succeeded, his reputation would follow in the rest.
2:21 The first day was spent in a headlong rush rather than the skills of a veteran army. Men heavy with food and wine came up to the walls out in the open and careless. In that fight the amphitheater, a very beautiful building outside the walls, burned down. Either the attackers set it on fire while they were hurling torches, sling bullets and flaming missiles at the defenders, or the defenders did while they were throwing them back. The townspeople, always ready to suspect, believed that outsiders from neighboring colonies had slipped in fuel out of envy and rivalry, because no other building in Italy was so big. However it happened, while worse things were feared they made light of it, but once they felt safe again they mourned it as if they could have suffered nothing worse. Caecina was driven back with heavy losses among his men, and the night was spent preparing siege works. The Vitellianists got ready screens, hurdles and sheds to protect the men undermining the walls and the attackers. The Othonianists got ready stakes and enormous masses of stone, lead and brass to smash and crush the enemy. Shame at failing and hope of glory drove both sides. The encouragement on each side was different. One side praised the strength of the legions and the German army. The other praised the distinction of the city troops and the Praetorian cohorts. The first sneered at their enemies as lazy, idle soldiers spoiled by the circus and the theaters. The others called them foreigners and outsiders. At the same time they praised or cursed Otho and Vitellius, and they found more to provoke each other with in insults than in praise.
2:22 At first light the walls were packed with defenders and the fields glittered with armed men. The dense column of the legions and the scattered bands of auxiliaries went after the higher parts of the walls with arrows and stones, and attacked the neglected or crumbling parts at close quarters. The Othonianists hurled javelins down with steadier, surer aim at the German cohorts, who were rushing up recklessly, shaking their shields over their shoulders with savage chanting in their native way, their bodies bare. The legionary soldiers, covered by screens and hurdles, dug under the walls, built up a ramp, and worked at the gates. Against them the Praetorians rolled down huge rocks, placed ready for just that, with a tremendous crash. Some of those coming up were buried under them, others pierced, bloodless, or torn apart. The panic added to the slaughter, and they were wounded all the more fiercely from the walls, so they fell back with their reputation broken. Caecina was ashamed of an assault he'd started so rashly. He didn't want to sit in the same camp as a laughingstock with nothing to show, so he crossed the Padus again and headed for Cremona. As he left, Turullius Cerialis surrendered to him with a number of men levied from the fleet, and so did Julius Briganticus with a few cavalrymen. Briganticus was a cavalry prefect, born among the Batavians. Turullius was a centurion of the highest rank, friendly to Caecina because he had commanded a company in Germany.
## The Othonianists' suspicions, and the trap at the Castors
2:23 Spurinna learned the enemy's route and wrote to Annius Gallus to tell him that Placentia had been defended, what had happened, and what Caecina was planning. Gallus was leading the 1st legion to help Placentia. He doubted that so few cohorts could hold out against a long siege and the force of the German army. When he heard that Caecina had been driven off and was heading for Cremona, he stopped at Bedriacum, though he could barely hold back the legion, whose eagerness to fight had gone as far as mutiny. Bedriacum is a village between Verona and Cremona, and it has since become notorious for two great disasters for Rome. In those same days Martius Macer won a battle not far from Cremona. Martius was a bold man, and he ferried his gladiators across the Padus in boats and landed them suddenly on the opposite bank. The Vitellianist auxiliaries there were thrown into disorder. The ones who stood their ground were cut down, and the rest fled to Cremona. But the winners' charge was held back, for fear the enemy would get fresh reinforcements and turn the battle around. The Othonianists were suspicious of this, since they put the worst reading on everything their generals did. The more cowardly a man was at heart, the louder his mouth, and they competed at accusing Annius Gallus, Suetonius Paullinus and Marius Celsus (Otho had put those two in command as well) with all sorts of charges. The strongest drivers of mutiny and discord were the murderers of Galba. Crazed by guilt and fear, they threw everything into confusion, now with open, mutinous talk, now with secret letters to Otho. Otho believed the meanest men and was afraid of the good ones, so he was always on edge. He lost his nerve when things went well and held up better in setbacks. In the end he sent for his brother Titianus and put him in charge of the war.
2:24 Meanwhile, Celsus and Paullinus led brilliant successes. Caecina was tormented by the fruitlessness of everything he had tried and by the fading reputation of his army. He had been driven off from Placentia. His auxiliaries had recently been cut up. Even in the clashes between scouts, which were frequent but not worth telling, he had come off worse. With Valens coming closer, Caecina was afraid all the credit of the war would go to him, and he hurried to repair his name, more eagerly than wisely. Twelve miles from Cremona, at a place called the Castors, he stationed his fiercest auxiliaries hidden in the woods that hang over the road. The cavalry were ordered to go forward a good way, and then, after provoking a fight, to retreat on purpose and draw the enemy into a hurried chase, until the hidden men could attack together. The plan was betrayed to the Othonianist generals. Paullinus took command of the infantry and Celsus of the cavalry. A detachment of the 13th legion, four auxiliary cohorts and 500 cavalry were placed on the left. Three Praetorian cohorts in deep ranks held the raised road. On the right front advanced the 1st legion with two auxiliary cohorts and 500 cavalry. Above these, a thousand more horsemen, Praetorian and auxiliary, were brought up to add weight to a victory or to support them if they struggled.
2:25 Before the lines clashed, the Vitellianists turned their backs. Celsus, who understood the trick, held his men back. The Vitellianists broke ranks recklessly, and as Celsus slowly gave ground they chased him too far and rushed into the trap themselves. The auxiliary cohorts hit them on the flanks, the legions faced them head on, and the cavalry swept around and closed the circle behind them. Suetonius Paullinus didn't give the infantry the signal to fight right away. He was slow by nature, and he liked careful, reasoned plans better than success that came by chance. He ordered the ditches filled in, the field cleared and the line spread out, thinking it soon enough to begin winning once he'd made sure they couldn't lose. That delay gave the Vitellianists room to escape into vineyards, which were hard to move through because of the vines tied together, and a small wood lay next to them. From there they dared to come out again and killed the foremost riders of Otho's Praetorian cavalry. King Epiphanes, who was loyally urging the troops on for Otho, was wounded.
2:26 Then the Othonianist infantry charged. The enemy line was crushed, and even the men coming up to help were turned to flight. Caecina hadn't sent in his cohorts together but one at a time, which added to the panic in the battle, since the scattered men were weak everywhere and were swept away by the fear of those running. A mutiny also broke out in the camp because the whole army hadn't been led out. The camp prefect Julius Gratus was put in chains, on suspicion of plotting treason for his brother, who was serving with Otho, since the Othonianists had put that brother, the tribune Julius Fronto, in chains on the same charge. In fact the fear was so great everywhere, among the fugitives and the men running to help, in the battle line and in front of the rampart, that it was commonly said on both sides that Caecina and his whole army could have been wiped out if Suetonius Paullinus hadn't sounded the recall. Paullinus said he was afraid of so much more work and a longer march, in case the Vitellianist soldiers came fresh out of their camp and attacked his worn-out men, and with them thrown into confusion there'd be no reserve behind them. A few people approved of the general's reasoning, but the army at large took it badly.
## Valens and the Batavians' mutiny
2:27 The defeat didn't frighten the Vitellianists so much as make them obedient. That was true not only of Caecina's troops, though Caecina put all the blame on his soldiers and said they were readier for mutiny than for battle. It was true also of the forces of Fabius Valens, who by now had reached Ticinum. They dropped their contempt for the enemy, wanted to make up for it, and obeyed their general with more respect and more evenness. They'd had a serious mutiny, though. It doesn't fit into the story of Caecina's campaign, which I shouldn't have interrupted, so I'll go back and take it up from an earlier point. I've told how the Batavian cohorts, who had split off from the 14th legion during Nero's war and were on their way to Britain, heard about Vitellius's rising and joined Fabius Valens in the country of the Lingones. They acted arrogantly. Whenever they went to any legion's tents, they boasted that they'd held the men of the 14th in check, that they'd taken Italy away from Nero, and that the whole fortune of the war was in their hands. The soldiers found that insulting and their general found it bitter. Discipline was wrecked by arguments and brawls, and in the end Valens suspected that their insolence would turn into treachery.
2:28 So when word reached him that the cavalry wing of the Treveri and the Tungri had been beaten by Otho's fleet and that Gallia Narbonensis was being surrounded, he decided to protect an allied people and also, with a soldier's cunning, to break up cohorts that were troublesome and, if they stayed together, too strong. He ordered part of the Batavians to go to the rescue. When this was heard and became known, the allies were upset and the legions grumbled. They said, "We're being robbed of the help of our bravest men. Those veterans, winners of so many wars, are being pulled away, as if from the battle line, just when the enemy is in sight. If a province matters more than the city and the safety of the empire, we should all follow them there. But if victory's health, its support and pillar, rests in Italy, you can't tear the strongest limbs off the body."
2:29 They kept shouting this defiantly, and when Valens sent in his lictors to put down the mutiny, they attacked him personally, threw stones at him, and chased him as he ran. Shouting that he was hiding the plunder of the Gauls and the gold of the people of Vienna, the pay for their own labors, they tore apart his baggage and probed the general's tent and the very ground with javelins and lances. Valens, meanwhile, was hiding at the quarters of a cavalry officer, dressed as a slave. Then the camp prefect Alfenus Varus, seeing the mutiny dying down little by little, added a plan. He forbade the centurions to go around to the sentries and stopped the trumpet calls that summon soldiers to their duties. So everyone froze and looked around at each other in shock, frightened by the very fact that nobody was in command. With silence, with patience, and finally with prayers and tears, they begged for pardon. And when Valens came out, wretched and weeping, saved when no one expected it, they were moved to joy, pity and even affection. They swung into delight, the way a crowd goes to extremes in either direction. They cheered him and congratulated him, and with the eagles and standards around him they carried him to the platform. With useful self-control he didn't demand punishment for anyone. And to avoid looking more suspicious by pretending nothing happened, he rebuked a few, knowing that in civil wars the soldiers get more freedom than the generals.
## Caecina and Valens join forces
2:30 While they were fortifying a camp at Ticinum, news came of Caecina's setback, and the mutiny nearly started again, since they thought they had missed the battle through Valens's trickery and delays. They wanted no rest, wouldn't wait for their general, marched out in front of the standards, and pushed the standard-bearers along. By a rapid march they joined Caecina. Valens didn't have a good name with Caecina's army. They complained that they'd been left exposed to the enemy's full strength while so much fewer. In saying so they excused themselves and also, by flattery, played up the strength of the newcomers, so that those troops wouldn't look down on them as beaten and cowardly. And although Valens had more men, nearly twice the number of legions and auxiliaries, the soldiers' sympathies still leaned toward Caecina. He was thought quicker to be generous, and he was also younger, tall, and well liked in a rather empty way. So the two commanders were rivals. Caecina mocked Valens as a filthy, disgraceful man, and Valens mocked Caecina as puffed up and vain. But they buried their hatred and pursued the same advantage. They sent letter after letter heaping insults on Otho, with no thought of forgiveness, while the generals on Otho's side, though they had plenty of material for abuse against Vitellius, held back.
2:31 Before the deaths of those two men (and Otho won great honor by his death, as Vitellius won the foulest disgrace by his), people feared Vitellius's lazy comforts less than Otho's burning passions. The murder of Galba had made Otho feared and hated, while no one held it against Vitellius that he'd begun the war. Vitellius disgraced himself by his belly and his gluttony. Otho, with his extravagance, cruelty and recklessness, was thought more dangerous to the state. Once Caecina's and Valens's forces were joined, the Vitellianists had no reason left to put off a battle with their whole force. Otho held a discussion on whether to drag the war out or try his luck. Then Suetonius Paullinus, who thought it fitting for his reputation (no one at that time was considered a more skilled soldier) to give his opinion on the whole way to run the war, argued that haste helped the enemy and delay helped them.
2:32 "Vitellius's whole army has already arrived," he said. "And there isn't much strength behind it, because Gaul is ready to rise, and Vitellius can't afford to leave the Rhine bank with such hostile tribes ready to pour in. The army of Britain is held back by a hostile people and by the sea. Spain isn't overflowing with troops. Gallia Narbonensis was shaken by our fleet's attack and by a defeat. Italy north of the Padus is shut in by the Alps, can't be supplied from the sea, and has been stripped bare by the army marching through. There's no grain for that army anywhere, and an army can't be held together without supplies. And the Germans, the fiercest part of the enemy's force, will weaken and fall apart under the change of land and climate if the war is dragged into summer. Many wars that were terrifying at first have faded away through weariness and delay. We, on the other hand, have everything in abundance and have loyal people. We have Pannonia, Moesia, Dalmatia, and the East with its armies untouched. We have Italy and Rome, the head of the world, the Senate and the people, names that are never dim, even if they're sometimes overshadowed. We have the wealth of the state and of private citizens, and a huge supply of money, which in civil strife is stronger than iron. Our soldiers are used to Italy or to even greater heat. We have the river Padus in front, and cities safe behind strong garrisons and walls, none of which will yield to the enemy, as the defense of Placentia has proved. So let Otho drag the war out. In a few days the 14th legion, already famous, will arrive with the Moesian troops. Then he can think it over again, and if he decides on battle, we'll fight with more strength."
## Otho decides to fight and withdraws to Brixellum
2:33 Marius Celsus agreed with Paullinus. Annius Gallus had been badly hurt a few days earlier in a fall from his horse. The men sent to ask his opinion reported that he agreed too. Otho was leaning toward a decisive fight. His brother Titianus and the Praetorian prefect Proculus were inexperienced and impatient, and they swore that fortune, the gods and Otho's own spirit were with their plans and would be with their efforts. So that nobody would dare to oppose them, they had turned to flattery. Once the decision to fight was made, there was doubt whether it would be better for the emperor to be at the battle or to stay out of it. Paullinus and Celsus stopped arguing, since they didn't want to seem to be putting the emperor in the way of danger. And the same men who had pushed the worse plan, Titianus and Proculus, talked him into withdrawing to Brixellum, out of the dangers of battle, to save himself for the highest matters and for ruling. That day was the first to crush Otho's cause. A strong force of Praetorian cohorts, of his bodyguard and of cavalry went away with him, and the spirit of those who stayed behind was broken, because the soldiers distrusted their generals. Otho, the only man the soldiers trusted, and who himself trusted no one but them, had left the generals' authority on shaky ground.
## The bridge and the fight at the island
2:34 None of this escaped the Vitellianists, since in civil war there are many deserters, and their scouts, busy asking about the other side's plans, didn't hide their own. Caecina and Valens stayed quiet and watchful. The enemy was heading blindly for ruin, and so they waited for other people's blunders, which was their stand-in for wisdom. They began building a bridge, pretending to cross the Padus there, with a force of gladiators drawn up against them. They also wanted to keep their own soldiers from wasting time in idleness. Boats were set at equal distances from one another, joined at both ends by strong beams, and pointed upstream, with anchors thrown out above to hold the bridge steady. The cables were left slack, not tight, so the boats could rise with the river without upsetting the arrangement. A tower stood on the end of the bridge, built out on the last boat, so that engines and machines could drive the enemy back from it. The Othonianists built a tower on the bank and hurled stones and burning torches from it.
2:35 There was an island in the middle of the river. While the gladiators were struggling toward it in boats, the Germans swam ahead of them. By chance quite a few had already crossed when Macer loaded some light galleys with his boldest gladiators and attacked them. But gladiators don't have the steadiness in battle that soldiers have, and on rocking boats they couldn't aim their blows the way men can who stand firm on a bank. As the frightened men swayed this way and that, rowers and fighters got mixed up and thrown into confusion. Then the Germans jumped into the shallows on their own, grabbed the sterns, climbed up the sides, or sank the boats by hand. All this happened in full view of both armies. The more it delighted the Vitellianists, the more fiercely the Othonianists cursed the cause and the author of the disaster.
2:36 The fight ended with the survivors fleeing in the boats that were left. Then they demanded Macer's execution. He had been wounded by a spear thrown from a distance, and the soldiers had rushed him with drawn swords when the tribunes and centurions stepped in and saved him. Soon afterward Vestricius Spurinna, on Otho's orders, joined the army with his cohorts, leaving only a modest garrison at Placentia. Then Otho sent Flavius Sabinus, the consul-elect, to command the troops Macer had led. The soldiers were glad of the change of leaders, and the leaders, because of the constant mutinies, were turning with disgust from such a hateful command.
## Would the armies have chosen peace? Tacitus on civil war
2:37 I find in some authors that either fear of war or disgust at both emperors, whose shame and disgrace were becoming more openly known every day, made the armies hesitate. They wondered whether to put down the fight and either talk it over among themselves or let the Senate pick an emperor. And for that reason, they say, Otho's generals urged waiting, Paullinus most of all, since he had hopes for himself: he was the senior of the ex-consuls and famous as a soldier, and he had earned glory and a name in his campaigns in Britain. I'll grant that a few people, in silent prayer, wanted peace in place of strife, and a good, blameless emperor in place of the worst and most shameful ones. Still, I don't think Paullinus, with all his good sense, hoped to find such restraint in the crowd in a thoroughly corrupt age, that people who had wrecked peace out of love of war would now lay down war out of love of peace. I don't think armies so different in language and customs could have come together in such an agreement. And I don't think the commanders and generals, most of them aware of their own extravagance, poverty and crimes, would have put up with any emperor who wasn't himself stained and tied to them by favors they'd done.
2:38 The old, long-planted human craving for power grew and burst out as the empire grew great. When things were modest in scale, equality was easy to keep. But once the world was conquered and rival cities and kings were wiped out, there was time to lust after wealth that was safe to enjoy, and the first fights between senators and people flared up. Sometimes the tribunes stirred up trouble, sometimes the consuls were too powerful, and the first attempts at civil war were made in the city and the Forum. Soon Gaius Marius, from the lowest of the people, and Lucius Sulla, the cruelest of the nobles, turned the liberty that their arms had beaten into a dictatorship. After them came Gnaeus Pompeius, who hid his character better but was no better. After that the fight was never about anything except supreme power. Armies of citizens didn't lay down their arms even at Pharsalia and Philippi, let alone were the armies of Otho and Vitellius likely to end the war on their own. The same anger of the gods, the same madness of men, the same causes of crime drove them into conflict. That those wars were settled, as it were, by single blows was the fault of the leaders' laziness. But thinking over the old ways and the new has carried me too far. Now I come back to the order of events.
## The Othonianists march out
2:39 After Otho left for Brixellum, the honor of command rested with his brother Titianus, while the real force and power lay with the prefect Proculus. Celsus and Paullinus, since no one used their good judgment, only served as empty names of generals to cover other people's mistakes. The tribunes and centurions were uncertain, because the better men were pushed aside and the worst ones had the influence. The soldiers were eager, but they preferred to second-guess their officers' orders rather than carry them out. They decided to move the camp forward to the fourth milestone from Bedriacum, but they did it so badly that, though it was spring and rivers were everywhere around, the men went short of water. Then they debated whether to fight. Otho kept pressing them in letters to hurry, and the soldiers demanded that the emperor be at the battle. Many asked for the troops across the Padus to be brought up. It isn't as easy to say what would have been best as it is to be sure that what was done was the worst.
2:40 They set out as if for a whole campaign, not for a battle, heading for the place where the Padus and the Arda meet, sixteen miles away. Celsus and Paullinus protested against throwing soldiers tired from the road and weighed down with baggage at an enemy who was sure to attack them. He would be traveling light and would have marched barely four miles, and he would catch them either strung out on the march or scattered and busy digging a rampart. Titianus and Proculus lost the argument and fell back on their right to command. And it was true that a Numidian had arrived at a gallop with an angry message, in which Otho scolded the generals for their slowness and ordered them to bring things to a decision. He was sick of delay and couldn't stand the wait.
## The battle at Bedriacum
2:41 That same day, while Caecina was busy with the work on his bridge, two tribunes of the Praetorian cohorts came to him asking for a conference. He was getting ready to hear their terms and give his own when scouts rushed in to report that the enemy was close. The tribunes' talk was cut off. So it stayed unclear whether they meant deception, treason, or an honorable arrangement. Caecina sent the tribunes away and rode back to camp. There he found that Fabius Valens had given the signal for battle and that the soldiers were under arms. While the legions were drawing lots for their place in the march, the cavalry charged. Strange to say, they were nearly driven back onto the rampart by a smaller Othonianist force, and were stopped only by the courage of the Italian legion, which, with swords drawn, forced the beaten riders to turn back and fight again. The Vitellianist legions formed up calmly, because although the enemy was near, thick underbrush blocked the sight of their weapons. Among the Othonianists the generals were afraid, the soldiers hated their officers, the baggage wagons and camp servants were mixed in with the ranks, and with steep ditches on both sides, the road was too narrow even for a calm march. Some were gathering around their standards, others looking for them. Everywhere there was the confused shouting of men running up and calling to each other. As each man's courage or fear drove him, he pushed to the front or slipped back to the rear.
2:42 Their minds, stunned by the sudden terror, were then lulled into slackness by false joy, when some people turned up who lied that Vitellius's army had gone over. It was never clearly learned whether the rumor was spread by Vitellius's scouts or whether it started on Otho's own side, by trickery or by chance. The Othonianists dropped their fighting spirit and greeted the enemy. They were answered with a hostile roar, and since many on their own side didn't know why the greeting was made, they started to fear treachery. Then the enemy line came down on them with its ranks unbroken, stronger and more numerous. The Othonianists, though scattered, outnumbered and tired, took up the fight fiercely anyway. The ground was cluttered with trees and vines, so the battle took many shapes. They fought close and at a distance, in bands and in wedges. On the raised road they stood foot to foot, shoving with their bodies and shield bosses, and, giving up throwing javelins, they hacked through helmets and breastplates with swords and axes. They knew each other and could be seen by everyone else, and they were fighting to decide the whole war.
2:43 In an open field between the Padus and the road, two legions happened to meet. For Vitellius there was the 21st, called the Rapax, famous for old glory. For Otho there was the 1st, called the Adjutrix, which had never gone into battle before, but was spirited and hungry for its first honor. The men of the 1st cut down the front ranks of the 21st and took their eagle. The 21st, burning with grief at that, drove the 1st back in turn, killed the legate Orfidius Benignus, and seized many flags and standards from the enemy. In another part of the field the 13th legion was driven back by the charge of the 5th, and the 14th was surrounded by greater numbers. Otho's generals had long since run away, and Caecina and Valens were strengthening their men with reserves. A fresh force arrived: Alfenus Varus with his Batavians, who had scattered the band of gladiators, which had been ferried across the river and which the cohorts opposite cut down right in the water. So, flushed with victory, they charged the enemy's flank.
2:44 The center was broken, and the Othonianists fled in all directions toward Bedriacum. The distance was huge and the road was blocked with heaps of bodies. The slaughter was worse because prisoners taken in a civil war bring no profit. Suetonius Paullinus and Licinius Proculus took different routes and avoided the camp. Vedius Aquila, legate of the 13th legion, blindly panicked and ran straight into the angry soldiers. He entered the rampart while it was still day and was surrounded by the roaring of mutineers and runaways. They didn't hold back from insults or from blows. They called him a deserter and a traitor, not with any particular charge against him, but in the way of a mob, each pinning his own crime on someone else. Night helped Titianus and Celsus. By then the sentries were posted and the soldiers calmed, since Annius Gallus had won them over by advice, pleading and his authority not to add to the disaster of defeat by killing one another. He told them that whether the war was over or they chose to take up arms again, the beaten had one comfort only, in standing together. The rest of the army's spirit was broken, but the Praetorian soldiers growled that they'd been beaten not by courage but by treachery. "The Vitellianists didn't win without a price," they said. "Their cavalry was beaten, and a legion lost its eagle. We still have the troops across the Padus, and Otho himself. The Moesian legions are coming. A large part of the army stayed behind at Bedriacum, and those men, at least, have not been beaten. And if it has to be, we'd die more honorably in battle." Out of the utter despair in these thoughts, grim or frightened men were driven on, more often to anger than to fear.
## The surrender
2:45 The Vitellianist army made camp at the fifth milestone from Bedriacum. The generals didn't dare attack the enemy camp that same day. They also hoped for a voluntary surrender. Though they had no baggage and had come out only to fight, their weapons and their victory were protection enough. The next day it was clear what Otho's army wanted, and the angriest men were leaning toward regret. They sent envoys, and Vitellius's generals didn't hesitate to grant peace. The envoys were held for a short while, which made people uneasy, since they didn't yet know whether they'd succeeded. Soon the envoys were sent back and the camp was thrown open. Then winners and losers both burst into tears, cursing the fate of civil war with a miserable joy. In the same tents, some tended the wounds of brothers, others of relatives. Their hopes and rewards were uncertain, but death and grief were sure, and no one had come through so free from misfortune that he didn't mourn someone's death. The body of the legate Orfidius was searched for and cremated with the usual honors. A few were buried by their own relatives. The rest of the crowd was left lying on the ground.
## Otho's last words and death
2:46 Otho was waiting for news of the battle, not at all afraid and firm in his purpose. First came gloomy rumors, then fugitives from the battle revealed that all was lost. The soldiers' eagerness didn't wait for the emperor to speak. They told him to keep his spirits up, that fresh strength remained, and that they themselves would suffer and dare the worst. This wasn't flattery. They were burning with a kind of frenzy and drive to go back into the fight and raise their cause's fortunes. Those standing far off stretched out their hands, and those nearest clutched his knees. Plotius Firmus was the most eager of all. He was prefect of the Praetorian Guard, and he begged Otho again and again not to abandon so loyal an army and soldiers who had served him so well. "It takes more courage," he said, "to bear misfortune than to be rid of it. Brave and determined men hold on to hope even against fortune, and cowards and slackers rush into despair out of fear." Under these words, as Otho softened or hardened his face, there were cheers and groans. And it wasn't only the Praetorians, who were Otho's own soldiers. The men who had been sent ahead from Moesia reported the same stubborn spirit in the army that was coming, and said that the legions had entered Aquileia. So nobody can doubt that the war could have been renewed, terrible, grim, and uncertain for winners and losers alike.
2:47 Otho himself turned away from any plan for war. "I think," he said, "it would be putting too high a price on my life to risk this spirit and courage of yours again. The more hope you show me, if I chose to live, the finer my death will be. Fortune and I know each other by now, and don't count how long. It's harder to keep your balance with good fortune you don't expect to last. The civil war began with Vitellius, and he was the one who first made us fight with weapons over the throne. The example of fighting only once will be mine. Let posterity judge Otho by that. Vitellius can keep his brother, his wife and his children. I need no revenge and no comfort. Others have held the throne longer, but no one has left it so bravely. Am I to let so many young Romans and so many fine armies be cut down again and taken from the state? Let this spirit go with me, as if you were going to die for me, but live on. And let's not put it off any longer, so that I don't get in the way of your safety, nor you of my firmness. To say too much about the end is partly cowardice. Take as the clearest proof of my resolve that I complain about no one. Blaming gods or men is for someone who wants to live."
## Otho's death and burial
2:48 After saying this, he spoke kindly to each man according to his age or rank, urging them to go quickly and not stir up the winner's anger by staying. With the young he used his authority, with the old he pleaded. His face was calm and his words fearless as he held back his friends' ill-timed tears. He ordered boats and carriages given to those leaving. He destroyed the notes and letters that were remarkable for their loyalty to him or their insults against Vitellius. He handed out money, sparingly, and not like a man about to die. Soon he even comforted Salvius Cocceianus, his brother's son, a very young man, who was frightened and grieving, praising his loyalty and scolding his fear. "Do you think Vitellius will be so hard-hearted that he won't even return this favor, when his whole house is safe? By hurrying my end I earn the winner's mercy. It was not in utter despair, but with my army demanding battle, that I gave up the last throw of the dice for the state. I've won enough fame for myself and enough nobility for my descendants. After the Julii, the Claudii and the Servii, I was the first to bring the throne into a new family. So take up life with a brave heart, and never forget that Otho was your uncle, or dwell on it too much."
2:49 After that he sent everyone away and rested a little. He was turning over his last cares in his mind when a sudden uproar pulled him away. He was told of the panic and wild behavior of the soldiers. They were threatening death to any who tried to leave, and were most violent toward Verginius, whose house they had shut and were besieging. Otho scolded the ringleaders of the disturbance, went back, and spent his time speaking with those who were leaving, until all had gone unharmed. As evening came on, he quenched his thirst with a drink of cold water. Two daggers were brought to him. He tested the edge of each, then put one under his head. After making sure his friends had set out, he spent a quiet night, and it is even said that he slept. At first light he fell on the blade with his chest. At the groan of the dying man, his freedmen and slaves came in, along with Plotius Firmus, prefect of the Praetorian Guard. They found one wound. The funeral was hurried. He had begged for this with earnest prayers, so his head wouldn't be cut off and made a mockery. The Praetorian cohorts carried his body with praise and tears, covering his wound and his hands with kisses. Some of the soldiers killed themselves beside the funeral pyre, not from guilt or fear, but out of rivalry for his glory and love of their emperor. Afterward this kind of death became common, without distinction of rank, at Bedriacum, Placentia and the other camps. A plain tomb was built for Otho, and so it was likely to last.
2:50 So Otho ended his life in the 37th year of his age. He came from the town of Ferentium. His father was of consular rank and his grandfather of praetor rank. His mother's family was less distinguished, though not without honor. What his boyhood and youth had been like, I have already shown. By two bold acts, one monstrous and one remarkably noble, he earned about as much good name as bad from later generations. It would be beneath the dignity of the work I've taken on to collect marvels and entertain readers with made-up tales, but I wouldn't dare to discredit what common report and tradition say. The locals tell that on the day the fight was going on at Bedriacum, a bird of an unfamiliar kind settled in a well-visited grove near Regium Lepidum. It wasn't scared off or driven away by the crowds of people or the flocks of birds flying around it, until Otho killed himself. Then it vanished from sight. And when they counted the times, the beginning and end of the wonder matched the end of Otho's life.
## After Otho: Verginius, the cohorts and the senators
2:51 At his funeral the soldiers' mutinous spirit flared up again with grief and pain, and there was no one to hold it back. They turned to Verginius, and with threats they begged him, now to take the throne, now to act as an envoy to Caecina and Valens. Verginius slipped out secretly by the back of his house and cheated them when they burst in. Rubrius Gallus carried the petition of the cohorts that had been at Brixellum, and pardon was granted at once. The troops Flavius Sabinus had commanded gave in to the winner through him.
2:52 With the war over everywhere, a large part of the Senate was in the gravest danger. They had left Rome with Otho and then been left behind at Mutina. News of the lost battle reached them there. But the soldiers refused it as a false rumor. They thought the Senate was hostile to Otho, so they watched what the senators said and read the worst into their looks and manner. At last, with insults and abuse, they looked for a cause and a start for a massacre. The senators were also under another fear: that now that Vitellius's side was so strong, they'd be thought to have been slow to welcome his victory. So, afraid and anxious both ways, they met together, with none of them having a clear plan of his own, each feeling safer in many people's shared guilt. Their worries were piled higher when the council of Mutina offered them weapons and money and called them Conscript Fathers, an ill-timed compliment.
2:53 There was a notable quarrel in which Licinius Caecina attacked Eprius Marcellus for speaking ambiguously. The rest didn't give their opinions, but the name of Marcellus, hated for the memory of his informing and exposed to ill will, had stirred up Caecina, who was still new and only recently admitted to the Senate, to make a name for himself with big enemies. The moderation of the better men broke up the quarrel. They all went back to Bononia to deliberate again, and also hoped for more messengers in the meantime. At Bononia they posted men along the roads to question each newcomer. When one of Otho's freedmen was asked why he had left, he answered that he had his master's last orders. He said Otho was still alive when he left him, but cared only about posterity and had torn himself from the pleasures of life. This stirred their admiration and a shame at asking more, and then everyone's thoughts turned toward Vitellius.
2:54 Vitellius's brother Lucius Vitellius was there at their discussions, and was just offering himself to flatterers, when suddenly Caenus, a freedman of Nero, threw them all into a panic with a vicious lie. He claimed that the 14th legion had arrived, joined with the forces from Brixellum, and cut the winners down, and that the fortunes of the sides had turned. The reason for making this up was to bring Otho's passports, which were being ignored, back into force with happier news. Caenus was rushed off to Rome, but a few days later paid the penalty on Vitellius's order. The senators' danger grew because Otho's soldiers believed what was being said was true. What made it scarier was that leaving Mutina had looked like an official decision to abandon the cause. They didn't meet again as a body, and each looked out for himself, until letters from Fabius Valens arrived and took away their fear. And besides that, the glory of Otho's death made the news travel faster.
## Rome and Italy receive the news
2:55 In Rome, though, there was no alarm. The games of Ceres were watched as usual. When reliable messengers brought word into the theater that Otho was dead and that all the soldiers in the city had sworn loyalty to Vitellius under Flavius Sabinus, the prefect of the city, the people cheered Vitellius. They carried Galba's images around the temples, decorated with laurel and flowers, and piled wreaths in the shape of a burial mound near the Lake of Curtius, on the very spot Galba had stained with his dying blood. In the Senate, all the honors that had been put together under the long reigns of other emperors were voted at once. Praise and thanks were added for the German army, and a delegation was sent to congratulate them. A letter from Fabius Valens to the consuls was read, written in a not unreasonable style, but they liked it better that Caecina, out of modesty, hadn't written at all.
2:56 Italy, meanwhile, was suffering worse and more terrible things than war. Vitellius's soldiers, scattered through the towns and colonies, were stealing, grabbing, and defiling every place with violence and lust. They were greedy or ready to be bought for anything, right or wrong, and spared nothing sacred or ordinary. There were even men who killed their personal enemies disguised as soldiers. The soldiers themselves knew the regions well, and marked out rich farms and wealthy owners for plunder or, if there was resistance, for death. Their commanders were in their power and didn't dare stop them. Caecina was less greedy than ambitious for popularity. Valens was so notorious for his profits and gains that he looked the other way at other people's crimes. Italy's resources had long been worn down, and so great a force of infantry and cavalry, with their violence, losses and wrongs, was hardly bearable.
## Vitellius learns of his victory
2:57 Meanwhile Vitellius, who didn't yet know of his victory, was bringing up what was left of the German army's strength as if for a war still to be fought. He left a few of the old soldiers in winter quarters, and drafts were hurried through Gaul to fill out the names of the legions that stayed behind. The guarding of the Rhine bank was entrusted to Hordeonius Flaccus. Vitellius himself added 8,000 men picked from the army of Britain to his own force. He had gone only a few days' march when he got news of the victory at Bedriacum and of the end of the war with Otho's death. He called an assembly and heaped praise on the soldiers' courage. The army demanded that he give his freedman Asiaticus the rank of Roman Knight, and he checked the disgraceful flattery. Then, with his usual fickleness, in the privacy of a dinner party he handed out what he had refused in public, and honored Asiaticus with the rings. Asiaticus was a foul slave, eager for power by crooked means.
2:58 About the same time word came to Vitellius that both Mauretanias had come over to his side and that the procurator Albinus had been killed. Lucceius Albinus had been put in charge of Mauretania Caesariensis by Nero, and Galba had added the governing of the province of Tingitana. He had a force that couldn't be sneered at: nineteen cohorts, five cavalry wings, and a huge number of Moors, a band suited to war by robbery and raiding. When Galba was killed, he leaned toward Otho. Africa wasn't enough for him, and he threatened Spain, which is separated from it only by a narrow strait. That frightened Cluvius Rufus, who ordered the 10th legion to move up to the shore as if he were about to cross. He sent centurions ahead to win the Moors over to Vitellius. That wasn't hard, since the fame of the German army was great in the provinces. On top of that, a story was spread that Albinus, scorning the title of procurator, was taking the badges of a king and the name Juba.
2:59 With feelings changed, Asinius Pollio, a cavalry prefect and one of Albinus's most loyal men, and Festus and Scipio, prefects of two cohorts, were killed. Albinus himself, as he was going from the province of Tingitana to Mauretania Caesariensis, was butchered when his ship touched the shore. His wife threw herself in front of the killers and was killed with him. Vitellius made no inquiry into any of this. He got through even big matters with a short hearing, and was not up to serious cares. He ordered the army to go by land and himself went down the river Arar. His travel had no imperial show about it and was marked by his old poverty, until Junius Blaesus, governor of Gallia Lugdunensis, a man of famous family, generous in spirit and as rich as he was generous, surrounded the emperor with attendants and escorted him freely. By that very thing he made himself unwelcome, though Vitellius veiled his hatred with slavish flattery. At Lugdunum the leaders of the winning and losing sides were waiting for him. He praised Valens and Caecina before an assembly and seated them on either side of his chair of office. Then he ordered the whole army to come out and meet his infant son. He had the boy brought, wrapped in a general's cloak, held him on his lap, called him Germanicus, and surrounded him with all the signs of imperial fortune. It was too much honor for good times, but it served as comfort in bad.
## Reprisals and rewards; the prophet Mariccus
2:60 Then the boldest centurions among the Othonianists were put to death. That, more than anything, turned the Illyrian armies against Vitellius. At the same time the other legions, infected by the example and by envy of the German soldiers, were thinking of war. Vitellius held Suetonius Paullinus and Licinius Proculus in gloomy, squalid delay until, when they were heard, they used a defense that was necessary rather than honorable. They claimed treason as a credit to themselves, blaming on their own double-dealing the long march before the battle, the exhaustion of Otho's troops, the line tangled up with wagons, and many things that were really accidents. Vitellius believed their treachery and acquitted them of loyalty. Salvius Titianus, Otho's brother, never faced any danger, since his brotherly devotion and his laziness were taken as excuses. Marius Celsus kept his consulship. But it was widely believed, and was later thrown in Caecilius Simplex's face in the Senate, that Simplex had tried to buy that office with money, and with it Celsus's death. Vitellius refused, and later gave Simplex a consulship that didn't have to be bought with crime or money. Trachalus was protected from his accusers by Galeria, Vitellius's wife.
2:61 Amid the crises of great men, it is embarrassing to tell how a man named Mariccus, from the common people of the Boii, dared to shove himself into the game of fortune and challenge Rome's arms by pretending to be divine. Already he was calling himself the champion of Gaul and a god (for that was the title he'd given himself), and had gathered 8,000 men and was pulling in the nearest villages of the Aedui. Then that most serious state attacked him with a picked force of its young men, joined by some cohorts Vitellius added, and scattered the crowd of fanatics. Mariccus was captured in that fight. Soon he was thrown to wild beasts, and because they didn't tear him up, the foolish crowd believed he couldn't be hurt, until he was killed in the presence of Vitellius.
## Vitellius' feasting and first edicts
2:62 After that there was no further cruelty against the other side or against anyone's property. The wills of those who had fallen in Otho's army were honored, and for those who died without wills the law was followed. In fact, if Vitellius could have reined in his love of luxury, no one would have had to fear his greed. He had a scandalous, insatiable passion for feasts. Delicacies were carried to him from the city and from Italy until the roads from both seas rang with traffic. The leading men of the states were drained by the cost of preparing his banquets, and the states themselves were ruined. The soldiers lost their drive and courage through the habit of pleasure and contempt for their leader. He sent an edict ahead to the city. In it he put off the title of Augustus, refused the name of Caesar, and took nothing away from his actual power. The astrologers were driven out of Italy. Under severe penalties, Roman Knights were forbidden to disgrace themselves in gladiator schools and the arena. Earlier emperors had pushed that with money, and more often with force, and many towns and colonies competed to lure the most corrupt young men with pay.
2:63 But Vitellius, after his brother arrived and as teachers of tyranny crept in on him, became prouder and crueler. He ordered Dolabella killed, the man Otho had sent off to the colony of Aquinum, as I told. After hearing of Otho's death, Dolabella had come into the city. Plancius Varus, a former praetor and one of Dolabella's close friends, accused him of this before Flavius Sabinus, the prefect of the city. He said Dolabella had broken out of custody and offered himself as leader of the beaten side. He added that the cohort stationed at Ostia had been tampered with. For all these huge charges he offered no proof at all. Then he changed his mind and, after the crime, sought a pardon that came too late. Flavius Sabinus hesitated over so serious a matter. Then Triaria, the wife of Lucius Vitellius, who was fierce beyond what you'd expect of a woman, frightened him: she warned him not to chase a name for mercy at the emperor's risk. Sabinus was gentle by nature, but when fear came on him, he was easily swayed. Frightened for himself in another man's danger, and not wanting to seem to have helped him, he pushed the falling man over.
2:64 So Vitellius, out of fear and hatred, since Dolabella had married his former wife Petronia, summoned him by letter. He ordered him to avoid the crowded Flaminian Road, turn aside to Interamna, and be killed there. The executioner found that too slow. He threw his prisoner on the ground on the road at an inn and cut his throat. It brought great hatred on the new reign, and it was seen as its first sample. Triaria's recklessness was made harder to bear by the decent example close by: Galeria, the emperor's wife, took no part in these cruelties, and neither did Sextilia, the mother of the two Vitellii, a woman of the same decency and of the old school. Sextilia is even said to have remarked, on reading her son's first letters, that she had given birth to Vitellius, not to a Germanicus. And no later temptation of fortune or flattery from the state ever swept her into joy. She felt only her house's misfortunes.
## Cluvius Rufus and the provinces
2:65 Marcus Cluvius Rufus, who had left Spain, caught up with Vitellius after he had left Lugdunum. He wore a look of joy and congratulation, but in his heart he was anxious, knowing he had been attacked with accusations. Hilarus, a freedman of the emperor, had reported that when Cluvius heard Vitellius and Otho were both claiming the throne, he had tried to win power for himself and hold Spain, and that was why he'd put no emperor's name at the head of his passports. Hilarus also presented some passages from Cluvius's speeches as insults to Vitellius, meant to win Cluvius popularity. But Cluvius's influence prevailed so far that Vitellius ordered his own freedman punished. Cluvius was added to the emperor's retinue, and Spain was not taken from him. He governed it while away, on the model of Lucius Arruntius. But Tiberius Caesar kept Arruntius at home out of fear, while Vitellius kept Cluvius with no fear at all. Trebellius Maximus didn't get the same honor. He had fled Britain because of the soldiers' anger, and Vettius Bolanus, one of those with the emperor at the time, was sent to replace him.
2:66 Vitellius was troubled by the spirit of the beaten legions, which was not at all broken. They were scattered across Italy and mixed in with the winners, and they talked like enemies. The soldiers of the 14th legion were especially fierce. They denied they'd been beaten, saying that at the battle of Bedriacum only the detachments were beaten and the legion's real strength wasn't there. It was decided to send them back to Britain, from which Nero had summoned them, and that in the meantime one of the Batavian cohorts should camp with them, because of the old feud between them and the 14th. With such hatred between armed men, peace didn't last long. At Augusta of the Taurini, a Batavian was chasing some craftsman as a cheat, and a legionary took the man's side as his host. Each one's comrades gathered around him, and from shouting they went to killing. A savage battle would have broken out if two Praetorian cohorts hadn't taken the side of the 14th, giving them confidence and making the Batavians afraid. Vitellius then ordered the Batavians attached to his own force as a reward for their loyalty, and ordered the legion to cross the Graian Alps and take a route that avoided Vienna, since the people of Vienna were also under suspicion. On the night the legion left, part of the colony of Taurina was burned by the fires left behind all over. As with most of the evils of war, that loss was forgotten in the greater disasters of other cities. When the 14th came down from the Alps, the most mutinous among them carried their standards toward Vienna. The agreement of the better men held them back, and the legion was shipped across to Britain.
2:67 Vitellius's next fear was the Praetorian cohorts. They were first separated, then, with the soothing offer of an honorable discharge, told to hand their weapons over to their tribunes. But as the war started by Vespasian grew, they took up service again and became the backbone of the Flavianist cause. The 1st legion of marines was sent to Spain, so that in the peace and quiet there it would calm down. The 11th and 7th were sent back to their winter quarters. The men of the 13th were ordered to build amphitheaters, since Caecina at Cremona and Valens at Bononia were getting ready to put on gladiator shows. Vitellius was never so absorbed in the cares of ruling that he forgot his pleasures.
## Riot at Ticinum, and the army cut down
2:68 He had quietly broken up the beaten side, but among the winners a mutiny started. It began as a game, but the number of dead added to the ill will against Vitellius. Vitellius was sitting at a banquet at Ticinum and had invited Verginius as his guest. Legates and tribunes copy the character of their emperors. They either imitate his strictness or enjoy early banquets, and the soldiers likewise are either attentive or lax. Around Vitellius everything was disorder and drunkenness, more like a night of revels than a camp with discipline. So two soldiers, one from the 5th legion and the other a Gallic auxiliary, were playfully egged into a wrestling match. When the legionary was thrown and the Gaul jeered at him, the crowd that had gathered to watch split into sides, and the legionaries charged out and killed two auxiliary cohorts. The cure for the riot was another riot. A cloud of dust and the glint of weapons showed in the distance, and someone shouted that the 14th legion had turned back and was coming to attack. But it was the rear guard of the column. They were recognized, and the worry went away. Meanwhile a slave of Verginius happened to run into them and was accused of being Vitellius's would-be assassin. The soldiers rushed to the banquet, demanding Verginius's death. Even Vitellius, though he was frightened of every suspicion, had no doubt of his innocence. Still, he could hardly hold back the men who were demanding the death of an ex-consul who had once been their own general. No one was threatened by more outbursts than Verginius. He was still admired and still famous, but they hated him like people who feel looked down on.
2:69 The next day Vitellius heard the Senate's delegation, which he'd ordered to wait for him there. Then he went on to the camp and actually praised the soldiers' loyalty highly. The auxiliaries grumbled that the legionaries were being given so much freedom from punishment and so much arrogance. The Batavian cohorts were sent back to Germany, so they wouldn't dare anything more violent. Fate was preparing the occasions for civil war and war abroad at once. The Gallic auxiliaries were sent back to their states, a huge number, taken on in the very first stage of the revolt for empty show. Besides this, so that the empire's resources, already drained by handouts, would stretch far enough, he ordered the numbers of the legions and auxiliaries cut and forbade recruiting. Discharges were offered to anyone. This was ruinous for the state and unpopular with the soldier, who now found the same duties spread among fewer men, so that hard work and danger came around more often. Their strength was also eaten away by luxury, against the old discipline and the customs of their ancestors, under whom Rome stood firmer on courage than on money.
## Vitellius tours the field of Bedriacum
2:70 Then Vitellius turned toward Cremona. After watching the gladiator show Caecina put on, he developed a longing to stand on the plains of Bedriacum and look with his own eyes at the traces of his recent victory. It was a foul and horrifying sight. Not forty days had passed since the battle, and there lay mangled bodies, severed limbs, the rotting shapes of men and horses, the ground soaked with decay, and trees and crops trampled down into a dreadful wasteland. The part of the road that the people of Cremona had covered with laurel and roses was just as ghastly. They had built altars and sacrificed victims in the manner of a king. The celebration was pleasant for the moment, and soon proved their ruin. Valens and Caecina were there and pointed out the places where the fighting had been. Here the column of legions had burst in, there the cavalry had risen up, over there the bands of auxiliaries had swung around. The tribunes and prefects, each praising his own deeds, mixed lies and truth and exaggeration together. The common soldiers also turned off the road with shouts and joy, to recognize the stretches of the fighting and look in amazement at the heap of weapons and the piles of bodies. There were some who were moved to tears and pity by the shifting turns of fortune. But Vitellius didn't turn his eyes away or shudder at so many thousands of unburied citizens. Instead he was delighted, and unaware of how close his own fate was, he held a sacrifice to the gods of the place.
2:71 Next, Fabius Valens put on a gladiator show at Bononia, with all the equipment brought in from Rome. The closer Vitellius got to Rome, the looser his march became, with actors and crowds of eunuchs mixed in, and all the other habits of Nero's court. For Vitellius used to show his admiration for Nero himself, and used to follow him around when he sang, not under compulsion like all the most honorable men, but bought and owned by luxury and fattening. To open months for Valens and Caecina to hold the consulship, the terms of others were cut short. Martius Macer's was passed over, as he had been a general on Otho's side. Valerius Marinus, whom Galba had named consul, was put off, with no offense on his part, only because he was mild and would take an injury meekly. Pedanius Costa was left out. The emperor disliked him for having dared to act against Nero and for pushing Verginius into action, though other reasons were given. And in the end, out of the habit of servility, thanks were voted to Vitellius.
## A false Scribonianus Camerinus
2:72 However sharp its beginning, a lie started now lasted only a few days. A man had appeared who claimed to be Scribonianus Camerinus. He said that, out of fear of Nero's times, he had hidden in Histria, because the dependents, the estates and the popular name of the old Crassi family still survived there. He took all the worst of his followers into the secret of the story, and the gullible crowd and some of the soldiers, either from not knowing the truth or from love of upheaval, rushed to join him. But when he was dragged before Vitellius and asked who on earth he was, nobody believed his answers, and his owner recognized him as a runaway slave named Geta. He was punished the way slaves are.
2:73 It's hard to believe how much arrogance and laziness grew in Vitellius after scouts from Syria and Judea reported that the East had sworn loyalty to him. The reports were still vague and uncertain, but Vespasian was on people's lips and in rumor, and Vitellius was often stirred up at his name. Now he and his army, with no rival in sight, broke out into cruelty, lust, plundering, and foreign ways.
## Vespasian weighs the risks
2:74 Vespasian, meanwhile, was sizing up the war, the arms, and the forces, near and far. His soldiers were so ready that when he led the oath and prayed for every blessing on Vitellius, they listened in silence. Mucianus's attitude wasn't hostile to Vespasian and leaned even more toward Titus. Tiberius Alexander, the prefect of Egypt, had joined in the plans. He counted the 3rd legion as his own because it had moved from Syria to Moesia, and the other Illyrian legions were expected to follow. For the whole army had been set aflame by the arrogance of the soldiers who came from Vitellius, who were fierce in body and rough in speech and sneered at everyone else as inferiors. But in so huge a war there is usually delay. Vespasian, high in hope one moment, sometimes thought through the bad outcomes. What a day it would be when, at sixty, with his two young sons, he'd hand himself over to war! In private ventures, he thought, a man can go forward and take more or less from fortune, as he likes, but for those who want the throne, there's nothing between the heights and the cliff.
2:75 The strength of the German army was always in front of his eyes, well known to a military man. His own legions had no experience of civil war, he thought, while Vitellius's legions were winners, and among the beaten there were more complaints than strength. Loyalty was shaky because of the soldiers' quarrels, and there was danger from individuals. What good would cohorts and cavalry be if one man or another could take, by an immediate crime, the prize the other side would pay for it? That was how Scribonianus was killed under Claudius, and how his killer Volaginius was raised from the ranks to the highest military post. It was easier to sway whole armies than to avoid a single assassin.
## Mucianus urges Vespasian to take the throne
2:76 These fears left him wavering, but other legates and friends steadied him, and so did Mucianus, who, after many private talks, now also spoke before them all. "Everyone who takes up plans for great matters," he said, "should ask whether what's being started helps the state, brings them glory, is easy to carry out, or at least not very hard. And the one giving the advice has to be looked at too: does he add his own risk to his counsel, and if fortune favors the attempt, who gets the highest honor? I call you, Vespasian, to the throne. How good it would be for the state, how splendid for you, lies, next to the gods, in your own hand. And don't be afraid that this looks like flattery. Being chosen after Vitellius is closer to an insult than to praise. We're not rising against the sharpest mind of the divine Augustus, or Tiberius's most careful old age, or even against the house of Gaius, Claudius or Nero, rooted in long rule. You even gave way to Galba's family portraits. To stay asleep any longer and leave the state to be fouled and destroyed would look like sloth and cowardice, even if slavery were as safe for you as it is shameful. The time when you could seem not to have wanted the throne is gone and past. The throne is now your only refuge. Have you forgotten Corbulo's murder? He was of a more splendid family than ours, I admit, but Nero was nobler by birth than Vitellius. Anyone who is feared is famous enough to the man who fears him. And that an army can make an emperor, Vitellius himself is the proof, with no service record, no military fame, lifted up by hatred of Galba. Not even Otho, who was beaten not by any general's skill or enemy strength but by his own rush to despair, has been spared by Vitellius: he's made Otho look like a great emperor, already missed. Meanwhile Vitellius scatters his legions, disarms his cohorts, and hands out new seeds of war every day. Whatever fire and spirit the soldier had is being worn away in taverns and drinking parties and in copying their emperor. You have nine fresh legions from Judea, Syria and Egypt, worn out by no battle, spoiled by no mutiny, soldiers toughened by experience and masters of foreign war. You have strong fleets, cavalry and cohorts, the most loyal kings, and more experience than anyone else."
2:77 "For myself I'll claim nothing more than not to rank behind Valens and Caecina. But don't spurn Mucianus as a partner just because you don't find a rival in him. I rate myself above Vitellius, and you above myself. Your house has the name of a triumph and two young sons, one already fit for the throne and famous, in his first years of service, even with the German armies. It would be ridiculous for me, if I were emperor myself, not to step aside for the man whose son I'd adopt. Between us, though, success and failure won't be divided in the same way. If we win, I'll take whatever honor you give me. The danger and risk we'll share equally. Better yet, you command your armies, and hand me the war and the uncertainties of battle. Today there's stricter discipline among the beaten than among the winners. Anger, hatred and the wish for revenge fire the beaten to courage, while the winners go dull from contempt and stubbornness. The war itself will uncover and open up the hidden, swelling wounds of the winning side. And I'm no more confident because of your watchfulness, thrift and wisdom than because of Vitellius's laziness, ignorance and cruelty. But once we're at war, our cause is better than it could ever be in peace, because those who deliberate about revolt have already revolted."
## Omens for Vespasian
2:78 After Mucianus's speech the others crowded around Vespasian with more boldness, urging him on and recalling the answers of prophets and the movements of the stars. He wasn't free of such superstition himself, since later, as master of the world, he openly kept an astrologer named Seleucus as his adviser and forecaster. Old omens came back to his mind. A cypress on his land, remarkable for its height, had suddenly fallen, and the next day rose again from the same spot, taller and broader, and was green. The diviners agreed it was a great and favorable sign, and that the highest fame was promised to Vespasian when he was quite a young man. At first the honors of a triumph, the consulship and the glory of his Jewish victories seemed to have fulfilled the omen. When he had won those, he began to believe it meant the throne. Between Judea and Syria is Carmel. That's the name of both the mountain and the god. Their ancestors handed it down that the god has no statue or temple, only an altar and reverence. There Vespasian was sacrificing, turning his secret hopes over in his mind, when the priest Basilides examined the entrails again and again and said: "Whatever it is you're planning, Vespasian, whether to build a house, to enlarge your fields, or to add to your slaves, you're being given a great dwelling, huge boundaries, and many men." Rumor had caught up these puzzling words at once and now spelled them out. Nothing was more often on the lips of the crowd. In Vespasian's own presence there was even more talk, since more is said to those who hope.
## Vespasian hailed as emperor
2:79 With their purpose no longer in doubt, they parted: Mucianus for Antioch, Vespasian for Caesarea, the first the capital of Syria and the second of Judea. The move to hand the empire to Vespasian began in Alexandria, under Tiberius Alexander's hurried action. He made the legions swear loyalty to Vespasian on July 1st. That day was celebrated afterward as the first day of his reign, though the army of Judea swore in front of Vespasian himself on July 3rd, with such enthusiasm that they wouldn't even wait for his son Titus, who was on his way back from Syria as the go-between for Mucianus and his father. It was all done by the soldiers' impulse, with no formal assembly and without the legions brought together.
2:80 While they were looking for the right time and place, and, what is hardest in such a business, someone to speak first, with hope and fear and the chances of success and failure going through their minds, Vespasian came out of his room one day. A few soldiers standing there in the usual way, to greet him as their legate, hailed him as emperor. Then the others ran up and piled on the names Caesar and Augustus and all the titles of the throne. Their minds had gone from fear to confidence in fortune. In Vespasian himself there was nothing swollen or arrogant, nothing changed by the change in his situation. As soon as he'd shaken off the mist that so great a height had spread over his eyes, he spoke to them like a soldier and took in the good news pouring in from all sides. This was exactly what Mucianus had been waiting for, and he swore the eager soldiers in to Vespasian at once. Then he went into the theater at Antioch, where the people customarily hold their discussions. They crowded in and poured out flattery, and he spoke to them. He was fairly polished even in Greek, and in everything he said or did he had a certain skill at showing himself off. Nothing stirred up the provincials and the army like Mucianus's claim that Vitellius had decided to move the German legions to Syria, for easy and rewarding duty, while the Syrian legions would be sent in exchange to the German winter camps, which were harsh in climate and work. The provincials, from long habit, enjoyed living alongside the soldiers, and many were tied to them by friendship or family. And the soldiers, after many years of service, loved the familiar camps like their own home.
## The East lines up
2:81 Before July 15th all of Syria was under the same oath. Sohemus joined with his kingdom, no small force, and so did Antiochus, with his huge ancestral wealth, the richest of the subject kings. Soon Agrippa, called away from Rome by secret messages from his people while Vitellius still knew nothing, was hurrying across the sea. Queen Berenice backed the cause with no less spirit. She was in the bloom of youth and beauty, and pleasing even to old Vespasian by the magnificence of her gifts. All the provinces washed by the sea as far as Asia and Achaia, and all the country inland toward Pontus and Armenia, swore the oath. But the legates of those provinces had no troops, since Cappadocia hadn't yet been given legions. A council on the whole conduct of the war was held at Berytus. Mucianus came there with legates and tribunes and the most distinguished centurions and soldiers, and from the army of Judea came picked men of high standing. Such a gathering of cavalry and infantry, and of kings competing with each other in display, gave the look of an emperor's fortune.
2:82 The first concern of the war was to raise troops and call back veterans. Strong cities were assigned to make weapons. At Antioch gold and silver were coined. All of this was pushed forward in its proper place by suitable agents. Vespasian himself went around, urged them on, praised the good, spurred the lazy by example more than by force, and overlooked his friends' faults more than their virtues. Many he honored with prefectures and procuratorships, and a good number with senatorial rank, all of them outstanding men who soon reached the top. For some, luck took the place of merit. Mucianus, in his first speech, had held out only a modest donative to the soldiers. Vespasian didn't offer more in civil war than others had in peacetime, and was remarkably firm against paying the soldiers off, and so he had a better army. Envoys went to Parthia and Armenia, and care was taken that, with the legions turned to civil war, their backs wouldn't be left bare. It was arranged that Titus would press on in Judea and Vespasian would hold the gates of Egypt. Against Vitellius, part of the forces, Mucianus as commander, Vespasian's name, and the fact that nothing is hard for fate seemed enough. Letters went out to all the armies and legates, with instructions to invite the Praetorians, who were hostile to Vitellius, with the prize of renewed service.
2:83 Mucianus set out with a light force, acting more like a colleague of the emperor than his servant. He went neither slowly, so as not to seem to hesitate, nor in a hurry, and so he let rumor grow stronger with the distance. He knew his force was modest and that people believe bigger things of those they can't see. Behind him, though, came the 6th legion and 13,000 men from the detachments in a huge column. He had ordered the fleet to be brought from the Pontus to Byzantium. He hadn't made up his mind whether to bypass Moesia and move on Dyrrachium with his infantry and cavalry while his warships closed the sea against Italy. That would leave Asia and Achaia safe behind him, but they had no troops and would be at Vitellius's mercy unless garrisoned. And Vitellius himself, with enemy fleets threatening Brundisium, Tarentum and the shores of Calabria and Lucania, wouldn't know which part of Italy to defend.
2:84 So the provinces rang with the preparation of ships, soldiers and weapons. But nothing wore people down like the hunt for money. Mucianus kept saying that money was the sinews of a civil war. In his hearings he looked not at right or truth but only at the size of a man's wealth. Informers were everywhere, and the richest men were picked out for plunder. These measures were heavy and unbearable, but excused by the necessity of war, and they stayed on even in peacetime. Vespasian himself, at the start of his reign, was not so stubborn about keeping the injustices up, until indulgence from fortune and bad teachers taught him, and he dared. Mucianus helped the war with his own money too. He was generous with his private funds because he took from the state more greedily. The others followed his example in giving money, but very few had the same freedom in getting it back.
## The Illyrian legions rise
2:85 Meanwhile Vespasian's enterprise was speeded up by the eagerness of the Illyrian armies, which had gone over to his side. The 3rd legion set the example for the other legions of Moesia. These were the 8th and the 7th (Claudius's), which were steeped in favor for Otho though they had not been at the battle. They had advanced to Aquileia and acted like enemies: roughly shoving aside those who brought news about Otho, tearing up the flags that bore Vitellius's name, and in the end seizing the money and dividing it among themselves. Fear followed, and from fear a plan: that what needed excusing to Vitellius could be credited to Vespasian. So the three Moesian legions tried by letters to win over the Pannonian army, or prepared force if it refused. In the midst of this upheaval Aponius Saturninus, governor of Moesia, dared a terrible crime. He sent a centurion to kill Tettius Julianus, legate of the 7th legion, over a personal feud, which he dressed up as party zeal. Julianus, learning of the danger, took guides who knew the country and fled through the trackless parts of Moesia beyond Mount Haemus. He took no further part in the civil war, dragging out a journey to Vespasian that he'd set out on, with various delays, hurrying or lingering according to the news.
2:86 In Pannonia, though, the 13th legion and the 7th (Galba's), still holding on to their pain and anger over the battle at Bedriacum, joined Vespasian without hesitation, mostly through the force of Primus Antonius. This man had broken the law and been convicted of fraud in Nero's time. Among the other evils of the war, he had won back his senatorial rank. Galba had put him in command of the 7th legion, and he was widely believed to have written often to Otho, offering himself as a general to that side. Slighted by Otho, he had no part in the Othonianist war. When Vitellius's cause began to totter, he followed Vespasian and added great weight to it. He was vigorous in action, ready with words, an artist at stirring up hatred against others, powerful in strife and rebellion, a grabber and a spender, the worst in peace and in war not to be despised. The armies of Moesia and Pannonia, once united, pulled the soldiers of Dalmatia along, though the consular legates did nothing to stir things up. Tampius Flavianus held Pannonia and Pompeius Silvanus Dalmatia, both rich old men. But there was a procurator, Cornelius Fuscus, in the prime of life and of famous family. In his early youth, love of quiet had led him to give up his senatorial rank. Later he led his own colony for Galba, and through that service got the procuratorship. When he took Vespasian's side, he carried the fiercest torch for the war. He took pleasure not so much in the rewards of danger as in danger itself, and preferred the new, the uncertain and the risky to what was certain and long held. So he and Antonius set about shaking and stirring up every weak spot anywhere. Letters were written to the 14th legion in Britain and the 1st in Spain, because both of those legions had been for Otho and against Vitellius. Letters were also scattered through the Gauls, and in a moment a huge war was blazing, with the Illyrian armies in open revolt and the rest ready to follow fortune.
## Vitellius moves on Rome
2:87 While Vespasian and his side's leaders were doing all this in the provinces, Vitellius was growing more contemptible and lazier by the day. He stopped to enjoy the charms of every town and villa along the way, and came toward the city with a heavy column. Sixty thousand armed men followed him, spoiled by license. The number of camp servants was even bigger, with a cheek that was the worst even among slaves. A retinue of so many legates and friends would have been hard to keep in order even under the strictest discipline. The crowd was made heavier by senators and Roman Knights who came out from the city to meet him, some from fear, many from flattery, and the rest, little by little all of them, so as not to be left behind while others set out. From the worthless lower classes came buffoons, actors and charioteers, known to Vitellius from their shameful services. He took a strange delight in such disgraceful friendships. Not only the colonies and towns were drained by having to supply this crowd, but the very farmers and their fields, with the harvest ripening, were plundered like enemy ground.
2:88 There were many savage killings among the soldiers themselves. After the mutiny at Ticinum the rivalry between legions and auxiliaries lingered, though they pulled together whenever they had to fight country people. But the worst slaughter came at the seventh milestone from the city. There Vitellius was dividing prepared food among the soldiers, one by one, like the feeding of gladiators. The common people had poured out and mixed in with the whole camp. With a slave's homegrown city wit, some of them robbed the careless soldiers, cutting their belts in secret and asking whether they were armed. The soldiers, unused to being insulted, couldn't stand the joke. They drew their swords and attacked the unarmed people. Among those killed was the father of a soldier, who was with his son. Later he was recognized, and when the killing became known, they held back from the innocent. In the city, though, there was panic, with soldiers running ahead in all directions. They headed mostly for the Forum, wanting to see the place where Galba had lain. The men themselves were no less frightening to look at, bristling with animal skins and huge spears. In their ignorance of the place, they kept bumping into the crowd, and where the street was slippery or someone ran into them and they fell, they went from arguing to fists and swords. The tribunes and prefects too went flying about with terrifying bands of armed men.
2:89 Vitellius himself, on a showy horse, in a general's cloak and sword, came from the Mulvian bridge, driving the Senate and the people in front of him. His friends' advice scared him off from entering as if the city had been captured. He put on a civilian toga and moved forward with his army in order. The eagles of four legions were in front, with the same number of flags from other legions on either side. Next came the standards of twelve cavalry wings, and behind the infantry ranks, the cavalry. Then came thirty-four cohorts, sorted by the names of nations or the kind of weapons. In front of the eagles were the camp prefects, the tribunes and the chief centurions in white, and the others each beside his own company, glittering with arms and decorations. The soldiers' medals and neck-chains shone. It was a handsome sight, and an army not worthy of an emperor like Vitellius. So he went up to the Capitol, and there he embraced his mother and honored her with the name Augusta.
## His speech and the Senate
2:90 The next day, as if speaking to the Senate and people of some other country, he delivered a grand speech about himself, praising his own energy and self-control. The very people who were there knew about his shameful acts, and so did all of Italy, through which he had traveled in a shameful haze of sleep and luxury. Still, the crowd, free of any worries and unable to tell truth from falsehood, had learned the usual flattery, and applauded with shouts and cries. When he held back from the name Augustus, they pushed him to take it, a gesture as empty as his earlier refusal.
2:91 The city, ready to read meaning into everything, took it as a gloomy omen that Vitellius, once he became chief priest, issued a proclamation on public ceremonies for July 18th, a day marked since ancient times as unlucky by the disasters at the Cremera and the Allia. He was as ignorant of all law, human and divine, as he could be, and with freedmen and friends just as careless, he lived as if among drunks. But he held the consular elections in a civil way, standing with the candidates like an ordinary citizen, and he chased every scrap of the lowest crowd's talk, as a spectator in the theater and as a fan at the races. All that would have been pleasant and popular if it came from good qualities, but with the memory of his earlier life it was taken as tasteless and cheap. He kept coming to the Senate, even when only small matters were up for debate. Once Helvidius Priscus, praetor-elect, spoke against his wishes. Vitellius was upset at first, but went no further than calling on the tribunes of the people to back his insulted authority. Then his friends, who feared his anger ran deeper, tried to calm him. He answered that it was nothing new for two senators to disagree in a free republic, and that he himself used to oppose Thrasea. Most of them laughed at the nerve of the comparison, though some were pleased that he had picked, not one of the most powerful men of the day, but Thrasea as his model of true glory.
## Caecina, Valens, and the soldiers in the city
2:92 Vitellius had put Publilius Sabinus, a cohort prefect, and Julius Priscus, who was then a centurion, in command of the Praetorian Guard. Valens's favor lifted Priscus and Caecina's lifted Sabinus. Between two men at odds, Vitellius had no authority. The duties of the throne were carried out by Caecina and Valens. Their hatreds were old. They had been badly hidden in the war and the camp, and were made worse by their corrupt friends and by a city that breeds quarrels. In their scramble for popularity, their retinues and their huge crowds of callers, they competed and were compared, while Vitellius's favor swung now to one, now to the other. Power is never very safe where it's excessive. Vitellius himself, who swung between sudden anger and ill-timed affection, they scorned and feared at once. Still, that didn't make them any slower to seize houses, gardens and the wealth of the empire, while a sorry, needy crowd of nobles, whom Galba had brought back to their country along with their children, got no help from the emperor's pity. By an edict that pleased the leading men and also won the approval of the people, Vitellius gave back to the returned exiles their rights over their freedmen. Servile cleverness tried every way to undo the favor, hiding money in hidden or influential pockets, and some freedmen moved into Caesar's household and became more powerful than their own masters.
2:93 Meanwhile the soldiers, with the camp full and overflowing, wandered through porticoes, temples and the whole city. They didn't know their headquarters, stood no watches, and didn't toughen themselves by work. With the pleasures of the city and shameful things not worth mentioning, they wore down their bodies with idleness and their minds with lusts. Finally, careless even of their health, a large part of them camped in the unhealthy parts of the Vatican, and so there were many deaths among the troops. The Tiber was next to them, and the Germans' and Gauls' greed for the water and impatience with the heat broke down bodies that were prone to disease. On top of that, the order of the service was thrown into confusion by crooked dealing and favoritism. Sixteen Praetorian and four city cohorts were being enrolled, each with a thousand men. In that levy Valens dared more than Caecina, claiming to have rescued Caecina himself from danger. It was true that his arrival had revived the cause, and his winning battle had turned around the bad talk about his slow march. The whole army of Lower Germany also followed Valens, and that is believed to be where Caecina's loyalty first began to waver.
2:94 Much as Vitellius indulged his generals, he allowed the soldiers more freedom still. Each man chose his own service. However unfit, he was put in the soldiers of the capital if he preferred. Soldiers of good character were allowed to stay with their legions or in the cavalry if they liked, and many did, worn out by sickness or put off by the unhealthy climate. But the best of the legions and cavalry was drained into the capital's force, and the old glory of the Praetorian camp was ruined by twenty thousand men taken from the whole army, mixed together, not selected. While Vitellius was addressing the troops, they called for the execution of Asiaticus, Flavus and Rufinus, leaders of the Gauls, because they had fought for Vindex. Vitellius didn't stop such cries. Apart from the cowardice built into his soul, he knew a donative was coming and he had no money, so he gave the soldiers everything else. The freedmen of earlier emperors were ordered to contribute like a tax, in proportion to the number of their slaves. Vitellius himself, with no thought except to throw money away, was building a stable for his charioteers, filling the circus with shows of gladiators and wild beasts, and wasting his funds as if he had a great surplus.
2:95 Caecina and Valens even celebrated Vitellius's birthday by putting on gladiator shows in every district of the city, on a vast scale never seen before that day. This delighted the worst sort of people and disgusted the decent ones, as did his building altars on the Campus Martius and holding funeral rites for Nero. Animals were sacrificed and burned in the state's name, and the Augustales lit the fire. That is a priesthood which Tiberius Caesar dedicated to the Julian family, as Romulus did one for King Tatius. Not yet four months after the victory, Vitellius's freedman Asiaticus was rivaling the Polycleti, the Patrobii and all the old hated names. Nobody in that court competed in honesty or hard work. There was one road to power: to feed Vitellius's insatiable appetites with lavish dinners, wild spending and gluttony. The emperor himself, thinking it enough to enjoy the moment and giving no thought to the future, is believed to have squandered nine hundred million sesterces in a very few months. Rome, great and wretched, which had put up with an Otho and a Vitellius in one year, lived through a changing and shameful fate among the Vinii, the Fabii, the Iceli and the Asiatici, until Mucianus and Marcellus came, and different men, but not different morals.
## First news of revolt
2:96 The first revolt Vitellius heard of was the 3rd legion's, in letters sent by Aponius Saturninus before he too joined Vespasian's side. But Aponius, flustered by the sudden event, hadn't written all of it, and flattering friends softened its meaning. They said it was a mutiny of one legion only, and the loyalty of the other armies was certain. Vitellius spoke to the soldiers in the same way. He lashed out at the Praetorians who had recently been discharged, saying that false rumors were being spread by them, and he insisted there was no danger of civil war. He kept Vespasian's name quiet, and soldiers were sent wandering around the city to hold down the people's talk. That more than anything fed the rumors.
2:97 Still, Vitellius called up auxiliaries from Germany, Britain and Spain, slowly and trying to hide his need. The legates and the provinces were just as slow. Hordeonius Flaccus, already suspicious of the Batavians, was anxious about a war of his own, and Vettius Bolanus had in Britain a province that was never quite quiet. Both were wavering. Nor was there any hurry from Spain, which had no governor of consular rank just then. The commanders of its three legions, equal in rank, who would have competed in obedience while Vitellius was doing well, all held back from his failing fortune alike. In Africa, the legion and the cohorts raised by Clodius Macer, which Galba had soon afterward disbanded, went back into service by Vitellius's order, while the rest of the young men gave their names eagerly. For Vitellius had held that province as proconsul honestly and to people's liking, while Vespasian's term had been notorious and hated. So the allies guessed how each would rule, but the result was the opposite.
2:98 At first Valerius Festus, the legate, loyally backed the zeal of the provincials. Then he began to waver, supporting Vitellius openly in letters and edicts and Vespasian in secret messages, ready to defend one or the other, depending on which grew strong. Some soldiers and centurions who came through Raetia and Gaul were caught with letters and edicts from Vespasian, sent to Vitellius, and put to death. More slipped through, hidden by the loyalty of friends or their own cleverness. So Vitellius's preparations were known, while most of Vespasian's plans stayed unknown. First it was Vitellius's laziness, and then the Pannonian Alps, taken over by garrisons, that held back the messengers. The sea too, with the Etesian winds, was favorable to those sailing east but against anyone coming back.
## Caecina and Bassus turn
2:99 At last Vitellius, frightened by the enemy's invasion and by threatening news from every side, ordered Caecina and Valens to take the field. Caecina was sent ahead. Valens, only just getting up from a serious illness, was held back by weakness. The German army looked very different as it marched out of the city. There was no strength in their bodies and no fire in their spirits. The column was slow and thin, weapons loose in hand, horses listless. The soldiers couldn't stand the sun, the dust and the weather. The less able they were to bear hard work, the readier they were for mutiny. On top of that came Caecina's old vanity and his new laziness. Too indulged by fortune, he had gone soft in luxury. Perhaps he was already planning to betray Vitellius, and weakening the army's spirit was part of that. Many believed that his mind had been shaken by the advice of Flavius Sabinus, with Rubrius Gallus carrying the messages, saying that the terms for changing sides would be honored by Vespasian. At the same time he was reminded of his hatred and envy of Fabius Valens, so that, as the weaker of the two in Vitellius's favor, he would win favor and strength with the new emperor.
2:100 Caecina left Vitellius's embrace with great honor and sent part of the cavalry ahead to take Cremona. Next came the detachments of the 1st, 4th, 15th and 16th legions, then the 5th and 22nd. At the tail of the column came the 21st Rapax and the 1st Italian legion, with detachments of three British legions and picked auxiliaries. After Caecina left, Fabius Valens wrote to the army he had led himself, telling it to wait for him on the road. That was what he and Caecina had agreed. Caecina, who was there in person and therefore stronger, pretended the plan had changed, so that the whole mass could meet the growing war. So the legions were ordered to hurry to Cremona, and part of them to head for Hostilia. Caecina himself turned aside to Ravenna, on the pretext of addressing the fleet. Soon he looked for the privacy of Patavium to arrange his treason. For Lucilius Bassus, whom Vitellius had put in charge of the fleets at Ravenna and Misenum after commanding a cavalry wing, was avenging an unjust anger, because he hadn't been made Praetorian prefect at once, with a shameful betrayal. It can't be known whether he drew Caecina in or whether, as happens among bad men, who are alike, the same wickedness drove both.
2:101 The writers of the time, who set down the records of this war while the Flavian house held power, gave as the reasons concern for peace and love of the state, twisted into flattery. To me, apart from their natural fickleness and a loyalty cheapened after the betrayal of Galba, these men seem to have ruined Vitellius out of rivalry and envy, so that others wouldn't get ahead of them in his favor. Caecina, once he caught up with the legions, tried by every sort of trick to undermine the stubborn loyalty of the centurions and soldiers to Vitellius. Bassus had less trouble with the same attempt, since the fleet, remembering how recently it had served for Otho, was easy to turn.
