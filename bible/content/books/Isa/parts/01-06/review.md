# Hostile review: Isaiah 1–6 (part 01-06) and the book's front pages (book.json)

Reviewer's witnesses: the Hebrew through `node tools/literal.js Isa 1–6` and `texts/orig/Isa.json`; the Qumran copies through `tools/dss.py Isa 1–6` (1QIsa-a, 4Q55, 4Q56, 4Q59, 4Q60, 4Q63, 4Q69, Mur 3); the Greek through `tools/lxx.py Isa 1–6` (Swete); Rashi and the Targum on Sefaria; Justin's Dialogue on New Advent; other checks by web search, as noted. `VERBOSE=1 node tools/build.js --check Isa/01-06` reports 0 errors and 0 warnings.

## Verdict

This is a strong part. The translation is built from the Hebrew and does not drift: the familiar passages (1:18; 2:2–4; 5:1–7; 6) avoid NIV and ESV wording except where the Hebrew forces it. The Plain keeps the parallels between refrains (2:9 = 5:15; 2:10, 19, 21; 2:11 and 2:17 with their verbs swapped as in the Hebrew; 5:25 = 9:12 and 10:4). The notes are dense, accurate and fair, and most of the facts I checked hold up: the haftarot, Shabbat Chazon, Josephus *Antiquities* 11.5–6, Yevamot 49b, Justin's *Dialogue* 120, Pseudo-Matthew 14, Mishnah Shabbat 6:4, Bava Batra 75a, Kiddushin 40a, the 25 uses of "the Holy One of Israel" (12 + 13) and the 21 uses of *bara'*. The weak spots are on the front page and in the Qumran evidence for 6:9–10. book.json says Isaiah is "the longest of the prophets", which is false. It mixes two chronologies of the kings. It misreports Duhm, and it states the debated audiences of 40–66 as fact. The one-Isaiah view contradicts the book's own account of the scroll's break after 33:24. The 6:10 variant cites the Great Isaiah Scroll for a reading it does not have. The Targum's and Rashi's reading of 6:1 (Uzziah "struck" with skin disease) is missing, and the claim the writer made from memory about Rashi comes from his comment on 1:1, not on 6:1. Nothing is critical. Ten findings are major and twenty-three are minor.

## Findings

### Major

**1. book.json `lead[0]`: "Isaiah is the longest of the prophets" is false.** Isaiah has the most chapters (66), but Jeremiah is longer in both verses and words, and Ezekiel is longer in words. From `texts/orig`: Isaiah has 1,292 verses and 16,931 Hebrew words; Jeremiah has 1,364 verses and 21,835 words; Ezekiel has 1,273 verses and 18,730 words. *Fix:* "Isaiah has more chapters than any other prophet, and it is the prophet the New Testament quotes most."

**2. variants.json 6:10 ("Make this people's heart fat,"), with 6:9: the Great Isaiah Scroll is cited for a reading it does not have, and its real differences are missing.** The entry lists 1QIsa-a under the Masoretic reading הַשְׁמֵן, and its `why` says "All the Hebrew witnesses have the commands of 6:9–10." dss.py shows that 1QIsa-a reads «השמ» (no final nun) at 6:10 and «ועל» for MT ואל, twice, at 6:9: `שמעו שמוע «ועל» תבינו ראו «ראו» «ועל» תדעו`. With ʿayin, *'al* can be read "upon / because" rather than "do not". Brownlee proposed reading the scroll as a reworking of the commission (roughly "keep on hearing, because you will understand ... make this people's heart appalled"). Evans gives a chapter to the Qumran form ("Isaiah 6.9-10 in Qumran", *To See and Not Perceive*, which the entry already cites). Whether the change is deliberate or only spelling is debated, but a careful reader should know about it. *Fix:* remove 1QIsa-a from the MT witness list at 6:10. Add a variant at 6:9 (and 6:10) that gives the scroll's ʿl and hšm, its possible sense, and the view that it is only a spelling or sound change. Cite Evans and Brownlee, and check the exact proposal in Evans before writing it.

**3. Chronology of the kings is inconsistent and miscited (notes 1:1, 6:1; book.json `facts` Date; chapters 6 `views.H` and settings).** Note 1:1 gives Uzziah "about 783–742", Ahaz "about 735–715" and Hezekiah "about 715–687" (Albright/Bright-type dates), and cites `hayes-hooker-chronology`. Hayes and Hooker's chronology is very different: it ends Hezekiah's reign in 699 and starts Manasseh in 698. The book.json Date fact ("put to death under Manasseh, after 698 BC") silently takes the Hayes–Hooker date. The 1:1 note has Uzziah reigning to 742, while 6:1 and the settings date his death "about 740". *Fix:* choose one chronology for the book (for example Thiele/Cogan–Tadmor: Uzziah died about 740/739, Ahaz 735/732–716/715, Hezekiah 716/715–687/686, Manasseh's sole reign from 687/686). State once that other systems differ by up to 20 years, and give Hayes–Hooker by name only as an alternative. Make 1:1, 6:1, the Date fact and the "Isaiah and his times" section agree. Change "after 698 BC" to "after about 687 BC (697 if he ruled with his father first)".

**4. book.json `authorship[1]` and `audience.views[1]`: Duhm is misreported.** The text says Duhm made Second Isaiah "a prophet in Babylon in the 540s BC" and Third Isaiah "prophets in Judah after the return of 538". Duhm placed the author of 40–55 in Phoenicia or Lebanon, not Babylon. For example, a 1917 JBL article notes "Duhm has held that Deutero-Isaiah lived in Phoenicia". Duhm's "Tritojesaja" was a single author in Jerusalem shortly before Nehemiah (mid-400s), not prophets just after 538. He also set the servant songs apart as another author's work. The Babylonian setting for 40–55 and the plural, early-Persian Third Isaiah are later consensus positions. *Fix:* "Duhm (1892) divided them into Second Isaiah (40–55), a prophet of the 540s (Duhm himself placed him in Phoenicia; most later scholars place him among the exiles in Babylon), and Third Isaiah (56–66), whom Duhm dated to the generation before Nehemiah; most scholars now think of several prophets in Judah after 538."

**5. book.json `audience.to` and `facts` "Setting": the debated audiences of 40–55 and 56–66 are stated as fact.** The brief says: "do not state either as settled". `audience.to` says 40–55 "speak to Jews who ... live as exiles in Babylon" and 56–66 speak "to a community back in Judah, rebuilding Jerusalem". The Setting fact says "chapters 56–66 speak to a community in Judah rebuilding its city". Traditional readers (Young, Motyer, Oswalt) do not take 56–66 as speech to a post-538 community. Several read 56–59 and 65–66 against Isaiah's or Manasseh's own day, and Oswalt reads 56–66 as addressed to a future community. *Fix:* reword as "Chapters 40–55 address the exiles in Babylon, whether as a prophecy written ahead of time or as preaching among them (see the views), and 56–66 address a community in Judah after the return, or, on the traditional view, Isaiah's own people looking ahead to it."

**6. book.json `authorship[0]` (one Isaiah): the view contradicts the book's own scroll section, and is not in its strongest form.** It says "no ancient witness, including the Great Isaiah Scroll, shows any break in the book". The section "The Great Isaiah Scroll and the other Qumran copies" correctly says the scroll leaves three blank lines after 33:24 and was made in two halves, with a change of scribe at chapter 34 (confirmed: the 2021 handwriting study and Fidanzio's work on the two-part manufacture, reported in the Times of Israel). The argument that unity scholars actually make is narrower and stronger: there is no break at 39/40, since 40:1 begins on the last line of the column that ends chapter 39. Also: "Defenders ... today, such as E. J. Young, O. T. Allis" names men who died in 1968 and 1973. And the view names no Jewish defender of single authorship, although the brief describes it as held by "many conservative Jewish and Christian scholars". *Fix:* restate the scroll point as "no break between chapters 39 and 40". Say "Defenders include E. J. Young and O. T. Allis, and more recently Motyer and Oswalt". Name a Jewish defender, for example Rachel Margalioth, *The Indivisible Isaiah* (1964), and check the citation.

**7. Note 6:1 and chapters 6 `views.J` / `deep`: the Targum's and Rashi's reading of "the year King Uzziah died" is missing, and the Rashi claim made from memory comes from another comment.** The Targum of 6:1 reads בְּשַׁתָּא דְאִתְנַגַע בָּהּ מַלְכָּא עֻזִיָה, "in the year that King Uzziah was struck". *Itnega'* uses the root of *nega'*, the word for a skin-disease affliction. Rashi on 6:1 says בִּשְׁנַת מוֹת: כְּשֶׁנִּצְטָרַע, "when he was struck with skin disease", citing Tanchuma Tzav 13 (on that reading, the Talmud says a man with skin disease counts as dead). This is the major Jewish interpretation of the verse, and it ties directly to the writer's own point about 2 Chronicles 26. On the writer's flagged claim: Rashi does say that chapter 6 was Isaiah's first prophecy and that the book is not in order, but he says it in his comment on **1:1**, not 6:1, and his comment on 6:1 says nothing of the kind. The underlying tradition is older (Mekhilta, Shirata). *Fix:* add the "struck with skin disease" reading to note 6:1 (term: "skin disease", never "leprosy"), with the Targum (Chilton) and Rashi on 6:1. Where the site says "Rashi takes chapter 6 as Isaiah's first prophecy", cite Rashi on Isaiah 1:1.

**8. variants.json / notes: the Plain adds "one of them" at 5:26, and the singular/plural problem has no note.** The Hebrew is וְשָׁרַק לוֹ, "and he will whistle for **it**". The Plain "he will whistle for one of them" decides an interpretive question in the text. The verse moves from "nations" (*goyim*) to a singular "it", and many scholars read *goy mimmerchaq*, "a nation far away" (compare Jeremiah 5:15), by re-dividing the letters (*lgwym mrḥwq* → *lgwy mmrḥq*). The Greek has plurals throughout (συριεῖ αὐτοὺς ... ἔρχονται). *Fix:* render "he will whistle for it" and add a `deb` note on the emendation and the Greek. Or keep "faraway nations" and note that the Hebrew turns singular, so that the singular is Assyria.

**9. book.json `facts` "Place in the Bible": the Christian order given is only the Protestant one.** "In Christian Bibles it is the first of the Major Prophets, after the Song of Songs" is true of Protestant Bibles only. In Catholic Bibles Isaiah follows Wisdom and Sirach. In the Septuagint order used by the Orthodox, the Twelve come before Isaiah. *Fix:* "In Protestant Bibles it follows the Song of Songs; in Catholic Bibles it follows Sirach; in Orthodox Bibles, which keep the Septuagint order, it comes after the Twelve."

**10. chapters.json ch. 6 settings 6:1–8: `to` does not name the first hearers.** The card says "His hearers and readers, as the ground of his authority". The brief and STYLE ask for exact first hearers, and the text gives a lead. 6:1–8:18 is a first-person memoir, and 8:16 has Isaiah seal his teaching "among my disciples". *Fix:* `to`: "Isaiah's disciples and the people of Jerusalem who heard him in the 730s, as the account behind his memoir (6:1–8:18; 8:16)". Add `views`: written for the disciples when the message was refused, or part of his public preaching.

### Minor

**11. variants.json 2:9: 4QIsa-b is miscited.** The scroll reads «ולא» (*velo'*), not MT ואל (*ve'al*): `וישח אדם וישפל איש «ולא» [תשא] להם`. That agrees with the Greek καὶ οὐ μὴ ἀνήσω αὐτούς ("I will not forgive them"). 4QIsa-a preserves only וישח and להם. *Fix:* say so in `b`, which strengthens the entry's point about the Greek.

**12. variants.json 2:3: 4QIsa-b (4Q56) is listed as a witness for "to the mountain of the LORD", but its text of the verse begins at ונלכה.** 4QIsa-e has the phrase only in brackets, so it is restored (it preserves אל ... ואל בית). 4QIsa-f (4Q60) does preserve אל הר יהוה. *Fix:* cite 4QIsa-f, and 4QIsa-e as "the spacing implies it"; drop 4QIsa-b.

**13. variants.json 1:31: 4QIsa-f is said to agree with the MT, but its החסן is restored.** It preserves ופעלו, "his work". *Fix:* "4QIsa-f preserves 'his work', agreeing with the Masoretic Text."

**14. Missing small variant at 1:24.** 1QIsa-a has מצריו ... מהאיבו, "his foes ... his enemies", for MT "my foes ... my enemies". The dss.py header warns that -o "his" for -i "my" can be a real difference. A one-line mention in the note on 1:24 is enough.

**15. Missing variant at 6:8.** The Greek has τίς πορεύσεται πρὸς τὸν λαὸν τοῦτον, "who will go **to this people**", with no "for us". This matters for the "for us" note, which says many church fathers heard the Trinity. The Greek fathers read a text without the plural. *Fix:* add a sentence to the note on 6:8, or a short variant.

**16. Note 6:9: Romans 11:8 is misdescribed.** The note says Paul "blends it with Deuteronomy 29:4 (Romans 11:8)". Romans 11:8 combines Deuteronomy 29:4 with **Isaiah 29:10** ("a spirit of deep sleep"), not 6:9–10. *Fix:* "Romans 11:8 joins Deuteronomy 29:4 to Isaiah 29:10, in words close to 6:9–10".

**17. Note 1:16: Justin, *First Apology* 44 is not about baptism.** Chapter 61 quotes 1:16–20 for baptism. Chapter 44 quotes the passage on free will. *Fix:* "(First Apology 61; he also quotes it on free will in 44)".

**18. Note 4:1: "Jewish printings ... join 4:1 to chapter 3" is inaccurate.** Hebrew printed Bibles number it 4:1 like Christian ones. What joins 4:1 to chapter 3 is the Masoretic paragraphing: there is no break after 3:26, and there is a *setumah* after 4:1. *Fix:* "The Masoretic paragraph marks join 4:1 to 3:16–26, as do most commentators."

**19. Chapters 2 settings 2:2–4, view "An older Temple song ... (Roberts)": the attribution is doubtful.** As far as I can tell, Roberts ("Isaiah 2 and the Prophet's Message to the North", *JQR* 75, 1985; *First Isaiah*, 2015) argues that the oracle is Isaiah's own early preaching. The "older liturgy" view is usually credited to others (for example von Rad's "The City on the Hill"). *Fix:* check Roberts, then move him to the first view or name the right scholar.

**20. book.json `lenses` X: "Early writers called Isaiah 'the fifth gospel'", and "more than any other prophet except the Psalms".** Early writers called Isaiah an *evangelist* (Jerome's preface; Augustine, *City of God* 18.29). "The fifth gospel" is a later label, the title of Sawyer's study. The Psalms are not a prophet. *Fix:* "Jerome said Isaiah should be called an evangelist rather than a prophet; later Christians called the book 'the fifth gospel'", and "which quotes Isaiah more than any other book except the Psalms".

**21. book.json `outline` 56:9–57:21: "the high and lofty One" is not the site's wording.** The lead decision fixes *ram venissa'* as "high and lifted up", and part 56-66 has "the One who is high and lifted up" at 57:15. "High and lofty One" is the KJV's wording. *Fix:* "the One who is high and lifted up dwells with the lowly".

**22. book.json `spec`: the "seventh woe" idea is already published.** The theory is labeled correctly, but "counting 6:5 as the deliberate seventh is my suggestion" is not new. An editorial in *Toward the Mark* 13.5 (1984, edited by Harry Foster) calls 6:5 "the seventh and final use" of woe after chapter 5, and the idea is common in sermons. *Fix:* say that it overlaps with that devotional tradition, and keep the honest point that *hoy* and *'oy* are different words.

**23. book.json `facts` Date says Ben Sira wrote "about 200 BC", and `authorship[0]` says "about 180 BC".** *Fix:* use "about 180 BC" in both.

**24. Terms: hoy at 1:24 is "Ha!", which the prophets' term list does not allow** (it allows "Woe to", "Alas", or "Hey!"/"Come!"). The note explains the choice, so either add "Ha! (a threat, 1:24)" to `content/proposals/prophets/terms.json` or use one of the listed forms.

**25. Terms: ne'um at 3:15 is "declares the Lord GOD of Armies".** The term rule says "the Lord GOD of Armies declares", at the end of a clause and set off by commas. *Fix:* "... and grind the faces of the poor?" / the Lord GOD of Armies declares.

**26. Terms: *gerim* at 5:17 is "foreigners passing through".** The site term for *ger* is "foreigner who lives among you" (a resident foreigner). "Passing through" says the opposite. *Fix:* "foreigners" (or "foreigners living there"), with the variant unchanged.

**27. Plain 6:13: "If a tenth part is still left in it" adds a condition.** The Hebrew is a statement, וְעוֹד בָּהּ עֲשִׂרִיָּה, "and still a tenth is in it". *Fix:* "Even if a tenth is left in it" is defensible; "And though a tenth is still left in it" or "A tenth will still be left in it" is closer.

**28. Plain 5:7: "listen, an outcry!"** The Hebrew repeats וְהִנֵּה, "look", both times. Changing the second to "listen" adds an interpretive touch (an outcry is heard), and the note's wordplay analysis depends on the two lines being parallel. Optional fix: "look, an outcry!"

**29. chapters.json ch. 4 `views.C`: "a shorter one in 4:5" (Greek) is doubtful.** Swete's Greek is not clearly shorter. It differs: καὶ ἥξει, "and he will come", for "the LORD will create" (reading *uva'* for *uvara'*), and "all its surroundings" for "her assemblies". *Fix:* "a different text in 4:5 ('he will come' for 'he will create')". The *bara'* note on 4:5 should mention this, since its point rests on the verb.

**30. Settings cover 4:1 twice** (3:16–4:1 in ch. 3 and 4:1 in ch. 4), so readers of 4:1 see two cards. *Fix:* drop the 4:1 card or narrow the ch. 3 card to 3:16–26.

**31. Traditions card 6:10: the Catholic last sentence is unsourced, and the Lutheran view is missing.** The Catechism does not comment on 6:10. *Fix:* source the "God permits / withholds grace" reading to Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I–II q. 79 a. 3. Consider a Lutheran view (Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration XI: God's foreknowledge is not the cause of hardening), since Lutherans differ from both Reformed and Methodist teaching here.

**32. Traditions card 2:4.** The Catholic view can cite CCC 2317, which quotes Isaiah 2:4 itself. The Orthodox answer ("never developed a formal just war teaching") should mention the Russian Orthodox *Bases of the Social Concept* (2000), section VIII, which sets out when a defensive war is allowed, so the answer is not one-sided.

**33. Small overstatements in notes.** Note 3:18: "the longest list of women's finery in ancient literature" cannot be checked; say "one of the longest". Note 1:25: changing *kabbor* to *bakkur* is swapping two letters, not changing "one letter". Note 6:6: mention that the Greek has ἄνθραξ, "a coal", beside the Plain's "hot stone". book.json `lead[1]`: "From then on his name for God is 'the Holy One of Israel'" sits oddly with 1:4 and 5:19, 24; say "His name for God throughout the book". "Seraphs" in `lead[1]` against "seraphim" everywhere else.

## Checks that passed (for the writer's information)

- **NIV/ESV drift.** 1:18 avoids "reason together" and the familiar "though ... as white as snow" pattern. 2:2–4 ("set firm at the head of the mountains", "pound ... pruning knives", "never again learn how to make war") is independent. 5:1–7 shares only what the Hebrew forces (the order of *ashirah na lididi shirat dodi*; "bloodshed", "an outcry"). In chapter 6, "high and lifted up" is the lead decision, and "Here I am! Send me" (*hinneni shelacheni*), "Go, and say to this people" and "and turn back, and be healed" are forced by the Hebrew. 6:3 ("Everything that fills the earth is his glory") is more literal than either version. 4:4 "a spirit of judgment and a spirit of burning" is forced. Clean.
- **Facts verified.** The seven haftarot of consolation and their ranges, Shabbat Chazon (1:1–27) and Yom Kippur (57:14–58:14) are all correct. Josephus *Ant.* 11.5–6 (Niese sections; Whiston 11.1.2) is correct. Yevamot 49b (the cedar and the saw) is correct. Justin *Dial.* 120 does say the passages about Isaiah's death were cut out. The *Martyrdom and Ascension* (Knibb; Ge'ez), the *Lives of the Prophets* (Siloam) and Hebrews 11:37 are correctly described. The 1QIsa-a facts (17 sheets, 54 columns, about 7.3 m; three blank lines after 33:24; second half from 34) are supported. The Targum at 6:10 has וְיִשְׁתְּבֵק לְהוֹן, "and it be forgiven them", which agrees with Mark 4:12, as the writer says. The Holy One of Israel occurs 25 times (12 in 1–39, 13 in 40–66), and *bara'* 21 times; both correct. 1QIsa-a's double "holy" (6:3), its single "six wings" (6:2), the missing 2:9b–10, the skip between the two "by day" (4:5–6), *boshet* (3:24), "and Judah" (4:2), *sa'ar* (4:4), the 1:15 plus, *mšlkt* and *bmh* (6:13) and *kis'o* (6:1) are all confirmed in dss.py. The Greek readings cited at 1:13, 1:27, 1:31, 2:9–10, 2:22, 3:10, 3:12, 3:13, 3:24, 4:2, 5:2, 5:9, 5:17, 6:1, 6:5 and 6:13 are all confirmed in Swete.
- **Outline.** All 66 chapters are covered, the ranges are in order and the descriptions match the text, apart from finding 21.
- **Traditions cards.** Both cards (2:4 nonviolence; 6:10 hardening) show real conflict. The quoted sources are accurate: CCC 2307–2309, 1037 and 600; Basil, *Letter* 188 canon 13; Augsburg Confession 16; Westminster Confession 23.2; Schleitheim article 6; the 1995 Mennonite Confession article 22; the Quaker declaration of 1660; Dort I.6 and I.15; John of Damascus, *Exact Exposition* 2.29; Wesley, *Free Grace*.
