# Hostile review: 3 Maccabees 4-7 (part 04-07)

Reviewer: separate hostile-reviewer agent, 2026-10-31. Checked: plain.txt (136 verses) against `texts/orig/3Ma.json` (Swete, Codex Alexandrinus) and Swete's footnotes in `texts/src/3Ma.app.json`; Brenton in `texts/en/3Ma.json`; notes.json (80), variants.json (16), glosses.json (49), chapters.json (4), topics.json (12 contributions), sources.json; part 01-03 and book-level sources; the site's Plain for every 66-book verse quoted. `cd bible && VERBOSE=1 node tools/build.js --check 3Ma/04-07` gives 0 errors, 0 warnings.

## Verdict

The Plain is mostly faithful, readable and well sourced, and the part is honest about the Venetus and Alexandrinus differences where it flags them. I found no invented source, reading or fragment, and no sign of copying from a copyrighted translation (a few phrases match Brenton, which is public domain). The five "Swete's text" entries (4:3, 4:16, 6:4, 6:35, 7:16) and the 6:6 dew entry match Swete's apparatus. The weak points are these. (1) The three departures from Swete at 7:20-22 are not equally justified, and 7:21 states a comparative ("more than before") that the Venetus reading as Swete reports it does not contain. (2) Several Plain readings rest on Swete's conjectures or emendations (5:43, 5:32) and carry no variant entry. (3) The dating views, and several scholarly claims, rest on a Wikipedia article that does not say all that is attributed to it. (4) The treatment of 7:10-15 is candid about the killing but states "the New Testament rules it out" as the churches' settled view. It points readers to a 1 Kings 18 card that has no Orthodox answer, and the Orthodox, Jewish and Western "lens" sentences are unsourced. (5) The 6:6 canon note and lens misdescribe the dew as an addition to "the Bible", which is wrong for Catholic and Orthodox Bibles. Nothing here is critical. Seven major and sixteen minor findings follow.

Verified in this session and found correct: Aristeas 13 and 36 (Charles, ccel.org); Josephus, Against Apion 2.53-55 and the "celebrate this day" sentence (Whiston, lexundria.com); Strabo 17.1.16 on Schedia (scriptorium.ro; "four schoeni", boat station, toll, bridge of boats); Polybius' 73 and 102 elephants (secondary summary only; Polybius' Greek not opened); Epip 1 = 25 June Julian (Wikipedia, "Epip"); Leviticus 26:44 in Greek (Blue Letter Bible snippet); the forty-day count (Pachon 25-30 = 6, Payni 30, Epiphi 1-4 = 4); "Hades" occurring four times (4:8, 5:42, 5:51, 6:31); ἐνέργεια at 4:21, 5:12 and 5:28; every 66-book, Apocrypha and Esther-variant quotation I traced matches the site's current Plain (Esther 4:3, 8:17, 9:15; Luke 2:2, 22:25; Acts 13:1; 1 Peter 1:17; Revelation 2:10; and so on). Exceptions are in findings 12 and 13.

## Findings

### Major

1. **7:20-21: "more authority than before" is not in the Greek of either codex.** Location: plain.txt 7:21; variants.json 7:20 and 7:21; chapters.json ch. 7 summary ("more standing than before"). Swete's note reports Venetus at 7:20 as `εις την] + ιδιαν· και πλειστην`. So V has "his own. And very great (πλείστην) ... before (ἔμπροσθεν), among the enemies, having authority". "Πλείστην" is a superlative with no comparative "than". The variant entry then invents glosses: `"en": "and very great, beyond before"` and the "why" says V supplies "'more' than before". "More ... than before" is Brenton's smoothing ("more weight than before"), which the Plain adopts. A's text is also defective, but the Plain should render only what V as reported has. Fix: "and very great authority in the sight of their enemies" (or similar), correct the `en` and `plain` of the variant, and change the chapter summary. Say openly that V's reading is known only from Swete's brief note, and that the sentence is not fully recoverable.

2. **7:22: departure from Swete (φόρου to φοβου) is a choice, not a repair, and is argued weakly.** Location: plain.txt 7:22; variants.json 7:22. At 7:20-21 A is broken, so following V is defensible. At 7:22 A is intelligible: `μετὰ φόρου μεγίστου`, "with very great payment". Φόρος can mean tribute or payment, and a return of property "with a very great payment" (restitution with a penalty) makes sense next to `ἐξ ἀπογραφῆς`. The entry's reason ("tribute is hard to fit, since the goods are only being returned") assumes the answer. Following V and Brenton ("terror") may be right, but it is a conjectural preference, and the Plain gives no sign of it. Fix: either follow Swete and show V as the alternative, or keep V but say in the entry that this is a choice between two defensible readings, give the Esther 8:17 parallel as an argument and not as proof, and mark the Plain's wording. Also add a missing variant: V has μεγίστου for A's μεγάλου at `τοῦ μεγάλου θεοῦ` (apparatus, 7:22). It matters because "Most Great God" is a fixed term of the book.

3. **Plain follows Swete's conjectures at 5:43 and 5:32 with no variant entry.**
   - 5:43: Swete prints `πρή[σα]ν[τα]` in brackets. His note reads `πρησαντα] πρην AV`: both codices have `πρην` ("before"), so "burn with fire their temple" rests entirely on Swete's reconstruction. The Plain presents it as plain Greek. Add an entry.
   - 5:32: Swete prints `συστροφίας` (a gathering, a conspiracy); the Plain says "who were raised with me", i.e. an unflagged emendation to συντροφίας. The note (5:32) discusses it, but the Plain departs from the printed text without a variants.json entry. STYLE requires one wherever the Plain departs from the base text. Add an entry and give the printed word.
   - Smaller gaps in the same class (add entries or fold into one): 6:15, V omits `ἔθνεσιν` (so V reads "to all"), while the Plain, the prayer and the Lev. 26:44 note all depend on "the Gentiles"; 7:16, V `σωτηρίας ἀπόλαυσιν` ("enjoyment of salvation") for Swete's `σωτηρίαν ἀπόλυσιν`; 5:49, A corrupt (`ελκονται αλλ`).

4. **Scholarly claims rest on one Wikipedia article that does not say all of them.** I read the article (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3_Maccabees) this session. It says "the widest plausible range ... between 100 BC and 70 AD". It names Bickerman, Anderson and Johnson (late Ptolemaic); Hadas, Tcherikover and Orian (about 25-15 BC); Ewald, Willrich and Collins (about AD 40). It says laographia was "a rare word before the Romans came", that the Romans took a census in 24 BC, and that Ptolemy "does not claim he is a divinity". It does not do the following:
   - say "most scholars" (chapters.json `settings.readers`, four times). The article says "scholars generally advocate for one of three periods". The "100 BC-AD 70" range is the article's "widest plausible range", not a majority view;
   - credit Anderson with the argument that a Ptolemaic registration could threaten native-Egyptian status, or credit Hadas and Tcherikover with "laographia is rare before Roman rule" (the article states the word's rarity without those names);
   - mention Greek Esther's Additions B and E at all (notes `7:1`, `6:24`, `7:15` cite `wikipedia-3macc` for "which book borrowed from which is debated"; the article's body only cites Esther 9);
   - say the angels are "only God's hands" (note `6:18` "Wikipedia's summary of scholarship mentions this reading"; the article only says the angels were invisible to the Jews);
   - say "most historians think both writers drew on one tradition" (note `6:21`); it says only that Josephus's account is a possible historical core, uncertain.

   The book-level sources already hold better material (Tcherikover in Zion 10, Emmet 1913, Swete's Introduction, Catholic Encyclopedia). Fix: cite those for the dating and for each named scholar's argument, open Anderson (OTP) or Hadas for what they argue, and delete or soften what cannot be sourced (STYLE rule 2). Drop "most scholars", and remove the Wikipedia attributions that the article does not support (the Additions debate, the "God's hands" reading, "most historians"). The part's own list in sources.json names Orian, but the chapters do not.

5. **7:10-15 and the other "lens" statements: the killing is stated plainly, but the Christian verdict is stated as settled and the tradition sentences are unsourced.** Location: notes `7:12` (canon), chapters.json ch. 7 `views` O, J, W, `deep` "Against the Bible", the 7:10-11 `views`, topics `phinehas`; also the O and J lines of chs. 4-6. What is good: the text is not softened. The killing, the king's license "without any review", the priests' "Hallelujah" and the celebration are all stated, and a "Claude's reading" box is labeled. What is wrong:
   - "The New Testament rules out such killing" / "rules such killing out" (note, deep, topic) is a flat verdict. The pointed-to 1 Kings 18 card itself records that Catholic law (Lateran IV) and the Westminster Confession backed punishing heresy. Luke 9:54-55 and Romans 12:19 are about calling fire on a Samaritan village and about personal revenge; they do not decide a community's execution of apostates. Say "most Christian traditions today read ... as ruling this out" and name the dissenting history, or cite the card's answers by name.
   - The pointer "see the churches' answers on the card at 1 Kings 18" (ch. 7 W view and deep) is real (1Ki 17-22, ch. 18, topic `church-and-state`). But the card has Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, Anabaptist and Baptist answers and no Orthodox answer, in a book that is Orthodox Scripture, and its question is rulers punishing false religion, not a community killing its own renegades. Do not call it "the churches' answers". Add an Orthodox answer there (via the 1 Kings writer's lane) or add a short traditions card here.
   - The O view of ch. 7 ("read its stern ending in the light of the Gospel, which does not allow vengeance against those who fall away"), the O line of ch. 4 ("the Jews' passion in Egypt"), ch. 5 and ch. 6, and the J lines ("Jewish readers will hear Esther") state what Orthodox and Jewish readers "read" with no Orthodox or Jewish source. The Jewish tradition did not keep this book, so "Jewish reception" here is the modern scholars' observation. Source each (a catechism, a Fathers' use, the OSB, a Jewish scholar) or reword as "readers who know Esther will notice" and drop the church label.
   - The "renegades" are called "people who had eaten what the Law forbids" (note `7:10`; note `7:11`, "sums up apostasy as a matter of food"). 7:11 says `γαστρὸς ἕνεκεν`, "for the belly's sake", which is the writer's slur, and 2:30-33 (the Dionysus initiation for citizenship) is the stated cause. "Eating what the Law forbids" is an inference; label it so.
   - The Law cited is Deuteronomy 13:6-10 (the secret enticer) with the inquiry of 13:14 (which belongs to the idolatrous-city section). The nearest law for a Jew who himself serves other gods is Deuteronomy 17:2-7 (careful inquiry, two or three witnesses). Add it; it is the strongest form of the contrast with "no trial".

6. **6:6 dew: the canon note and the Western lens misdescribe the dew and overstate the dependence.** Location: notes `6:6`; variants.json 6:6; topics `shadrach-meshach-abednego`; chapters.json ch. 6 W view.
   - The ch. 6 W view says Catholic and Protestant readers test "its additions to the Bible (Jonah shown to his people, 6:8; the dew, 6:6)". The dew in the furnace is in Greek Daniel 3 (Dan 3:49-50 in the Catholic numbering), which the Catholic and Orthodox Bibles contain. For Catholics it is not an addition to their Bible. Fix: "an addition to the Hebrew and Aramaic text of Daniel" and say which churches read it.
   - The note says "The writer knew Daniel in Greek, with the Additions", and the variant says the prayer "follows" Greek Daniel 3:50. One shared image does not prove literary dependence; say "echoes" or "may rest on".
   - The note quotes "like a whistling wind of dew" with the pointer "see the variant at Daniel 3:23". The site's Daniel variant at 3:23 says "a moist wind" and gives no verse 3:50 and no "dew". The quoted phrase is therefore not the site's text, and the pointer does not support it. `texts/` holds no Greek Daniel or Greek Leviticus, so I could not check the Greek of 3:50 against an opened Daniel text in the project. The 3:49-50 verses are also not part of "the Prayer of Azariah" proper (Dan 3:24-45 Greek numbering). Say what text you opened, quote from it, and fix the label.

7. **7:12 `παραινέσας` is rendered "commended".** Location: plain.txt 7:12. The Greek is `ὁ δὲ τἀληθὲς αὐτοὺς λέγειν παραδεξάμενος καὶ παραινέσας`. `παραινέω` is "to urge, advise, exhort", not "to commend". The Plain's "accepted ... and commended them" matches Brenton's "and commended them" exactly (public domain, so the point is correctness, not copying). I could not open a lexicon this session (LSJ was not reachable), so this rests on standard usage and should be checked in LSJ or BDAG-style references. If the sense is "urged them", the verse is not softened by "commended" but changes who is doing what: the king encourages the killing. In the passage the brief requires to be unsoftened, the verb should be exact. Fix: "accepted that they were telling the truth, and encouraged them", after a lexicon check. Also check `βεβήλους χειρωσάμενοι` at 7:15 ("put the unholy to death"): `χειρόομαι` is "to subdue, overpower"; the killing is already stated by `ἀνεῖλον`, so "overpowered the profane" is the literal wording.

### Minor

8. **4:11 "voyage along the coast".** `παράπλου` is a sailing past or along; the notes (4:9, the ch. 4 summary) say the boats came down the Nile and a canal to Schedia, and Strabo's Schedia is on the canal, not on the coast. "Coast" imports a route the text does not name. Say "the voyage" or "the passage by the water".

9. **Schedia note (4:11): two small errors.** In Strabo 17.1.16 (opened) the canal to Schedia leaves the Canobic road "a little way past Eleusis, on the right", i.e. it leads to Schedia; the note says a canal "ran toward the city". "So the prisoners were landed short of the city and marched to the hippodrome" is the writer's inference; label it. "A schoenus ... a few miles" is vague; Strabo's schoenus varies. Say so or drop it.

10. **Note 5:28 (`5:35` "King of kings"): wrong verse for the Lamb.** "A title the Bible gives to ... the Lamb (Revelation 19:16)". Rev 19:16 names the rider on the white horse; the Lamb is Rev 17:14 (both in the site's Plain). Fix the reference.

11. **Note 6:2 (`6:2` views): "Eleazar's list comes in order of history".** Pharaoh, Sennacherib, the furnace, the lions, Jonah are not in historical order (Jonah's setting is before Sennacherib's). Say "in an order the writer chose". Note 6:4: the "chariots 'in great numbers' (Exodus 14:7)" quotation is 3 Maccabees' own Plain, not Exodus' wording, and "a boasting tongue" is not in Exodus 14; cite Exodus 15:9 or delete.

12. **Brenton dating (note `4:3`).** "Brenton's 1844 English": the text in `texts/en/3Ma.json` is the 1851 edition (and the shared source key `brenton-lxx` says 1844 for the first edition). Say which edition was read.

13. **Unsourced lexical and background claims (STYLE rule 2).** Note `4:1` (σκιρόω "used of a callus or of plaster setting", "very rare"); note `5:1`/`5:?` (Hermon "appears nowhere else"); note `5:32` (court "syntrophoi ... kept the title for life"); note 6:30 (Sōtēria feasts); chapter 7 deep and note `7:19` ("No calendar of Jewish festivals names it"); ch. 6 ("the only outside evidence that such a feast existed"). Each is an absolute or lexical claim with no opened source. Source each or soften ("I found no other"). Note `6:21`: Physcon is "a king of the later 100s BC"; his war with Onias belongs to the mid-100s BC; say 145-144 BC only if opened.

14. **Coptic month equivalents (note `6:38`, glosses 6:38).** Verified: Epip 1 = 25 June Julian (Wikipedia, "Epip"); my own count from the Coptic year gives Pachon 26 April-25 May, but the cited source covers Epip only, so the Pachon gloss is unsourced. The note's "fifth to seventh of Epiphi = 29 June-1 July" applies the Coptic (Alexandrian, fixed) calendar to a story set in 217 BC, when the Egyptian civil year had no leap day and drifted against the sun. The Julian fixing dates from the reform about 25 BC. The note says "a separate question", but then the equivalence has no use and misleads. Either delete it or say plainly that it applies only if the author's calendar was the reformed one (which would itself bear on the date).

15. **Gloss 5:14 "about 3:30 in the afternoon"** is false precision. Ancient hours were seasonal (the story is set at the end of June, long days), so the tenth hour is roughly mid to late afternoon. Say "mid-afternoon". **Gloss 7:22** "the official list of their seized goods" interprets `ἐξ ἀπογραφῆς` (earlier the register of people); say "the register" and explain in a note if kept.

16. **Variant entry slips.** 4:16: the A reading is glossed "speaking for things not fitting" in `en` and "against things not fitting" in `why`; Greek `εἰς` supports "against" or "to". 4:3: the A column's `plain` "were not" is not a reading. 7:16: Swete's note reads `αγιω σωτηρι] αγιων σοι A αιωνιω V`, so V replaces the whole phrase; the entry gives V "the eternal Savior of Israel", adding "Savior". 6:9 note: `μισύβρι` is V's spelling; Swete prints `μισούβρι`. 6:35: Swete reads `χορόν` through the corrector of V; fine.

17. **Esther echoes stated more strongly than shown.** Note `4:1`: "the same words open the Jews' mourning and the Jews' feast" compares the site's English Plain of the Hebrew; 3 Maccabees is Greek, and I did not open Greek Esther's wording, so "same words" and "the reader ... is meant to expect" are claims about intent that should be labeled as observation. Note `7:15`: "likely chosen to echo" Esther 9:15 is speculation (the 300 is a round number) and should be labeled. Note `6:36` / ch. 6: the shared "fourteenth" is offered as a close parallel, but Pachon-Epiphi and Adar are different calendars; say it is a coincidence of numbers unless a source argues otherwise. Topic `esther`: "3 Maccabees names God on every page" is not checkable; say "often".

18. **"Gentiles" versus "nations" (4:1 note).** The note's rule ("where a king or a prayer speaks of peoples in general, it says 'nations' (6:5, 26; 7:4)") is broken by the prayer's own `ἔθνη` at 6:13 and `πᾶσιν ἔθνεσιν` at 6:15, rendered "Gentiles", and part 01-03 uses "nations" for `ἔθνη` at 3:15, 3:19 and "Gentile" only at 1:11. The usage fits terms.json (`ἔθνη`: nations; Gentiles where non-Jews as people), but the note's stated rule is wrong. Restate the rule: "Gentiles" where the contrast is Jew/non-Jew in the plot, "nations" for the kings' wide speech.

19. **Source keys.** The same Wikipedia article now has three keys: `wikipedia-3macc` (04-07), `wikipedia-3-maccabees` (01-03), `wiki-3macc` (book). Reuse one (see STYLE: source keys are global). Also `wikipedia-synagogue` and `wikipedia-epip` are new keys; check that nobody has already added them in another lane.

20. **Settings gaps and repeats.** There is no setting for the king's orders to Hermon at 5:2 and 5:37-38, the feast order at 6:30, or the Jews' petition at 6:37 and 6:41; every letter and prayer has one, but these orders and the petition do not. The same long dating paragraph is copied into `readers` in three settings and the same three `views` blocks into three settings. State it once at the best place and point to it.

21. **Note 5:49 "Other women held newborn babies"** is fine; note `6:1` and `6:9` correct. Note `6:11`: the wordplay "mataiophrones ... mataiois" is real (`ματαιόφρονες`, `ματαίοις` in 6:11); but the Plain "bless their empty gods" supplies "gods" from context (Greek has `τοῖς ματαίοις`, "the empty things"); mention in the note.

22. **Note `4:6` (1 Corinthians 11:5) and Numbers 5:18.** Both quotations match the site's Plain, but 1 Corinthians 11:5 is about head covering in worship, a weak parallel for forced public humiliation; either drop it or say the parallel is loose.

23. **Drift check.** I could not open NRSV, RSV, NETS, the Lexham Septuagint or Anderson this session, so I compared only against Brenton (public domain). The Plain matches Brenton in sense at 7:12 ("commended"), 7:21 ("more ... than before") and 6:35 and differs in wording elsewhere. I found no phrasing that looks lifted from a modern translation, but cannot rule out a match with the copyrighted ones from the available material. The 7:12 and 7:21 renderings are the two to compare first when someone has those translations open.
