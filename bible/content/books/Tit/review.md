# Hostile review: Titus (`Tit`), one part, flat folder

Reviewer: hostile-reviewer agent. Files read: plain.txt, notes.json (78), variants.json (11), glosses.json (21), chapters.json (3), book.json, topics.json (36), sources.json; the Greek in `texts/orig/Tit.json` (SBLGNT and Byzantine, verse by verse); `content/proposals/nt/topics/` (`pastoral-letters`, `letter-to-titus`, `crete`); the Plain of 1 Timothy, 2 Timothy and the books quoted. `node tools/build.js --check Tit`: 0 errors, 0 warnings. The writer's `.scratch/nt/Tit/sources-checked.md` does not exist, so nothing could be compared against it.

## Verdict

This is a careful, well-organized book. The Plain follows the Greek closely and the key terms (sensible, godliness, Savior, appearing, "This saying is trustworthy") match 1 Timothy and 2 Timothy. Church quotations I opened this session were accurate: Chrysostom's Homilies (hom. 2, 3, 5), Calvin on 1:5, 1:7, 1:11, 1:12, 2:13 and 3:5, Lumen Gentium 20 and 28, the Baptist Faith and Message 2000 (VI, VII, XVIII), Eusebius HE 3.4.6, Callimachus (Mair), Philo *Embassy* 282, the Porter/Wallace bibliographic details. The Pastorals authorship question is handled fairly and points to `pastoral-letters`. The serious problems are these. (1) A claim in the NWT material contradicts itself and the sources: the 2:13 variant entry, the JW card and the note disagree about "Christ Jesus" or "Jesus Christ", and the 2013 NWT I opened reads "Jesus Christ". (2) The women's passage (2:4-5) has a two-voice card, and there is no card at all for the slavery passage (2:9-10) or the older women as teachers, although the brief planned them and the notes lean on them. (3) Several statements are asserted without a source and some contradict sources the writer lists: "oldest complete exposition" of Titus (Chrysostom), "Jerome is the first who certainly ascribes the work" to Epimenides (Clement is cited two sentences earlier), "the same nouns" as Romans 13:1, "apparently" on P61, "the church read it as his from the second century" supported by Eusebius. (4) Several quotes of other books' Plain do not match. (5) The 2:13 deep dive misdescribes the Plain's own comma and word order. No fabricated sources found. Verse-by-verse drift from NIV/ESV is mild (one place worth redoing).

## Findings

### Major

**1. major. variants.json 2:13 ("Jesus Christ or Christ Jesus?"), chapters.json ch2 traditions card (Jehovah's Witnesses), notes.json #54: the NWT is described three different ways, two of them wrong for the edition cited.**
- The variant entry's `why` says "The New World Translation follows the other order ('Christ Jesus')". The card says the verse speaks of "our Savior, Christ Jesus" and gives sources `watchtower-1981-titus` and `nwt-2013`. Note #54 quotes the NWT as "the great God and of our Savior, Jesus Christ."
- Checked: jw.org's NWT (2013 revision) Titus 2:13 reads "...glorious manifestation of the great God and of our Savior, Jesus Christ". The Watchtower Online Library page for *The Watchtower*, April 1, 1981, p. 31 quotes the older NWT wording as "...of [the] Savior of us, Christ Jesus." So "Christ Jesus" belongs to the 1981-era wording, not to `nwt-2013`. The variant entry is therefore false for the cited edition, and the card mixes editions inside one quotation.
- Fix: delete the NWT sentence from the variant `why` (it is not needed), or say which edition has which order. In the card quote the 2013 wording, or quote the 1981 article's own wording and name that edition. Keep note #54 as it is.

**2. major. chapters.json ch2, `traditions` card on 2:4-5 (top `headship`): two voices only, and the Catholic voice is one-sided.**
- The card has one Catholic entry (John Paul II, *Mulieris Dignitatem* 24, "mutual subjection") and one Baptist entry (BFM 2000 XVIII). Both quotations are accurate. But this sets "mutual" against "servant leadership" and leaves out the churches that differ on exactly this verse: Orthodox, Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, Pentecostal, and the egalitarian Baptist and evangelical bodies. The Baptist entry is Southern Baptist only, yet the label is "Baptist".
- The Catholic entry omits that the same document and the wider Catholic teaching also keep the husband's headship language; as written, the reader sees Catholics as the egalitarian side. That is the one-sided handling the brief warned about for the women's passages.
- Fix: add at least Orthodox, Lutheran, Reformed and Pentecostal (each from a document opened this session), an egalitarian evangelical or Anglican statement, and a second Catholic line so MD 24 is not the whole of Catholic teaching. If sources cannot be found, say plainly in the card that other churches are not yet shown rather than presenting two as the field.

**3. major. chapters.json ch2: no card for 2:9-10 (slavery) and none for 2:3 (older women as teachers, `women-in-ministry`).**
- brief-tit.md plans 4 to 6 cards and names both. The book has four. Note #46 says "Later readers used passages like this one to defend slavery, notably in the American South" and "Today the churches agree that slavery is wrong", the `slavery` topic repeats it, and the chapter's T lens says the passage "was used both to defend slavery and ... to undermine it". These are claims about church positions with no church document behind them.
- Fix: add a `slavery` card from churches' own statements (for example a papal bull or catechism line, the 1995 Southern Baptist resolution, an Orthodox or Methodist statement), each opened this session, or soften the claims to what the text and a named source say. Add a short `women-in-ministry` card (or fold 2:3-4 into a fuller headship card) with the teaching-limits views: complementarian, egalitarian, Catholic/Orthodox ordination.

**4. major. notes.json #28 (1:12, "The Cretan prophet: Epimenides?") and chapters.json ch1 deep dive "The Cretan liar": contradictory attribution history, with an unsourced "first" claim.**
- The note says Clement of Alexandria (*Stromata* 1.14) names Epimenides "about AD 200" and then says "Jerome is the first who certainly ascribes the work to him." Clement is earlier, and I opened *Stromata* 1.14 on New Advent: Clement writes of "Epimenides the Cretan, whom Paul knew as a Greek prophet" and quotes Titus 1:12. Whatever "the work" means, the sentence contradicts the one before it, and nothing I opened supports the Jerome claim. Koskenniemi's article is cited for the thesis (proverb, then paradox, then Epimenides); I could not confirm any Jerome statement in it.
- Also: Calvin (opened on CCEL) says he has "no doubt" the speaker is Epimenides; the note leaves Calvin out of the history. Chrysostom's attribution in Hom. 3 is correct as quoted, but the footnote there matters: some editions name Callimachus. The note gives only one side of that.
- Fix: delete the Jerome sentence or replace it with what Koskenniemi actually says (open the article, pp. 59-69); state in one line who attributes (Clement, Chrysostom, Calvin) and who doubts.

**5. major. notes.json #29 (1:12, "A proverb, an insult, and a puzzle") and book.json `audience.why`: a contested reading is stated as settled.**
- The note says Paul "uses it to describe the false teachers, not every Cretan", and that the line is "a rebuke inside a family, not a verdict on a people". The Greek of 1:12 has "one of them... their own prophet" (`ἐξ αὐτῶν... αὐτῶν`), and 1:13 says "this testimony is true". Whether "them" is the opponents of 1:10-11 or the Cretans at large is debated, and the Plain itself (rightly) leaves it open. The harsh reading (the writer endorses the stereotype and tells Titus to "correct them sharply") is a real view and is not given.
- Fix: present both: (a) aimed at the teachers, (b) endorsement of the Cretan reputation, with the second given its strongest form, as the brief's fairness rule requires. Mark the "not a verdict on a people" sentence as the Plain's reading of the letter's aim.

**6. major. book.json `reading[0]`: "the oldest complete Christian exposition of the letter" (Chrysostom).**
- A search this session found Ambrosiaster's commentary on the thirteen Pauline letters dated to the pontificate of Damasus (366-384) and Jerome's commentary on Titus dated c. 387 (fourthcentury.com, citing Kelly). I found no date for Chrysostom's Titus homilies beyond that they were preached at Antioch. So the "oldest complete" claim is unsupported and probably false.
- Fix: say "a full early exposition" or "late fourth century" only if a source gives the date; drop "oldest complete".

**7. major. notes.json #59 (3:1) and topics.json `church-and-state`: "the same nouns (archai, exousiai)" as Romans 13:1.**
- `texts/orig/Rom.json` 13:1 reads `ἐξουσίαις ὑπερεχούσαις` ("authorities"); `ἄρχοντες` ("rulers") is in 13:3. `ἀρχαῖς` is not in Romans 13. Titus 3:1 has `ἀρχαῖς ἐξουσίαις`. The Plain's quote of Rom 13:1 ("Every person must submit to the authorities above them") is exact, but the claim that the nouns are the same, and the topic's "same words for 'rulers' and 'authorities'", are wrong.
- Fix: "the same verb and the word 'authorities'; Titus adds 'rulers'".

**8. major. notes.json #77 and variants.json 3:15: "apparently" on P61, and unverified manuscript statements about the subscription.**
- Note #77: "(apparently in P61, about 700, and in Sinaiticus and Ephraemi); Alexandrinus adds that it was written from Nicopolis". Variant 3:15: "P61 (about AD 700, apparently)". STYLE rule 2 forbids hedges in content. I tried the LaParola site and a search this session and could not open the apparatus entries for the subscription, so I could not verify the manuscript-by-manuscript claims; the Byzantine form I did find confirmed ("Nicopolis of Macedonia") and a source (Heidegger, via a search) confirms the Greek subscription names Nicopolis of Macedonia.
- Fix: remove "apparently"; either open an apparatus (NA28 or the Tyndale House edition) and cite it, or say only that later copies add subscriptions and give the Byzantine wording. The same applies to the other first-hand and corrector claims in the 1:4, 1:5, 2:5, 2:7 and 2:11 variants: the writer's witness lists came from LaParola and I could not re-open them. Spot-check the corrector sigla (for example "a corrector of Sinaiticus" at 2:11) against a printed apparatus.

**9. major. notes.json #0 (1:1): Eusebius cited for "the church read it as his from the second century on".**
- The note's parenthesis is Eusebius, *HE* 3.4.6, which (verified: New Advent) says Titus received the episcopate of the churches in Crete. It says nothing about who wrote the letter, and Eusebius is fourth-century. The claim of second-century reading is in the shared core `pastoral-letters` with Irenaeus and Polycarp, but here it is tied to the wrong source. The book.json `authorship` entry "The traditional view" has the same problem. Neither mentions that Marcion's collection left the Pastorals out (the core covers this with Tertullian).
- Fix: cite Irenaeus or point to `pastoral-letters` for the second-century claim; keep Eusebius only for Titus on Crete.

**10. major. chapters.json ch2 deep dive on 2:13, last paragraph.**
- It says: "The Plain keeps the Greek word order, 'our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ', which most naturally reads as one person, while the comma before 'Jesus Christ' allows the reader to see the other reading." Both parts are wrong. The Greek order is "the great God and Savior of us, Jesus Christ"; the Plain moves "our" to the front, which is not the Greek order. And a comma before "Jesus Christ" marks apposition, which favors one person; it does not "allow" two. As the Plain stands, the translation has chosen the one-person reading (a defensible choice, and the brief's), but the book should say so openly instead of describing the choice as neutral.
- Also in the same section: stray ",." after "(1 Timothy 6:14; 2 Timothy 4:1, 8)"; "Most modern translations and commentators take this reading" and "most scholars and modern Bibles agree" (note #54) are unsourced; the two-person side is named only through the KJV, the NWT and "others", with Porter given as holding that Sharp's rule shows "only some unity". I could confirm Porter's review exists (JETS 53 (2010): 828-32, and the 2013 exchange in JETS 56/1) but could not open its text, so I cannot confirm this characterization, and the search summary says only that Porter argued Wallace misread the rule. Porter is not shown arguing for two persons.
- Fix: rewrite the closing paragraph to say the Plain adopts the one-person reading; name one or more scholars who read two persons (opened this session) or drop the unsupported characterization of Porter; remove the stray punctuation; give a source or soften "most".
